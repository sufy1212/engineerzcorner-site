Circuit Breaker Types Explained: MCB to OCB

Every circuit breaker does the same basic job — make and break a circuit, automatically, under fault current — but how it extinguishes the arc changes completely with voltage class, and that's what actually separates the types.

What a circuit breaker actually does

A circuit breaker is an electromechanical device inserted into a circuit to carry normal load current continuously and automatically interrupt that current when a fault — overload, short circuit, or earth fault — is detected. The hard engineering problem isn't opening the contacts; it's what happens in the instant they separate. Current doesn't stop flowing just because a gap opens — it keeps arcing across that gap until the arc is stretched, cooled, and starved of ionized gas enough to extinguish. Every breaker type is really just a different answer to that one problem: how to quench the arc, safely and repeatably, at a given voltage and fault level.

Types of circuit breakers

Different interrupting media trade off voltage class, breaking capacity, maintenance burden, and cost — no single type covers the full range from a distribution board to a grid substation.

MCB
Miniature circuit breaker
Fixed, non-adjustable thermal-magnetic trip up to roughly 100 A. The default breaker for final distribution circuits — lighting, sockets, small appliance feeders — where the trip setting never needs to change.
MCCB
Moulded case circuit breaker
Up to a few thousand amps, with an adjustable thermal-magnetic or electronic trip unit inside a moulded insulating case. Sits between the MCB and the ACB — sub-main distribution, motor and feeder protection.
ACB
Air circuit breaker
Draw-out construction rated up to several thousand amps, arc quenched in open air using arc chutes and splitter plates. The standard incomer and bus-coupler breaker on LV switchboards feeding whole buildings or plants.
VCB
Vacuum circuit breaker
Contacts separate inside a sealed vacuum interrupter, where the absence of gas makes the arc self-extinguish almost immediately. The dominant medium-voltage breaker today — compact, low maintenance, no toxic byproducts.
OCB
Oil circuit breaker
Contacts open inside mineral oil, which decomposes into a hydrogen-rich gas bubble that helps cool and quench the arc. Largely legacy equipment now — fire and maintenance concerns pushed most new installs to vacuum or SF6.
SF6 Breaker
Sulfur hexafluoride gas
Uses SF6 gas, an excellent dielectric and arc-quenching medium, sealed under pressure around the contacts. Covers medium- to extra-high-voltage duty in a far smaller footprint than an equivalent air-blast breaker.
Air-Blast Breaker
High-pressure air stream
A blast of compressed air, released the instant contacts part, sweeps the arc out and cools it. Handled very high voltages and fault levels well, at the cost of noise and a bulky compressed-air system — now mostly superseded by SF6.
GIS Breaker
Gas-insulated switchgear
An SF6 breaker built into a compact, fully enclosed gas-insulated switchgear bay along with the busbars, disconnectors, and CTs. Chosen where space is limited or the environment is too harsh for open-air (AIS) switchgear.

Main parts of a circuit breaker

  1. 1
    Frame / enclosureInsulating or metal housing that supports and protects all internal components.
  2. 2
    Fixed & moving contactsCarry load current when closed; separating them is what initiates current interruption.
  3. 3
    Arc-quenching mediumAir, oil, vacuum, or SF6 gas — whatever cools and de-ionizes the arc so it doesn't restrike.
  4. 4
    Arc chute / splitter platesOn air and oil breakers, stretches and splits the arc into shorter segments to help extinguish it faster.
  5. 5
    Operating mechanismSpring, solenoid, or pneumatic mechanism that physically drives the contacts open and closed, manually or on a trip signal.
  6. 6
    Trip unitThermal-magnetic or electronic release that senses overload and short-circuit current and commands the mechanism to open.
  7. 7
    TerminalsLine and load connection points, sized to the breaker's continuous current rating.
  8. 8
    Auxiliary & alarm contactsSmall extra contacts that mirror breaker status to indication lamps, PLCs, or a SCADA system.

Trip mechanisms

1Thermal-magnetic — a bimetal strip trips on sustained overload; a magnetic element trips instantaneously on short-circuit current.
2Electronic / microprocessor trip — measures current digitally and applies adjustable long-time, short-time, and instantaneous protection curves.
3Shunt trip — a coil energized by an external protection relay or emergency-stop circuit forces the breaker open remotely.
4Undervoltage release — trips the breaker automatically if supply voltage drops or is lost, preventing unexpected re-energization.
5Manual / independent manual operation — the handle both charges the closing spring and operates the mechanism, independent of operator speed.

Selection checklist

  • 1System voltage and the breaker's rated voltage class
  • 2Continuous current rating against the actual load, with margin
  • 3Required breaking (interrupting) capacity at the installation point
  • 4Trip unit type and settings — fixed thermal-magnetic vs adjustable electronic
  • 5Selectivity / discrimination with upstream and downstream breakers
  • 6Fixed vs draw-out construction, based on maintenance and uptime needs
  • 7Environment — ambient temperature, altitude, and enclosure ingress protection

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓Contact erosion and pitting from repeated fault interruption, reducing current-carrying capacity
  • ✓Trip unit drift or failure — a breaker that won't trip on a real fault is far more dangerous than one that trips nuisance-fast
  • ✓Loose or overheated terminal connections, often the actual root cause behind a breaker that "trips for no reason"
  • ✓SF6 gas pressure loss on gas breakers, monitored continuously since low pressure compromises both insulation and interruption
  • ✓Mechanism spring fatigue or lubrication issues causing sluggish closing or opening times
  • ✓Poor selectivity coordination, where an upstream breaker trips before the downstream one clears a local fault
  • ✓Insulation degradation in oil breakers, tracked through routine dielectric testing where they're still in service
Most breaker failures trace back to the trip unit or the terminal connections, not the contacts themselves — a breaker that looks fine on the outside can still fail to clear a fault if its protection settings have drifted or its terminals have loosened over time.

Common applications

Final distribution circuits LV switchboard incomers & bus couplers Motor and feeder protection Medium-voltage ring main units Substation transformer feeders Generator and utility interconnection Grid transmission switching Compact gas-insulated substations

Key takeaways

  • ✓Every breaker carries load and interrupts faults — the type is really a choice of how the arc gets quenched at that voltage class.
  • ✓MCB, MCCB, and ACB cover LV duty at increasing current and breaking capacity; vacuum and SF6 dominate MV and above.
  • ✓Oil and air-blast breakers are largely legacy technology, phased out for maintenance, fire risk, and footprint reasons.
  • ✓The trip unit and terminal connections fail more often than the contacts — check those first, not just contact wear.
