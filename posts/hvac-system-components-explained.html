HVAC System Components Explained

An HVAC system isn't one machine — it's a refrigerant-side loop moving heat, handed off to an air-side loop that delivers the result to the room. Here's every major component, in the order the heat actually travels.

02
The compressor doesn't create cold — it pressurizes refrigerant so heat can be rejected outside and absorbed inside at the right temperatures.

Two loops, not one machine

It's easy to look at an outdoor condensing unit and an indoor air handler and think of them as separate systems, but they're really one loop split into two halves. The refrigerant-side loop — compressor, condenser coil, expansion device, evaporator coil — does the actual work of moving heat from inside the building to outside. The air-side loop — the blower, ductwork, diffusers, and returns — is just the delivery system that gets the conditioned result to the people in the room.

The evaporator coil is the hinge between the two. Refrigerant absorbs heat from the coil's surface; air blown across that same surface loses the heat. Everything before the evaporator, in the refrigerant loop, exists to make that coil cold enough to work. Everything after it, in the air loop, exists to distribute the result.

1Compressor

  • Pulls in low-pressure refrigerant vapor from the evaporator and compresses it, raising both its pressure and temperature
  • That temperature rise is what makes the refrigerant hotter than outdoor air, so heat can flow out of it at the condenser
  • Effectively the pump that keeps refrigerant circulating around the whole loop — the system's main energy input

2Condenser Coil

  • Hot, high-pressure refrigerant vapor from the compressor flows through this coil and rejects heat to outdoor air (or condenser water)
  • Losing that heat is what condenses the refrigerant from a vapor back into a high-pressure liquid
  • Located outdoors on split systems, or in a rooftop/packaged unit's outdoor section, where heat has somewhere to go

3Expansion Device

  • Forces high-pressure liquid refrigerant through a small orifice, dropping its pressure sharply before the evaporator
  • That pressure drop is what makes the refrigerant cold enough to absorb heat from indoor air — this is the component that actually creates the cooling effect
  • A thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) meters flow to match the evaporator's load; fixed orifices do the same job more simply on smaller systems

4Evaporator Coil

  • Cold, low-pressure refrigerant flows through this coil while the blower pushes indoor air across its surface
  • Refrigerant absorbs heat from that air and boils back into a vapor — this is the actual "cooling" moment in the system
  • Moisture in the air also condenses on the cold coil surface here, which is why cooling systems dehumidify as a byproduct

5Air Handling Unit / Blower

  • Fan that pulls return air from the space, pushes it across the evaporator coil, and drives it back out through supply ductwork
  • Airflow rate here has to be matched to the coil — too little airflow risks the coil icing over; too much hurts dehumidification
  • On many systems also houses the filter bank and, on heating systems, the furnace burner or electric heat strips

6Ductwork & Diffusers

  • Supply ducts carry conditioned air from the blower to each zone; return ducts bring room air back to start the cycle again
  • Diffusers and grilles at the outlets control throw and spread so air actually mixes with the room instead of dumping in one spot
  • Undersized, leaky, or poorly balanced ductwork is one of the most common reasons a correctly sized system still under-performs

7Thermostat & Controls

  • Reads space temperature (and often humidity) against setpoint and calls for cooling, heating, or fan operation
  • On multi-zone systems, dampers and zone controllers route conditioned air only where it's currently being called for
  • Staging, deadbands, and lockouts here are what stop the equipment from short-cycling on and off too rapidly

How the cycle actually runs

1The compressor pulls in low-pressure refrigerant vapor and compresses it into a hot, high-pressure vapor.
2That hot vapor flows through the condenser coil, rejects its heat to outdoor air, and condenses into a high-pressure liquid.
3The expansion device drops the liquid's pressure sharply, cooling it just before it enters the evaporator coil.
4The blower pushes indoor air across the cold evaporator coil; refrigerant absorbs the heat and boils back into a vapor.
5That cooled, dehumidified air travels through supply ductwork and diffusers into the occupied space, while the refrigerant vapor heads back to the compressor to repeat the cycle.

Why the air side matters just as much

A correctly sized refrigerant loop can still deliver a poor result if the air side is neglected. Leaky ductwork loses conditioned air before it ever reaches a diffuser. A dirty filter or a blower running at the wrong speed starves the evaporator of airflow, which can ice the coil and cut capacity. Undersized returns starve the whole system of air to condition in the first place. In practice, most comfort complaints trace back to the air side, not the refrigerant side.

Common terms, decoded

TermMeaning
RefrigerantWorking fluid that absorbs and rejects heat as it changes state between liquid and vapor
SuperheatHow many degrees above its boiling point the refrigerant vapor is leaving the evaporator — used to check charge and airflow
SubcoolingHow many degrees below its condensing point the liquid refrigerant is leaving the condenser

Why this architecture is common

Efficiency
Serviceability
Zone Control

Splitting the job into a compact refrigerant loop and a separately routable air loop is what lets one piece of outdoor equipment condition an entire building's worth of rooms, each with its own setpoint. It also isolates failure modes for troubleshooting — a coil or compressor issue behaves very differently from a duct or damper issue — which matters when narrowing down why a specific zone is uncomfortable.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Every HVAC system is two loops in series: a refrigerant loop that moves heat, and an air loop that delivers it.
  • ✓The expansion device, not the compressor, is what makes the evaporator cold enough to absorb heat from the room.
  • ✓The evaporator coil is the hinge point where the two loops meet and heat actually transfers to the airstream.
  • ✓Airflow across the evaporator has to be right — too little risks icing, too much hurts dehumidification.
  • ✓Most comfort complaints trace back to the air side — ductwork, filters, and controls — more often than the refrigerant side.
