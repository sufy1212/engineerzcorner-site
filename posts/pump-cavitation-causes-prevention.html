Cavitation in Pumps: Causes, Warning Signs, and Prevention

A pump that sounds like it's pumping gravel usually isn't broken — it's cavitating. Here's what's actually happening inside the casing, why it eats impellers alive, and how to stop it before it does.

What's actually happening

Every pump needs the fluid arriving at its impeller eye to stay above its vapour pressure. If the local pressure there drops too low, the liquid flashes into tiny vapour bubbles — not because it's boiling from heat, but because pressure has fallen far enough for it to boil at the running temperature. Those bubbles are swept along with the flow, and the instant they reach a higher-pressure region further into the impeller, they collapse. That collapse isn't gentle: each bubble implosion releases a tiny, intensely localized shockwave against the nearest metal surface.

One bubble collapsing does nothing. Millions of them collapsing continuously, thousands of times a second, is what turns cavitation into a mechanical problem — it slowly erodes and pits impeller vanes and volute walls, the same way persistent hammering dents sheet metal.

Why it comes down to one comparison

NPSH available vs. NPSH required

Every pump has an NPSHrequired curve from the manufacturer — the minimum suction-side energy margin, above vapour pressure, that the pump needs at a given flow rate to avoid cavitating. Your piping and installation determine NPSHavailable — how much margin the actual system is providing at the pump's suction flange. Cavitation-free operation is simply keeping available above required, with a safety margin.

NPSHa itself is driven by atmospheric/tank pressure, static suction lift or head, vapour pressure at the operating temperature, and friction losses in the suction line — every one of them is something the installation controls, not the pump.

How it shows up in the field

SoundA rattling, gravel-like noise from the pump casing — often the first and most obvious tell.
VibrationErratic vibration that doesn't match normal running signatures, sometimes strong enough to feel through the baseplate.
Flow & pressureUnstable discharge pressure and a flow rate that fluctuates even with steady valve settings.
Long-term wearPitted, sponge-like erosion on impeller vanes and volute walls found at teardown.

Prevention checklist

  • 1Keep the suction line short, generously sized, and with the fewest fittings practical — friction loss is the easiest lever to pull.
  • 2Position the pump as close to the liquid source as the layout allows, minimizing static suction lift.
  • 3Check and clean suction strainers on a schedule, not just when something goes wrong.
  • 4Never throttle a control valve on the suction side — throttle discharge instead.
  • 5Confirm NPSHa against the manufacturer's NPSHr curve at the actual duty point, not just at best-efficiency point.
  • 6For hot or volatile fluids, re-check vapour pressure at true operating temperature, not room temperature.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cavitation is a pressure problem at the impeller eye, not a pump defect by default.
  • ✓The fix almost always lives on the suction side of the installation, not in swapping the pump.
  • ✓NPSHa must clear NPSHr with margin, at the real duty point and real fluid temperature.
  • ✓Noise and vibration are early warnings — pitting and erosion are what happens if they're ignored.
