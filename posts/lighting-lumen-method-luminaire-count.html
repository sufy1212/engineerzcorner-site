Lighting Design by the Lumen Method: Why Luminaire Count Isn't Just Lumens ÷ Lux

Two rooms with identical area and target lux level can need very different luminaire counts, because the room itself changes how much of the light actually lands where you need it.

The lumen method spreads the total lumens needed across a room's area, then derates by two factors before arriving at a luminaire count.
02
Utilization factor accounts for how much of a luminaire's output actually reaches the working plane, given room geometry and surface reflectances.

Maintenance factor accounts for lumen depreciation over time from dirt accumulation and lamp aging — without it, a design meets spec on day one and fails it a year later.

This is a quick layout check, not a substitute for a point-by-point photometric design where uniformity and glare matter.

The lumen method

N  =  (E × A)  /  (F × UF × MF)
N = number of luminaires · E = target illuminance (lux) · A = room area (m²) · F = lumens per luminaire · UF = utilization factor · MF = maintenance factor

E × A gives the total lumens theoretically required to hit the target average illuminance across the whole floor area. Dividing straight by a luminaire's rated lumen output (F) would tell you how many fixtures deliver that many lumens — if every lumen leaving the fixture landed usefully on the working plane. It doesn't, which is what UF and MF correct for.

Utilization factor: not all light reaches the desk

Some light from a luminaire is absorbed by walls, ceiling and furniture, or falls outside the working plane entirely, before it ever contributes to the measured illuminance. Utilization factor captures that loss, and it depends on room geometry (via the room index — a ratio of floor area to mounting height and room dimensions) and the reflectance of ceiling, walls and floor. A small, low-reflectance room wastes more light than a large, bright one — so the same target lux level and luminaire type can need meaningfully different fixture counts between two rooms of identical floor area.

Maintenance factor: designing for year five, not day one

Lamp output falls over its service life, and dirt accumulates on lenses and room surfaces between cleanings. A maintenance factor (commonly around 0.8 for typical indoor environments, lower for dirtier industrial spaces) derates the design lumen output so the installation still meets its target illuminance well into its maintenance cycle — not just when the lamps are brand new. Skipping this factor produces a design that measures correctly at commissioning and falls below spec within a year or two.

What this method doesn't check

The lumen method gives an average illuminance across the room, using an average-case luminaire count. It doesn't verify uniformity (whether that light is spread evenly or concentrated near fixtures), glare, or how illuminance falls off near walls and corners. For a space where those matter — open-plan offices, task areas, anywhere visual comfort is a design requirement — a point-by-point photometric calculation using the manufacturer's actual polar curve data is the next step after this quick layout check.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Total lumens needed comes from target lux × area, then two derating factors bring it down to a real luminaire count.
  • ✓Utilization factor depends on room geometry and surface reflectances — it isn't a fixed constant across different rooms.
  • ✓Maintenance factor designs for the lumen output partway through the maintenance cycle, not day-one brand-new output.
  • ✓This gives an average illuminance figure only — uniformity and glare need a point-by-point photometric study for spaces where they matter.
