Elapsed Time Calculations

14:40 minus 9:15 isn't 5.25 — it's 5 hours 25 minutes. Subtract clock times like ordinary decimals and the answer comes out wrong every time the minutes don't line up.

Why clock arithmetic isn't decimal arithmetic

A clock reading of 2:45 isn't the number 2.45 — it's 2 hours and 45 out of the next 60 minutes. Treating it like an ordinary decimal is the single most common source of time-math errors: subtracting 9:15 from 14:40 by ordinary decimal subtraction would give "5.25," which looks like it should mean 5 hours 25 minutes, but 0.25 of an hour is 15 minutes, not 25. The correct answer is 5 hours 25 minutes purely by coincidence of these particular numbers — change either time slightly and decimal subtraction stops working, because minutes carry in blocks of 60, not 10.

Subtracting times: when you need to borrow

Subtracting two clock times works one column at a time, hours and minutes separately — until the minutes being subtracted are larger than the minutes being subtracted from. At that point, just like borrowing 10 from the tens column in decimal subtraction, you borrow 60 minutes from the hours column.

14:20 − 9:45  →  borrow: 13:80 − 9:45  =  4:35
14:20 became 13 hours plus 80 minutes (14×60+20 = 860 minutes, same total) so that 80 − 45 doesn't go negative. The hours then subtract normally: 13 − 9 = 4.

Spans that cross midnight

A shift running from 22:00 to 06:00 can't be subtracted directly — 06:00 minus 22:00 gives a negative number, because the subtraction doesn't know the clock rolled over to a new day. The fix is to add 24 hours to the end time before subtracting, which accounts for the day boundary being crossed:

06:00 + 24:00 − 22:00  =  30:00 − 22:00  =  8:00
An 8-hour shift, correctly, once the day rollover is accounted for — without the +24:00 adjustment the raw subtraction would wrongly suggest a negative duration.

Converting to decimal hours

Timesheets and billing often need a duration expressed as decimal hours rather than hours-and-minutes — and this conversion divides by 60, it doesn't just move a decimal point. 45 minutes is 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75 hours, not 0.45. A duration of 5 hours 25 minutes becomes 5 + (25÷60) = 5.417 hours. Skipping the division and writing "5.25" for "5 hours 25 minutes" is a subtle-looking error that compounds badly across a full timesheet or invoice.

Common terms, decoded

TermMeaning
SexagesimalA base-60 counting system — the reason minutes and seconds each run 0–59 before carrying, inherited from ancient Babylonian mathematics
BorrowingTaking a unit from the next larger place value to allow a subtraction to complete without going negative — 60 minutes from an hour, just as 10 units are borrowed from a tens column in decimal
Decimal hoursA duration expressed as a single number of hours with a fractional part, found by dividing the minutes portion by 60
24-hour rolloverThe adjustment needed when a time span crosses midnight, adding 24 hours to the later clock reading before subtracting

Key takeaways

  • ✓Minutes and seconds run on base 60, not base 10 — subtracting clock times as if they were ordinary decimals gives the wrong answer whenever borrowing is needed.
  • ✓Borrowing in time subtraction moves 60 minutes into the minutes column, the same logic as borrowing 10 in decimal subtraction — just a different base.
  • ✓A span crossing midnight needs 24 hours added to the end time before subtracting, or the result comes out negative.
  • ✓Converting minutes to decimal hours means dividing by 60, not moving a decimal point — 45 minutes is 0.75 hours, not 0.45.
  • ✓Whenever a time calculation is done by hand, checking it against a rough estimate first catches most of these base-60 slip-ups before they reach a timesheet.
