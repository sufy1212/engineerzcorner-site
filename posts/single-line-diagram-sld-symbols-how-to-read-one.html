Single Line Diagram (SLD) Symbols & How to Read One

A single line diagram compresses a three-phase power system down to one line per connection — every generator, transformer, breaker and bus drawn once, as a symbol, instead of three times as actual wires.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical Drawings
01
"Single line" means exactly that — a balanced three-phase circuit is drawn as one line instead of three, with the symbol at each point telling you what equipment sits there.
02
North America generally follows IEEE Std 315 / ANSI Y32.2 symbols; most of the rest of the world follows IEC 60617 — the same equipment can look different depending on which standard a drawing was made to.
03
A number next to a breaker or relay symbol (52, 51, 87, 27...) isn't arbitrary — it's an ANSI/IEEE C37.2 device function number, and it tells you what that device actually does before you even look at the legend.

Read top to bottom, source to load: incoming supply or generation at the top, through transformation and protection, down to the bus, and out through feeder breakers to individual loads.

What a single line diagram is for

A single line diagram (SLD), also called a one-line diagram, is the standard way to represent a power system's electrical connections without drawing every phase conductor separately. Since most power equipment is symmetrical across the three phases, one line stands in for all three, and standardized symbols along that line show exactly what's connected — a generator, a transformer, a breaker, a bus, a load. It's the electrical equivalent of a process P&ID: not to scale, not showing physical layout, but showing exactly how the system is electrically connected and protected.

Core symbols

The handful of symbols that appear on almost every power SLD.

Generator / Source
A circle with "G", or an incoming arrow for utility supply
Marks where power enters the system — an on-site generator (circle with "G") or an incoming utility feed, usually shown at the top of the diagram with its voltage labeled.
Power Transformer
Two overlapping circles
Two overlapping circles represent the primary and secondary windings; small triangle or zigzag marks near the circles indicate delta or wye (star) winding connections and whether the neutral is grounded.
Circuit Breaker
ANSI device 52
A box on the line in the common ANSI convention (IEC drawings use a switch-contact symbol with a breaker qualifier instead); device number 52 next to it confirms it's an AC power circuit breaker rather than a disconnect.
Disconnect Switch / Isolator
Open blade, no interrupting rating
Drawn as an open switch blade — it isolates a section of circuit for safety or maintenance, but unlike a breaker it isn't rated to interrupt load or fault current and is normally only operated with the circuit already de-energized.
Fuse
Zigzag or rectangle on the line
A one-time overcurrent protection device shown as a zigzag line or a rectangle across the conductor — once it operates, it has to be physically replaced rather than reset like a breaker.
CT / VT (PT)
Instrument transformers
A current transformer (CT) is shown as a circle around the conductor with two leads out to metering or relays; a voltage transformer (VT/PT) looks similar but connects across the line rather than in series — both scale a high current or voltage down to a safe metering/protection level.
Busbar
Heavy horizontal or vertical line
Drawn noticeably thicker than the connecting lines — every device tapped off a bus shares that common connection point, which is why bus arrangement (single bus, main-tie-main, ring) matters so much for reliability.
Protective Relay
Circle or box with an ANSI device number
Shown as a small circle or box next to the breaker it operates, labeled with its ANSI/IEEE C37.2 device number — 51 for time overcurrent, 87 for differential, 27 for undervoltage, 59 for overvoltage, and so on.
Ground / Earth
Descending horizontal lines
A set of horizontal lines shrinking toward a point marks an intentional connection to earth — on a neutral point, a surge arrester, or an equipment frame — distinct from a floating or ungrounded point on the same diagram.

Line conventions

Line styleMeaning
Solid, heavyMain power path — busbars and primary current-carrying conductors
Solid, thinSecondary connections — metering, CT/VT leads, instrument wiring
DashedControl, interlock, or future/spare — not a live power path today
Two circles overlappingA transformer, not two separate devices

How to actually read one

1Find the source at the top — utility incoming feed or on-site generation — and note its voltage level.
2Follow it through the first protection device and transformer, checking the winding symbols for the delta/wye connection and grounding.
3Identify the bus arrangement the transformer feeds into — a single bus is simplest, but main-tie-main or ring buses change how a fault or outage propagates.
4Read each feeder breaker's device number to know what protection function is watching it, then follow it down to its load, motor, or capacitor bank.
The same physical switchgear can be drawn to either IEEE 315/ANSI Y32.2 or IEC 60617 conventions — before assuming a symbol means what it usually means, check which standard the drawing's title block or legend calls out.

Common applications

Electrical design drawings Protection coordination studies Arc flash studies Switchgear specification Commissioning & as-built documentation Operations & troubleshooting reference

Key takeaways

  • ✓A single line diagram trades physical realism for electrical clarity — one line, standard symbols, and it tells you the whole system's connectivity at a glance.
  • ✓Line weight and style carry meaning — heavy solid for main power, thin for instrumentation, dashed for control or spare — don't skim past them.
  • ✓ANSI device numbers on breakers and relays are a shorthand worth memorizing — they tell you the protection function before you find the legend.
  • ✓Read source-to-load, top-to-bottom — the diagram's layout usually already follows the direction power actually flows.
