UPS Battery Sizing: Why the Nameplate Ah Never Matches What You Actually Get

A battery's rated Ah is a lab number measured under conditions your installation will never actually see.

02
Depth of discharge (DOD) limits how much of the rated capacity you can actually use without shortening battery life or risking damage.
03
An aging factor accounts for capacity loss over the battery's service life — sizing for day-one capacity means it's undersized well before end of life.

Temperature matters more than most people expect: battery capacity drops meaningfully below the 20–25°C the nameplate rating was tested at.

The base calculation

Ah  =  (Pload × tbackup)  /  (Vbatt × η)
Pload = load power · tbackup = required backup time (hours) · Vbatt = battery string nominal voltage · η = inverter efficiency

This gives the raw Ah a battery string would need to deliver, at 100% of its rated capacity, for the whole backup window. Nobody actually sizes to this number directly — it's the figure the derating factors below get applied to.

Why the real figure is bigger

Depth of discharge (DOD)Discharging a battery fully, repeatedly, shortens its cycle life dramatically. Sizing to a limited DOD (commonly 80% for VRLA, lower for some chemistries) means dividing the required Ah by that fraction — the battery needs more nameplate capacity than the raw load calculation suggests.
Aging factorA lead-acid battery typically loses capacity as it ages, even with correct maintenance. Sizing for nameplate day-one capacity means the string is already undersized partway through its rated service life — an aging factor (often 1.25) inflates the sizing to compensate up front.

Temperature adds a third factor: capacity ratings are measured at 20–25°C, and capacity falls measurably at lower ambient temperatures — sometimes significantly so in an unconditioned plant room. A temperature correction factor accounts for the actual ambient the battery room will run at, hot or cold. Multiply the base Ah by DOD, aging and temperature factors (and divide by efficiency) and the resulting nameplate Ah can end up 40–60% larger than the raw load-and-time figure alone would suggest.

Why this is a simplified method

This constant-power method is a reasonable field/budgetary estimate, but a full battery sizing exercise (per IEEE 485) uses the manufacturer's actual discharge-rate curves — capacity isn't linear with discharge time, and a battery discharged fast for a short backup delivers proportionally less Ah than one discharged slowly over hours. For a critical UPS installation, the final string selection should always be checked against the battery vendor's rate tables, not just this simplified formula.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The raw Ah figure from load and backup time is only a starting point — real sizing needs DOD, aging and temperature factors layered on top.
  • ✓Limiting depth of discharge protects battery cycle life but means more nameplate Ah is needed for the same usable backup time.
  • ✓Sizing for day-one nameplate capacity leaves no margin for the capacity loss that happens naturally over the battery's service life.
  • ✓This constant-power method is a good field estimate — final selection should be checked against the battery manufacturer's discharge-rate tables.
