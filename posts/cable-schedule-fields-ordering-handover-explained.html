Cable Schedules Explained: What Goes in One and How It Drives Ordering and Handover

A cable schedule lists every cable with its tag, route, size, type and length. Here is what each field is for, how the totals become an order, and the checks a schedule cannot do for you.

A cable schedule is the list of every cable on a project: where it starts, where it ends, what it is and how long it is. It looks like paperwork, but the same list is used to order cable, to pull it, to test it and to hand the installation over. Getting the fields and the habits right early saves a great deal of confusion later.

What goes in each row

FieldWhat it recordsWhy it matters
Cable tagA unique referenceLinks the schedule to drawings, labels and test records
From / ToOrigin and destination equipmentTells the installer where it runs and the tester what to isolate
Size / coresConductor area and number of coresDrives ordering and termination hardware
TypeInsulation, armour and sheath, or special dutySets temperature rating, mechanical protection and fire behaviour
LengthRoute length in metresSets the order quantity and voltage drop
Notes / routeTray, duct or special routingAvoids clashes and mixing of power and instrument cables

Choosing the type column

  • XLPE or PVC insulation. XLPE is rated for a higher conductor temperature than PVC, commonly 90 °C against 70 °C, which usually gives it a higher current rating for the same size. See cable size and current capacity.
  • SWA, steel wire armour. Adds mechanical protection for buried or exposed runs, and the armour is often used as part of the earth path where the design allows it. Terminate it with the correct cable gland.
  • Fire-rated cable. Used where a circuit must keep working in a fire, such as alarms and emergency lighting. These are tested to circuit-integrity standards such as IEC 60331.
  • Instrument and control cable. Usually screened and kept apart from power cables to limit interference, which is why the route notes matter.

Worked example: turning the schedule into an order

Suppose a small schedule has eight cables with lengths of 45, 60, 35, 120, 80, 25, 150 and 40 m. The total is 555 m, and the average is about 69 m. Cables are cut from drums, and real routes are longer than a drawing suggests because of slack, bends and termination length, so an allowance is normally added. At an illustrative 10%, the order quantity is 555 × 1.1 = 610.5 m. The right allowance depends on the project, so treat 10% only as an example. Totals by cable type, which the cable schedule builder keeps as you add rows, give the quantity for each drum order.

What a schedule does not check

A schedule only records what you enter. It does not confirm that the cable can carry the current, that the voltage drop is acceptable or that the cable will survive a fault. Do those separately, with derating factors, voltage drop and short-circuit current, and then update the schedule with the sizes that result.

Habits that keep it useful

  • Use a tag system from the start, and never reuse a tag for a different cable.
  • Issue it with revisions, since the cable schedule changes whenever equipment moves.
  • Record as-built lengths once the cables are installed, so the handover set is accurate.
  • Test every cable. Insulation resistance results are normally recorded against the cable tag, as described in insulation resistance testing.
  • Keep tray and route notes honest, including separation between power, control and instrument cables. See tray, ladder, trunking and conduit.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Give every cable a unique tag and keep it for life.
  • ✓Add a length allowance for slack and termination when ordering, and check the allowance for your project.
  • ✓Size cables with separate calculations, then record the results in the schedule.
  • ✓Update the schedule with as-built lengths and tie test records to each tag.
