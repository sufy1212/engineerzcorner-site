RTD vs Thermocouple Explained

A Pt100 and a Type K thermocouple both end up as a temperature on the screen, but one changes its resistance and the other generates a tiny voltage. That difference decides how each one goes wrong: lead resistance for one, cold-junction error for the other.

Engineerz CorneRReference · IEC 60751 / IEC 60584
01
An RTD changes resistance with temperature: a Pt100 reads 100.00 Ω at 0 °C and 138.51 Ω at 100 °C. A thermocouple generates a small voltage from the temperature difference between its two junctions.
02
Each has its own signature error. For RTDs it is lead resistance (about 2.6 °C per ohm in a 2-wire Pt100). For thermocouples it is the cold-junction temperature, whose error passes straight into the reading.
03
Tolerance is a formula, not a single number: Class A Pt100 is ±(0.15 + 0.002|t|) °C, and a Type K Class 2 is the larger of ±2.5 °C and 0.75% of reading.
04
RTDs are the accurate, stable choice at moderate temperatures. Thermocouples cover higher temperatures, with Type K rated up to about 1200 °C, and are the more rugged and faster option.

Two different physical effects

A resistance temperature detector (RTD) is a length of platinum whose resistance rises predictably with temperature. A Pt100 is defined as 100 Ω at 0 °C with a temperature coefficient of 0.00385 per degree, so the change between 0 and 100 °C is 38.5 Ω, which is about 0.385 Ω per °C near ambient.

A thermocouple is two dissimilar metal wires joined at the measuring (hot) junction. The voltage it produces depends on the difference between the hot junction and the reference (cold) junction, not on the hot junction alone. Type K (nickel-chromium / nickel-aluminium) is the industrial default, with a sensitivity of about 41 µV per °C; Type J gives about 52 µV per °C.

Pt100: from temperature to resistance

Above 0 °C the IEC 60751 relationship is R(t) = R0 (1 + A t + B t²), with R0 = 100 Ω, A = 3.9083×10−3 and B = −5.775×10−7. Below 0 °C a cubic term is added.

Lead resistance: 2-, 3- and 4-wire

The transmitter measures the whole loop, so the cable resistance is added to the sensor's. With a 2-wire connection nothing cancels it. On a Pt100 each ohm of lead resistance reads as roughly 2.6 °C.

Worked example: 50 m of 1.5 mm² copper to the sensor, so 100 m of conductor in the loop. R = 0.0172 × 100 / 1.5 = 1.15 Ω, which reads as about 3.0 °C too high. A 3-wire connection lets the transmitter measure one lead and subtract it, so it cancels the error as long as the leads match in length and gauge, leaving a small residual. A 4-wire connection measures the sensor with separate current and sense pairs and removes lead resistance entirely, which is why it is used for calibration and high-accuracy work.

The classes also have validity ranges, which differ between wire-wound and film elements: for wire-wound, Class A covers −100 to +450 °C and Class B −196 to +600 °C; film elements are narrower (Class A −30 to +300 °C, Class B −50 to +500 °C). At 150 °C, Class A allows ±0.45 °C and Class B allows ±1.05 °C. The tolerance describes the sensing element; a complete assembly, wiring and transmitter add their own error.

Thermocouple tolerance classes (IEC 60584-1)

For types K, N and J, Class 1 is ±1.5 °C up to 375 °C and ±0.4% of reading above; Class 2 is ±2.5 °C up to 333 °C and ±0.75% above. Equivalently, the larger of the fixed value and the percentage applies. Type K Class 1 is defined from −40 to 1000 °C and Class 2 from −40 to 1200 °C. The values assume a 0 °C reference junction.

Compare a Class A Pt100 at 400 °C: ±0.95 °C. That gap, and the better long-term stability of an RTD, is why RTDs win wherever the temperature allows.

Cold-junction compensation

The thermocouple's voltage is the difference between the hot junction and the reference junction at the instrument terminals. Old practice held the reference in an ice bath at 0 °C. Instruments now measure the terminal block temperature, usually with a thermistor or RTD, and add the equivalent voltage electronically. That is cold-junction compensation (CJC).

Any error in the measured cold-junction temperature passes one for one into the reading. With a sensitivity of about 41 µV/°C, a 5 °C error at the terminals is about 0.2 mV of error, and 5 °C of temperature error on the screen.

Run the thermocouple wire, or compensating cable of the same type, all the way to the CJC point. Copper in between creates new junctions at the wrong places, and the instrument cannot compensate for them. The signal is also tiny (tens of microvolts per degree), so it is more exposed to noise than an RTD reading.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Pt100: 100.00 Ω at 0 °C, 138.51 Ω at 100 °C, about 0.385 Ω/°C. Type K: about 41 µV/°C relative to the cold junction.
  • ✓Use 3- or 4-wire for RTDs; in 2-wire each ohm of lead resistance is about 2.6 °C.
  • ✓Tolerances are formulas: Class A Pt100 ±(0.15 + 0.002|t|); Type K Class 2 the larger of ±2.5 °C or 0.75%.
  • ✓Cold-junction error equals reading error, so keep the thermocouple wire type all the way to the CJC.
  • ✓Confirm classes, ranges and conversion tables against the standard edition and the sensor datasheet.
