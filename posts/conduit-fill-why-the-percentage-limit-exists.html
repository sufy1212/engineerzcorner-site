Conduit Fill: Why 40% Isn't an Arbitrary Number

Passing the fill check doesn't mean the cables inside are rated for their full ampacity — that's a separate derating exercise.

02
Below the limit, cables can be pulled through without excessive friction or jamming against each other in the bends.
03
Above the limit, cables are packed tightly enough that trapped heat has nowhere to go, and bundled-cable ampacity derating gets worse.

The check itself

Fill %  =  (∑ Acable  /  Aconduit,internal) × 100
Acable = cross-sectional area of each cable's outer jacket · Aconduit,internal = internal cross-sectional area of the conduit

The arithmetic is simple: sum the cross-sectional area of every cable being pulled through, divide by the conduit's internal area, and check it against a maximum fill limit. Where the limit itself comes from is less obvious — and it's actually protecting against two unrelated problems.

Problem one: you have to be able to pull the cables in

Below a certain fill percentage, cables have enough clearance to slide past each other through straight runs and bends without excessive friction. As fill increases, cables press against each other and the conduit wall, and pulling tension rises — particularly through bends, where cables can jam against each other in the curve rather than sliding freely. Past a certain fill percentage, a pull that would otherwise be routine risks damaging cable insulation from the friction and compressive force alone, before the installation is even energized.

Problem two: heat has to be able to get out

Once cables are in service, a tightly packed conduit traps the heat each cable generates against its neighbours, instead of letting it dissipate to the surrounding air or structure. This is why ampacity tables for bundled or conduit-run cables carry separate derating factors on top of a single cable's free-air rating — the more cables grouped together, generally the larger the derating. Fill percentage and ampacity derating are related (both driven by how many cables share the same conduit) but they are separate checks: passing the fill limit tells you the cables physically fit and can be pulled in, not that their rated ampacity is unaffected by being grouped together.

Practical takeaway

Run both checks, not just one. A conduit under the fill limit with cables that still need a grouping derating applied to reach their actual safe current-carrying capacity is a common oversight — the fill check alone doesn't catch it.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Fill percentage is total cable cross-section against conduit internal area, checked against a maximum limit.
  • ✓The limit protects two things: pulling tension/jamming risk during installation, and heat dissipation once cables are energized.
  • ✓Passing the fill check doesn't mean cable ampacity is unaffected — grouping/bundling derating is a separate calculation.
  • ✓Always run the fill check and the ampacity derating check together, not just one or the other.
