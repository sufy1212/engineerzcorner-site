Junction Boxes Explained: Types, Parts & Ratings

A junction box looks like a plain metal or plastic can, but picking the wrong type — or sizing it wrong — is one of the most common causes of overheated, code-violating, and just plain unsafe connections.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical / Enclosures
01
A junction box's real job is to keep every splice enclosed, protected, and reachable — codes require splices to sit inside an approved box, not taped up loose in a wall or ceiling cavity.
02
The right box type — standard, pull, gang, weatherproof, explosion-proof, conduit body — is chosen mainly by environment and function, not just by how many cables need to fit.

What a junction box actually does

A junction box is an enclosure that houses electrical splices, taps, and terminations at a single, protected, accessible point. Wiring codes generally require every splice or connection to be made inside an approved box — not left floating in free air, and not buried behind drywall, plaster, or finished ceiling once the work is done. The box does three things at once: it contains the connection mechanically, isolates it from accidental contact and damage, and keeps it reachable for future inspection, testing, or rework.

Types of junction boxes and enclosures

The same basic idea — a box that houses a connection — takes very different forms depending on the environment and what's being connected.

Standard Junction Box
General purpose
The everyday box for housing splices along a circuit run — metal or plastic, mounted in a wall, ceiling, or on a surface, closed with a blank cover once the splice is made.
Pull Box
Long conduit runs
An oversized box placed along a long or bendy conduit run purely to let cable be pulled through in stages — usually no splices inside, just room to feed cable without exceeding bend limits.
Gang / Device Box
Switches & receptacles
Sized and shaped to mount a switch or receptacle directly, with "gang" referring to how many devices sit side by side in one box — single-gang, double-gang, and so on.
Weatherproof Box
Outdoor / wet locations
Gasketed body with a raised, hinged, or "in-use" cover so it stays sealed even with a cord plugged in — rated for outdoor, damp, or washdown areas rather than dry indoor space.
Explosion-Proof Box
Hazardous areas
Heavy cast body with a closely bolted, threaded, or flanged cover built to contain an internal arc or spark — used in Class/Zone-rated areas where flammable gas or dust may be present.
Conduit Body
"Condulet" fittings
A compact cast fitting (LB, LL, LR, T, C shapes) inserted directly into a conduit run to change direction or provide a pulling/access point, without needing a full-size box.
Terminal Box
Instrumentation / control
Houses a terminal strip rather than twisted splices — each field wire lands on its own numbered terminal, common on instrumentation, control, and multi-core cable interfaces.
Panel / Distribution Enclosure
Feeder & branch taps
A larger enclosure holding breakers, fuses, or terminal blocks that feed multiple downstream circuits — effectively a junction box scaled up with overcurrent protection built in.
Handhole / Underground Box
Buried cable runs
A below-grade concrete, polymer, or fibreglass box giving access to splices or pull points on underground conduit runs, with a load-rated cover flush with grade.

Main parts of a junction box

  1. 1
    Body / enclosureThe can itself — metal or nonmetallic — that physically houses the connection.
  2. 2
    Cover / lidCloses the box; can be a blank plate, a device plate, or a gasketed weatherproof cover.
  3. 3
    KnockoutsPre-scored circular cutouts for conduit or cable entry — removed only where an entry is actually needed.
  4. 4
    Cable connectors / clampsSecure the cable where it enters the box and keep pulling force off the splice inside.
  5. 5
    Terminal blocks / wire connectorsThe actual splice or connection point — twist-on connectors, terminal strips, or lugs.
  6. 6
    Grounding screw / pigtailBonds the box itself to the equipment grounding conductor.
  7. 7
    Mounting bracket / earsFixes the box to a stud, ceiling joist, or structural surface.
  8. 8
    Gasket / sealOn weatherproof and dust-tight enclosures, seals the cover against the body to hold the rating.

Box fill — sizing basics

Box fill capacity is a volume limit, not a visual "does it look full" judgement, and it's usually the actual constraint on how many conductors a box can take.

1Count every current-carrying conductor that enters the box, except any that pass straight through without a splice or termination.
2Count each device (switch or receptacle) as two conductors of the largest size connected to it.
3Count all grounding conductors in the box together as a single conductor.
4Count internal clamps or fittings as one additional conductor of the largest size present.
5Compare the total required volume against the box's rated cubic-inch (or cubic-centimetre) capacity, usually stamped inside the box.
6When it's close, size up — forcing extra conductors into a full box stresses insulation and makes proper splicing difficult.

Selection checklist

  • 1Environment: dry indoor, outdoor, washdown, or hazardous area
  • 2Required NEMA or IP rating for that environment
  • 3Material — metal for a grounding path and fire-rated areas, nonmetallic for corrosion or damp conditions
  • 4Expected number and size of conductors (box fill)
  • 5Number of conduit or cable entries needed
  • 6Accessibility — the box must stay reachable, never buried behind a finished surface
  • 7Mounting method available at the location — stud, surface, floor, or ceiling

Installation & inspection points

  • ✓Box fill stays within the box's rated cubic-inch capacity
  • ✓Every splice sits inside the box — none left in free air or behind drywall
  • ✓Only the knockouts actually in use are removed; the rest stay sealed
  • ✓Cable or conduit is secured to the box within the required distance of the entry
  • ✓Grounding conductor bonded to the box on metal enclosures
  • ✓Cover securely fastened, gasket intact on weatherproof types
  • ✓Box remains accessible once finishing work is done, not painted or plastered over
  • ✓Box depth is adequate for the number and size of devices installed
A junction box is only as good as its weakest opening — one unused knockout left uncapped, or one splice tucked just outside the box, undoes every other correct choice made around it.

Common applications

Residential wiring splices Lighting circuits Motor control wiring Solar DC combiner runs Outdoor / landscape lighting Hazardous-area process plants Underground cable runs Panelboard feeder taps

Key takeaways

  • ✓Every splice belongs inside an approved, accessible box — never taped in open air or buried behind a finished surface.
  • ✓Box type is driven by environment — dry indoor, washdown, hazardous, underground — as much as by the wiring going into it.
  • ✓Box fill capacity, not outward size, is what actually limits how many conductors a box can safely take.
  • ✓NEMA and IP ratings describe ingress protection only — they don't substitute for a box's electrical or hazardous-area rating.
