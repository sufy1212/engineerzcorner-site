LMTD Heat Exchanger Sizing

The temperature difference driving heat transfer isn't a simple average of the two ends — it's a log-mean, and getting the flow arrangement wrong changes how much area the exchanger actually needs.

Why "log-mean" and not just "mean"

Inside a heat exchanger, the temperature difference between the hot and cold streams isn't constant along its length — it's largest at one end and smallest at the other, and it doesn't shrink in a straight line between them. Averaging the two end values arithmetically would overstate the real driving force, because the temperature difference decays exponentially rather than linearly. The log-mean temperature difference (LMTD) accounts for that curve, and it's always smaller than the simple arithmetic average of the two ends — usually only slightly, but the gap grows as the two terminal differences pull further apart.

1Terminal Temperature Differences (ΔT1, ΔT2)

  • The gap between hot and cold stream temperatures at each end of the exchanger — not the inlet and outlet temperatures of either stream alone
  • Which stream temperature pairs with which at each end depends entirely on the flow arrangement, so the two must be assigned consistently before anything is calculated
  • These two numbers are the only inputs LMTD actually needs, beyond deciding which one is called ΔT1 versus ΔT2

2Flow Arrangement

  • Counter-current: the two streams flow in opposite directions — the hot stream's outlet meets the cold stream's inlet, keeping a driving temperature difference along the full length
  • Co-current (parallel): both streams flow the same direction — the temperature difference is largest at the shared inlet end and collapses fast, so the outlet end contributes little further heat transfer
  • For the same four terminal temperatures, counter-current always produces a larger LMTD, meaning less required area for the identical duty

3Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient (U)

  • Combines every resistance to heat transfer between the two fluids — film coefficients on each side, wall conduction, and any fouling layer — into a single number
  • Not a fixed property of the metal; it changes with fluid velocity, physical properties, and the exchanger geometry, so it has to be estimated or measured for the actual service
  • Fouling builds up over time in service and lowers U, which is why a clean-condition sizing calc is usually run against a fouled-condition design case as well

4Correction Factor (F)

  • The LMTD formula assumes pure counter-current or pure co-current flow; most shell-and-tube exchangers use multiple tube passes or crossflow, which fall short of that ideal
  • F is a multiplier (0 to 1) applied to the counter-current LMTD to correct for the real flow pattern, read from standard charts based on the temperature ranges and number of passes
  • A design that skips F on a multi-pass exchanger overstates the effective driving force and undersizes the required area

The formulas

LMTD  =  (ΔT1 − ΔT2)  /  ln(ΔT1 / ΔT2)
Undefined when ΔT1 equals ΔT2 exactly — in that limiting case, LMTD simply equals that common value.
Q  =  U × A × F × LMTD
Heat duty (Q) links required area (A) to the overall coefficient (U), the flow-arrangement correction (F), and the log-mean driving force — rearranged to solve for A once Q, U and F are known.

Working through the calculation

1Establish inlet and outlet temperatures for both the hot and cold streams from the process duty.
2Decide the flow arrangement and pair up the stream temperatures at each end to get ΔT1 and ΔT2.
3Calculate LMTD = (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1/ΔT2).
4If the exchanger isn't purely counter-current, look up the correction factor F for the actual pass arrangement.
5Estimate the overall heat transfer coefficient U for the fluids, geometry, and expected fouling condition.
6Solve for required area: A = Q / (U × F × LMTD).

Common terms, decoded

What each term is sensitive to

Flow-Arrangement Dependent
Time-in-Service Dependent
Pass-Count Dependent

A brand-new exchanger sized only against clean-condition U will show reduced performance as fouling accumulates, which is why fouling factors are built into U from the start rather than added as an afterthought. Similarly, adding tube passes for compactness pulls F further from 1, quietly working against the extra length gained — the two design choices trade off against each other rather than both being free wins.

Key takeaways

  • ✓LMTD is a log-mean, not an arithmetic average — it's always smaller than the simple average of the two terminal ΔTs.
  • ✓Counter-current flow produces a larger LMTD than co-current for the same four terminal temperatures, meaning less required area.
  • ✓Multi-pass or crossflow exchangers need a correction factor F applied to the counter-current LMTD — skipping it undersizes the area.
  • ✓U isn't a fixed property — it depends on fluid velocity, geometry, and fouling, and typically needs both a clean and a fouled design case.
  • ✓Required area comes from A = Q / (U × F × LMTD) — every term in that equation has its own separate source of uncertainty.
