CT Sizing & Burden Explained

A current transformer can meter perfectly all day at rated load and still saturate the instant a real fault hits — because normal load and fault current test completely different specs.

Metering and protection want opposite behavior

A current transformer steps primary current down to a standard secondary value — usually 5 A or 1 A — that relays and meters are built to accept. What separates a metering CT from a protection CT isn't the ratio, it's what each is designed to do once current climbs well above normal. A metering CT (class 0.5, 1, or 3) is meant to stay accurate near rated load, where billing and measurement actually happen, and is allowed — even expected — to saturate on high fault current, which protects the sensitive metering equipment behind it from a surge it was never meant to see. A protection CT (class 5P, 10P, or PX) does the opposite: it has to stay accurate through the fault, often to many times rated current, because that's exactly when the relay needs a faithful signal to decide whether and how fast to trip.

1Ratio & Rated Secondary Current

  • Standard ratio (e.g., 400/5, 400/1) chosen with headroom above the maximum continuous load current, not sized tight to it
  • The secondary rating (1 A or 5 A) has to match what the connected relay or meter is actually built to accept
  • Oversizing the ratio too far above actual load current wastes accuracy at the low end — the CT spends its normal operating life in the least accurate part of its range

2Burden (VA)

  • The total load the CT secondary has to drive — the connected relay or meter's rated burden, plus the resistance of the secondary lead wiring itself
  • Lead-wire burden grows with distance and shrinks with a larger conductor cross-section; long runs on small-gauge wire can outweigh the device burden entirely
  • Actual connected burden has to be checked against the CT's rated burden at its stated accuracy — a CT accurate at its rated 15 VA burden isn't automatically accurate wired at 25 VA of real-world load

3Accuracy Class & ALF

  • A protection CT's class (e.g., 5P20) states two things at once: the error limit at its Accuracy Limit Factor (5% error, in this case) and the ALF itself (20× rated current)
  • Below the ALF, the CT reproduces primary current faithfully; past it, the core saturates and secondary current stops tracking the real fault current
  • High-impedance protection schemes instead specify a class PX CT by its knee-point voltage — the point where a further rise in exciting voltage produces a disproportionate rise in exciting current, marking the onset of saturation

4Short-Circuit Withstand

  • Thermal withstand (Ith) — the rms current the CT primary can carry for a defined time (typically 1 second) without thermal damage
  • Dynamic withstand (Idyn) — the peak asymmetrical current the winding and mounting can survive mechanically during the first cycle of a fault
  • A separate check from accuracy class entirely — a CT can be perfectly accurate and still not survive the mechanical and thermal stress of the system's actual fault level

The formula for connected burden

Burden (VA)  =  Isec2 × (Rdevice + Rleads)
Rleads is the round-trip resistance of the secondary wiring — out to the relay and back — not just the one-way length, which is why lead burden climbs faster with distance than it might first appear.

Working through the sizing

1Determine the maximum continuous primary current and select a standard ratio with reasonable headroom above it.
2Confirm the secondary current rating (1 A or 5 A) matches what the connected relay or meter accepts.
3Sum the connected burden: device burden (VA) plus the round-trip resistance loss of the secondary leads.
4Choose the accuracy class for the application — metering class for measurement, protection class with sufficient ALF (or PX with adequate knee-point voltage) for relaying.
5Verify the stated accuracy actually holds at the real connected burden, not just the CT's rated burden on the nameplate.
6Check thermal (Ith) and dynamic (Idyn) short-circuit withstand against the system's actual fault level, separately from the accuracy check.

Where it actually goes wrong

The failure that's easy to miss at design stage: a CT specified years ago for an electromechanical relay with a high burden gets reused with a modern digital relay whose input burden is a fraction of that — which is usually fine, since lower burden generally improves accuracy margin. The opposite mistake is more damaging and far more common — long secondary cable runs to a remote panel, sized on installed convenience rather than calculated lead resistance, quietly pushing connected burden above what the CT was rated for. The CT still reads correctly at load current, so nothing looks wrong during commissioning. It's only during an actual fault, when the core saturates earlier than the protection scheme assumed, that the relay under-reads the fault current and either fails to trip or trips slower than it was coordinated to.

What each spec is sensitive to

Burden Dependent
Lead-Length Dependent
Fault-Level Dependent

A CT sized correctly for today's system fault level can end up under-rated after a network reinforcement raises available fault current at the same busbar — the ALF and short-circuit withstand that were adequate before no longer are. Both checks belong on the list whenever upstream fault levels change, not just at initial design.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Metering CTs are meant to saturate on fault current to protect meters; protection CTs are meant to stay accurate through the fault — the two classes serve opposite goals.
  • ✓Burden includes the secondary lead-wire resistance, not just the connected device's rated VA — long or thin lead runs can dominate the total.
  • ✓A protection CT's accuracy class only holds up to its ALF (or, for class PX, up to its knee-point voltage) — past that, the core saturates.
  • ✓Short-circuit thermal (Ith) and dynamic (Idyn) withstand are separate specs from accuracy — both need checking against actual system fault level.
  • ✓A CT reading correctly at normal load tells you nothing about its behavior during a fault — that's a completely different part of its rating.
