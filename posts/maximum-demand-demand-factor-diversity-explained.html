Maximum Demand, Demand Factor and Diversity: Sizing a Supply Below the Connected Load

Connected load is never the load you draw. Here is how demand factor and diversity bring a schedule down to a realistic maximum demand, with a worked example and the naming traps to watch for.

Demand factor is maximum demand over connected load, always 1.0 or less.

Diversity accounts for loads or feeders peaking at different times.

A 50 kW connected schedule becomes 34.2 kW of demand, or 58.1 A at 400 V three-phase, in the worked example.

Adding up every nameplate on a site gives a number nobody will ever draw at once. Sizing a supply for the connected load wastes money on a transformer, cable and breaker that stay lightly loaded, while sizing it too tightly risks nuisance trips and overheating. Demand factors and diversity are the tools engineers use to land between the two.

Three terms that get mixed up

Demand factor

Maximum demand divided by total connected load for one load or one group, always 1.0 or less. It reflects that equipment is not all running, or not all running at full output, at the same moment.

Diversity

It covers how the peaks of different loads or feeders fall at different times. Sizing a shared upstream point on the sum of their individual peaks overstates what it will actually carry.

Naming differs between references. In most formal definitions the diversity factor is the sum of individual maximum demands divided by the system maximum demand, so it is 1.0 or more, and its reciprocal is the coincidence factor, 1.0 or less. The load / demand calculator on this site takes a single “overall diversity factor” that multiplies the summed demand, so it behaves like a coincidence factor: 1.0 means all categories peak together, and lower values mean they peak at different times. Know which convention a table or colleague is using before applying a number.

Worked example

CategoryConnected (kW)Demand factorDemand (kW)
Lighting50.904.5
General power and sockets150.507.5
HVAC200.9018.0
Motors100.808.0
Total5038.0

With an overall diversity multiplier of 0.9 the maximum demand is 38.0 × 0.9 = 34.2 kW. The effective overall factor is 34.2 ÷ 50 = 0.684, so the supply is sized on about 68% of the connected load. For a three-phase 400 V supply at a power factor of 0.85, the current is 34,200 ÷ (√3 × 400 × 0.85) = 58.1 A, and a single-phase 230 V supply would carry about 175 A for the same demand.

Where the factors come from

The factors in the table are planning values: lighting near 0.9, general power commonly between 0.4 and 0.7, HVAC between 0.8 and 1.0, and motors between 0.75 and 0.85 depending on duty. They are only starting points. Wiring codes and utilities publish demand-factor tables by occupancy type, and these vary by country, so use the table that governs your project. Continuous loads, such as process equipment that runs flat out, deserve a factor close to 1.0, and loads that are planned to expand need a margin.

Using the result

  • Sizing the supply. The demand current sets the incoming supply, main breaker and transformer, with margin for growth. See load estimation as the first step in electrical design.
  • Not the cable or branch circuits. Each final circuit is still sized for the load it serves. Demand factors apply upstream, where many circuits are combined.
  • Motors and starting. Demand current does not capture starting inrush, so check generator or transformer capacity against starting separately, as in transformer and generator sizing for motor starting.
  • Cost. What you pay for demand on a tariff is a related but separate question, covered in the running cost of a load.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Apply a demand factor within a schedule, then a diversity or coincidence factor across the summed groups.
  • ✓Check which convention a table uses, because diversity can be quoted as 1.0 or more and coincidence as 1.0 or less.
  • ✓Use the demand factors from the code or utility that governs the project, not generic values.
  • ✓Check motor starting and future growth separately from the demand figure.
