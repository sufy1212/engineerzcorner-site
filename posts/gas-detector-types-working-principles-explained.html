Gas Detector Types & Working Principles Explained

Five different sensing technologies, five different jobs. Here's how catalytic bead, electrochemical, infrared, semiconductor and photoionization detectors each turn a gas concentration into a signal — and why no single type covers every application.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical / Safety Instrumentation
01
Every gas detector converts a gas concentration into an electrical signal — the five common types differ in what physical effect they use to do that.
02
Catalytic bead and semiconductor sensors both work by resistance change; electrochemical and PID both work by generating a measurable current.
03
Infrared detectors measure gas by absorption, not reaction — which is why they don't get "poisoned" the way catalytic sensors can.
04
No single sensor type covers every gas or every application — the target gas, environment and required sensitivity all drive the selection.

1. Catalytic bead (pellistor)

A catalytic bead detector uses two beads — an active bead coated with a catalyst and a reference bead — each forming one arm of a Wheatstone bridge. Combustible gas reaching the active bead burns on its catalytic surface, raising its temperature and therefore its electrical resistance. The reference bead doesn't react, so the bridge becomes unbalanced by an amount proportional to how much gas is present, and that imbalance is the output signal.

  • ✓Gas burns on the active bead, causing a temperature rise.
  • ✓The resulting resistance change is measured in a Wheatstone bridge.
  • ✓Output signal is proportional to gas concentration.

2. Electrochemical (EC)

Gas diffuses through a permeable membrane and reaches a working electrode inside an electrolyte cell. There it undergoes an electrochemical reaction against a counter electrode, with a reference electrode holding the working electrode at a fixed potential. That reaction produces a small current directly proportional to how much gas reacted — no external power is needed to generate the signal itself, only to measure it.

  • ✓Gas diffuses through the membrane and reaches the working electrode.
  • ✓An electrochemical reaction occurs, producing a current.
  • ✓Current is proportional to gas concentration.

3. Infrared (IR)

An IR source shines light through a gas chamber toward an IR detector. Many gases — CO₂ among them — absorb infrared light at specific wavelengths, so gas molecules in the chamber's path reduce the amount of light reaching the detector. The amount of absorption, measured by comparing detected intensity against a reference, is proportional to gas concentration. Because nothing is consumed or reacted, IR sensors avoid the poisoning and drift that can affect catalytic sensors.

  • ✓IR light passes through the gas chamber.
  • ✓Gas molecules absorb specific IR wavelengths.
  • ✓Absorption measured by the detector is proportional to gas concentration.

4. Semiconductor (metal oxide)

A heated metal-oxide semiconductor material changes its electrical resistance when target gas molecules react at its surface. That heater is essential — the reaction only proceeds efficiently at an elevated operating temperature, which is also why these sensors draw more standby power than most other types. The resistance change is converted directly into an output signal.

  • ✓Gas reacts with the heated semiconductor material.
  • ✓A resistance change occurs.
  • ✓The change in resistance is converted to an output signal.

5. Photoionization detector (PID)

A UV lamp ionizes gas molecules as they pass through the sensing chamber, knocking electrons loose and creating charged ions. An ion collector electrode gathers those ions, generating a current proportional to how many molecules were ionized — and therefore to gas concentration. PIDs are widely used for volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that other sensor types don't respond well to.

  • ✓The UV lamp ionizes gas molecules.
  • ✓Ions are collected at the electrode, generating current.
  • ✓Current is proportional to gas concentration.

Comparing the five types

Key takeaways

  • ✓Catalytic bead and semiconductor sensors both output a resistance change; electrochemical and PID sensors both output a current.
  • ✓Infrared sensing is non-consumptive — the gas isn't reacted, only its absorption of light is measured.
  • ✓Electrochemical and PID sensors are typically the choice for toxic gases and VOCs where catalytic combustion isn't practical.
  • ✓Selecting a gas detector means matching the sensing technology to the target gas, not just picking whatever's cheapest or most familiar.
