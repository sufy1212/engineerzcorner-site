Stakeholder Power-Interest Grid: Sorting Attention, Not Just People

Not every stakeholder needs the same amount of attention, and treating them as if they do spreads a project manager's time thin exactly where it can't afford to be.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Stakeholder Management
01
The grid plots every stakeholder on two axes: how much power they have over the project, and how much interest they take in it.
02
Where a stakeholder lands decides the engagement strategy — not just how much they're liked or how senior they are.
03
High power, high interest gets the most engagement effort. High power, low interest still needs attention — just a different kind.
04
Position on the grid isn't fixed — a stakeholder's power or interest can shift as the project moves through its lifecycle.

The two axes

Every stakeholder gets placed against two separate questions, kept deliberately independent of each other:

AxisQuestion it answers
PowerHow much ability does this stakeholder have to influence the project's direction, funding, or approval?
InterestHow much attention does this stakeholder actually pay to the project's progress and outcomes?

Keeping them separate matters because they don't move together. A regulator can have enormous power over a project while paying it almost no routine attention until something triggers a review. A site neighbour can have very high interest — watching every truck movement — with almost no formal power to change anything. Collapsing both into one "how important is this person" score erases that distinction.

The four quadrants

QuadrantEngagement strategyTypical example
High power, high interestManage closely — regular direct engagementProject sponsor, client's project director
High power, low interestKeep satisfied — enough visibility to prevent surprises reaching themSenior executive, funding authority
Low power, high interestKeep informed — regular updates, low decision-making burdenEnd users, site neighbours, technical leads
Low power, low interestMonitor — minimum effort, watch for quadrant shiftsPeripheral departments, distant suppliers

Why "keep everyone happy" isn't a strategy

Uniform engagement is a worse use of time than none at all A project manager with limited hours who spreads equal engagement effort across every stakeholder ends up under-serving the ones in "manage closely" and over-serving the ones in "monitor." The grid isn't about deciding who matters — every stakeholder on it matters enough to be listed — it's about deciding what kind and how much engagement each one actually needs, so the limited hours available go where a lapse in engagement would cause the most damage.

Worked example

A mid-size infrastructure upgrade, four stakeholders placed on the grid:

Placement & strategy

Client project sponsorHigh power, high interest → Manage closely
Regulatory authorityHigh power, low interest → Keep satisfied
Facility operations teamLow power, high interest → Keep informed
Adjacent-site tenantLow power, low interest → Monitor

The regulatory authority gets no weekly updates — that would waste their time and the project's — but it gets enough proactive visibility on compliance milestones that a routine inspection never turns into a surprise escalation. The operations team, with real interest but no formal authority, gets frequent updates precisely because keeping them informed avoids the kind of resistance that surfaces only once a change lands on their desk unannounced.

Positions aren't permanent

A stakeholder's quadrant can shift as the project moves through its lifecycle. A regulator with low interest during design can move sharply toward high interest once construction starts generating public complaints. A distant supplier with low power during planning can gain power the moment their delivery sits on the critical path. Re-plotting the grid at major project milestones — not just once at kickoff — is what keeps the engagement plan matching who actually needs attention now, not who needed it six months ago.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Score power and interest separately — they don't move together, and collapsing them loses the distinction the grid exists to make.
  • ✓Quadrant decides engagement strategy, not stakeholder importance — every stakeholder on the grid matters; the grid decides how.
  • ✓High power, low interest still needs proactive attention — just enough to prevent surprises, not routine involvement.
  • ✓Re-plot at major milestones — power and interest both shift as a project moves through its lifecycle.
