Functional Safety & SIL Explained

A SIL is not a rating you buy on a datasheet. It is a target for how much a specific safety function must reduce risk, and it is only claimed when failure probability, hardware architecture and engineering process all pass together. Here is what the number means, where the bands come from, and how a real loop gets checked against them.

Engineerz CorneRReference · IEC 61508 / IEC 61511

What functional safety actually means

Functional safety is safety that depends on something working. A relief valve, a high-high trip that closes a shutdown valve, a light curtain that stops a press: each only protects anyone if the function is carried out when called upon. That is different from inherent safety (a thicker vessel wall, a lower inventory), where nothing has to act.

Because these functions are built from sensors, programmable logic and actuators that can all fail, the standards ask for an evidence-based argument that the function will work with a stated reliability. The Safety Integrity Level is the shorthand for how demanding that argument must be.

The standards family

StandardScopeWho it is written for
IEC 61508Generic umbrella for electrical, electronic and programmable electronic safety systems; defines SIL 1–4Device and system manufacturers
IEC 61511Process-sector implementation of IEC 61508 for safety instrumented systemsDesigners, integrators and plant owners
ISO 13849-1Machinery safety-related control parts; uses Performance Levels PL a–eMachine builders
IEC 62061Machinery functional safety; uses SIL 1–3 (SIL 4 is not used for machinery)Machine builders
Which one applies to you: a plant installing a high-level trip uses IEC 61511. A transmitter manufacturer proving its device is SIL-capable works to IEC 61508. A guarded machine line uses ISO 13849-1 or IEC 62061. IEC 61511-1 was developed as the process-sector implementation of IEC 61508:2010, and its scope covers SIL 1 up to SIL 4, with SIL 1 as the floor below which it does not apply.

The safety instrumented function

SIF vs SIS
Function vs the system that carries it out

A safety instrumented function (SIF) is one specific protective action against one specific hazard, for example “close the inlet valve when level reaches high-high”. It is built from three subsystems in series: sensor(s), a logic solver, and final element(s). The safety instrumented system (SIS) is the collection of hardware that implements one or more SIFs. Each SIF gets its own required SIL, set by the hazard it addresses, so one SIS can contain SIL 1 and SIL 2 functions side by side.

What the SIL bands are

A SIL is defined by how often the function may fail dangerously. Which measure applies depends on how often the function is called on. Low demand mode means at most about one demand per year, which covers most process trips. High demand or continuous mode means more than one demand per year, which is typical of machinery.

SILPFDavg (low demand)Risk reduction factor (1/PFDavg)PFH per hour (high / continuous)
SIL 1≥10⁻² to <10⁻¹10 to 100≥10⁻⁶ to <10⁻⁵
SIL 2≥10⁻³ to <10⁻²100 to 1,000≥10⁻⁷ to <10⁻⁶
SIL 3≥10⁻⁴ to <10⁻³1,000 to 10,000≥10⁻⁸ to <10⁻⁷
SIL 4≥10⁻⁵ to <10⁻⁴10,000 to 100,000≥10⁻⁹ to <10⁻⁸

The numbers describe the whole function. The sensor, logic solver and final element PFDavg values add up, and the sum has to land inside the target band.

Where the required SIL comes from

Nobody picks a SIL because it sounds safe. It comes out of a hazard and risk assessment. The plant defines a tolerable frequency for the hazardous outcome, then credits each independent protection layer for the risk it removes. Whatever risk reduction is still missing is assigned to the SIF. A layer of protection analysis (LOPA) is one common way to do this arithmetic; risk graphs and risk matrices are others.

Illustrative LOPA: initiating event 0.2 /yr · tolerable outcome 10⁻⁴ /yr · one other layer, PFD 0.1
Required SIF PFD ≤ 10⁻⁴ ÷ (0.2 × 0.1) = 5 × 10⁻³ → RRF ≥ 200 → SIL 2

Those inputs are made-up round numbers to show the method. A real study uses the company's own risk criteria and credited layers.

What it takes to claim a SIL

Hitting the PFDavg band is only one of several gates. A SIF has to pass all of them:

  • ✓Random hardware failures. The summed PFDavg (or PFH) of sensor, logic solver and final element sits inside the target band.
  • ✓Architectural constraints. Each subsystem has enough hardware fault tolerance for its safe failure fraction and device type (tables below).
  • ✓Systematic capability (SC1–SC4). Systematic faults, such as design and software errors, cannot be calculated away. A device’s SC says how rigorous its development process was.
  • ✓Lifecycle and management. Specification, verification, proof testing, change control and competence are all part of the claim. A calculation without them is not a SIL.

Failure rates, SFF and device type

Safety data sheets split a device’s failure rate into categories. Safe failures push the process toward the safe state. Dangerous failures stop the function from working, and the question is whether the device’s own diagnostics catch them.

λD = λDD + λDU (dangerous detected + dangerous undetected)
SFF = (λS + λDD) ÷ (λS + λD)   ·   DC = λDD ÷ λD

Only λDU matters for PFDavg, because undetected dangerous failures sit hidden until a proof test or a real demand finds them. Example: λS = 4×10⁻⁷, λDD = 3×10⁻⁷, λDU = 1×10⁻⁷ per hour gives SFF = 87.5% and diagnostic coverage = 75%.

Devices are also classed by complexity. Type A elements have well-defined failure modes and no complex software, such as a pressure switch or a simple valve. Type B elements are complex, typically with a microprocessor, such as an electronic pressure transmitter.

Architectural constraints, Route 1H (IEC 61508-2)

This table gives the maximum SIL a subsystem can claim for its type, SFF and hardware fault tolerance (HFT). HFT of 1 means one dangerous fault can occur without losing the function.

SFFType A: HFT 0 / 1 / 2Type B: HFT 0 / 1 / 2
<60%SIL 1 / SIL 2 / SIL 3Not allowed / SIL 1 / SIL 2
60% to <90%SIL 2 / SIL 3 / SIL 4SIL 1 / SIL 2 / SIL 3
90% to <99%SIL 3 / SIL 4 / SIL 4SIL 2 / SIL 3 / SIL 4
≥99%SIL 3 / SIL 4 / SIL 4SIL 3 / SIL 4 / SIL 4
Reading it: the 87.5% SFF Type B transmitter above, used alone (HFT 0), is capped at SIL 1 on this table. Pair it with a second transmitter (HFT 1) and the cap rises to SIL 2; reaching SIL 3 needs an SFF of 90% or more at HFT 1. A second route, Route 2H, replaces SFF with proven failure-rate data and a minimum HFT per SIL; use whichever route your standard and device certificates allow.

Voting architectures and PFDavg

Redundancy is written as MooN: M channels must agree out of N to trip. 1oo2 trips if either channel sees the demand, so it is more likely to catch a dangerous failure but also more likely to trip spuriously. The simplified low-demand equations below use the proof test interval TI and a common cause fraction β.

VotingHFTSimplified PFDavg
1oo10λDU × TI / 2
1oo21[(1−β) λDU TI]² / 3 + β λDU TI / 2
2oo20λDU × TI
2oo31[(1−β) λDU TI]² + β λDU TI / 2

These ignore repair time, diagnostics and imperfect proof tests, so treat them as screening estimates. IEC 61508-6 gives fuller equations, and a certified SIL verification tool is the normal route for a final answer.

Worked example: a high-level trip

The values below are illustrative, not from any datasheet. Proof test interval TI = 1 year (8,760 h), β = 5%.

CaseSensorLogicValveTotal PFDavgResult
A. All 1oo18.8×10⁻⁴1.0×10⁻⁴4.4×10⁻³5.4×10⁻³SIL 2 band (RRF ≈ 187)
B. Valve 1oo28.8×10⁻⁴1.0×10⁻⁴2.4×10⁻⁴1.2×10⁻³SIL 2 band (RRF ≈ 821)
C. Sensor and valve 1oo24.5×10⁻⁵1.0×10⁻⁴2.4×10⁻⁴3.9×10⁻⁴SIL 3 band (RRF ≈ 2,585)

Inputs: sensor λDU = 200 FIT (2×10⁻⁷/h), valve λDU = 1,000 FIT (1×10⁻⁶/h), logic solver λDU = 22.8 FIT (PFDavg ≈ 1.0×10⁻⁴). You can run your own numbers in the SIL Verification Calculator. Three things stand out. First, the valve dominates in case A, which is typical: final elements are usually the weakest link. Second, fixing only the valve in case B still lands just inside SIL 2, because the sensor and logic contributions now matter. Third, case C reaches the SIL 3 PFDavg band, but the SIF only claims SIL 3 if each subsystem also passes the architectural constraints, systematic capability and lifecycle gates above.

Proof test interval is the other lever. In case A, halving TI to six months cuts the total from 5.4×10⁻³ to 2.7×10⁻³, because PFDavg scales almost linearly with TI for a 1oo1 subsystem.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓“We bought a SIL 3 transmitter, so the loop is SIL 3.” A SIL 3 capable device is a building block. The loop still has to meet PFDavg, architecture and lifecycle requirements with the actual valve, logic solver and test plan.
  • ✓Using a vendor PFDavg without checking its test interval. A datasheet number is only valid for the proof test interval and assumptions stated in the safety manual.
  • ✓Assuming proof tests are perfect. If the test does not exercise every dangerous failure mode, the real PFDavg is worse than the simplified formula says.
  • ✓Adding redundancy and ignoring common cause. Two identical transmitters on the same tapping share failure causes. β puts a floor under what duplication can buy, and 1oo2 also raises the spurious trip rate.
  • ✓Mixing up SIL with the PL or the demand mode. A low-demand PFDavg band cannot be applied to a high-demand function, and a PL is not automatically a SIL.

How it fits with what you already have

Alarms are not a SIFA basic process control alarm that asks an operator to act can be credited as a protection layer, but only a function that meets the SIL requirements counts as a SIF.
Relief devices sit alongsideA pressure safety valve is an independent mechanical layer. It is credited in the risk study, then the SIF covers what is left.
Hazardous-area rules are separateZone classification and intrinsic safety control ignition risk in the plant. Functional safety is about whether the protective function works when called.

Key takeaways

  • ✓SIL is a property of a safety function, set by a risk assessment and verified for the whole sensor, logic and final element chain.
  • ✓Low demand bands run from PFDavg 10⁻¹–10⁻² (SIL 1) to 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴ (SIL 4); high demand uses PFH per hour.
  • ✓PFDavg alone never grants a SIL: architectural constraints (SFF, HFT, device type), systematic capability and the lifecycle must hold too.
  • ✓Final elements and proof test intervals usually drive the number; redundancy helps but common cause failure limits it.
  • ✓Simplified equations are for screening. Confirm real designs against IEC 61508/61511 and a qualified functional safety engineer.
