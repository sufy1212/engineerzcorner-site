Retaining Wall Design Basics: Why Overturning, Sliding & Bearing All Have to Pass

A retaining wall doesn't fail because the stem cracks — it fails because one of three independent stability checks was quietly left short. Here's what each one is actually checking, and the safety factors engineers design to.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Retaining Wall Stability
01
A retaining wall is checked against three separate failure modes — overturning, sliding, and bearing — and it has to pass all three, not just the one that looks governing at first glance.
02
Every check compares a destabilizing force from the retained soil against a resisting force from the wall's own weight and the soil beneath it.
03
Typical minimum safety factors are 2.0 for overturning, 1.5 for sliding, and an allowable bearing pressure check on the founding soil — though local codes can adjust these.
04
Backfill drainage and surcharge loads change the active pressure driving all three checks, so they're never purely a geometry problem.

The load doing the pushing

Retained soil doesn't sit still against a wall — it exerts lateral pressure that increases roughly linearly with depth, forming a triangular pressure distribution behind the wall. This is the active earth pressure, and it's what every stability check ultimately has to resist.

Pa = ½ · Ka · γ · H²
Pa = active thrust per metre of wall · Ka = active earth pressure coefficient (from soil friction angle) · γ = soil unit weight · H = retained height — acting at H/3 above the base

Because Pa scales with the square of retained height, doubling the wall height roughly quadruples the overturning demand — which is why tall walls need disproportionately wider bases, not just taller stems.

Three checks, three different failure modes

Each one uses the same forces, compared a different way
Overturning
FS ≥ 2.0 typical
Rotation about the toe. Resisting moment from wall and soil weight vs. overturning moment from active thrust.
Sliding
FS ≥ 1.5 typical
Horizontal translation along the base. Friction (and passive resistance) vs. the active thrust's horizontal component.
Bearing
q₂ₓ ≤ q₀ₗₔ
Contact pressure under the base, including eccentricity, must stay within the soil's safe bearing capacity.

Overturning: moments about the toe

FSOT = ΣMresisting ÷ ΣMoverturning
Resisting moment = wall self-weight × lever arm to the toe, plus any soil on the heel · overturning moment = Pa × its H/3 lever arm above the base

If FSOT drops below the code minimum, the usual fix isn't a thicker stem — it's a wider heel, which pulls more soil weight onto the footing and pushes the resultant back toward the middle third of the base.

Sliding: friction at the base

FSSL = (μ · ΣV + Pp) ÷ Ph
μ = coefficient of friction between base and soil · ΣV = total vertical load on the base · Pp = passive resistance in front of the wall (often ignored for conservatism) · Ph = horizontal component of active thrust

Passive resistance in front of the toe is real but frequently excluded from the calculation, since shallow soil there can be disturbed, eroded, or simply absent at the time sliding matters most — treating it as a reserve rather than a relied-upon resistance.

Bearing: pressure under the base

qmax,min = (ΣV ÷ B) × (1 ± 6e ÷ B)
B = base width · e = eccentricity of the resultant from the base centreline — if e > B/6, part of the base lifts off and pressure redistributes over a smaller contact area

This is where overturning and bearing connect: a wall that barely clears its overturning safety factor usually has a large eccentricity, which concentrates pressure at the toe and can exceed allowable bearing capacity even though the moment check technically passed.

What changes the answer on site

  • 1Backfill drainage — a blocked weep hole turns dry backfill into saturated soil, raising γ and the active thrust it generates.
  • 2Surcharge loads — vehicles, stockpiled material, or an adjacent footing near the top of the wall add to Pa beyond the soil's own weight.
  • 3Backfill slope — sloped backfill above the wall increases Ka compared to a level surface at the same height.
  • 4Seismic loading — in active zones, a pseudo-static increment (e.g. Mononobe-Okabe) is added to the static active pressure.
  • 5Founding soil variability — the allowable bearing pressure should come from an actual geotechnical investigation, not an assumed value.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Overturning, sliding, and bearing are independent checks — a wall must clear all three, and the governing one isn't always obvious from geometry alone.
  • ✓Active earth pressure grows with the square of retained height, so tall walls need proportionally wider bases, not just thicker stems.
  • ✓A large eccentricity that barely passes overturning often concentrates bearing pressure at the toe — check both together.
  • ✓Drainage, surcharge, backfill slope, and seismic load all change the active thrust the wall has to resist — not just its own weight and geometry.
