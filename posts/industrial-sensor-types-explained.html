Industrial Sensor Types Explained

Every sensor converts a physical quantity into a signal a controller can read — but what it senses, how it senses it without contact, and what kind of signal comes out the back all change completely from one type to the next.

What an industrial sensor actually does

A sensor detects a physical condition — presence, distance, temperature, pressure, level, or rotation — and converts it into an electrical signal a PLC, DCS, or other controller can use. That signal is either discrete, meaning it simply switches on or off at a threshold, or analog, meaning it varies continuously to represent a measured value. Every sensor type below is really a different physical trick for detecting its target quantity without needing to physically touch or disturb it, and picking the right one starts with knowing exactly what needs to be sensed and what it's made of.

Types of industrial sensors

Different sensing principles trade off target material, sensing range, environmental tolerance, and whether the output is a simple switch or a continuous measurement.

Inductive Sensor
Detects metal only, no contact
Generates an oscillating magnetic field and detects the eddy currents induced when metal enters it. Fast, rugged, and completely unaffected by dust or oil — the default choice for detecting metal parts on machinery.
Capacitive Sensor
Detects almost any material
Senses the change in capacitance as any material — metal, plastic, liquid, powder — enters its field. Less precise than inductive, but useful for detecting non-metallic targets and liquid or solids levels through a container wall.
Photoelectric Sensor
Uses a light beam to detect a surface
Emits a light beam and detects a target either by interruption (through-beam), reflection off the target (diffuse), or reflection off a fixed reflector (retro-reflective). Long range and works on nearly any surface, but sensitive to color, gloss, and dirty lenses.
Ultrasonic Sensor
Measures distance by sound echo
Emits a high-frequency sound pulse and times its echo to measure distance. Works on clear, dark, or reflective targets that defeat photoelectric sensors — common for level detection and distance measurement in dusty environments.
RTD / Thermocouple
Temperature sensing
An RTD measures temperature via a resistance that changes predictably with heat; a thermocouple generates a small voltage from the junction of two dissimilar metals. RTDs are more accurate over a moderate range; thermocouples survive far higher temperatures.
Pressure Transmitter
Converts pressure to a signal
A diaphragm deflects under process pressure, and that deflection is converted into a proportional electrical output. Used across gauge, absolute, and differential pressure measurement in nearly every process and HVAC system.
Level Sensor
Point or continuous level
Covers a range of technologies — float switches for a simple point level, and ultrasonic, radar, or hydrostatic pressure sensors for continuous level — matched to the vessel, fluid, and precision the process actually needs.
Rotary Encoder
Measures position or speed
A rotating disc with optical or magnetic markings generates pulses as it turns, giving precise shaft position, speed, or direction feedback. Incremental encoders count pulses from a reference; absolute encoders report a unique position at every angle.

Discrete vs analog sensing

Selection checklist

  • 1Target material — metal only, any material, liquid, or a surface at a distance
  • 2Discrete presence/absence detection vs a continuous analog measurement
  • 3Required sensing range and repeatability/accuracy
  • 4Environmental conditions — dust, moisture, temperature extremes, washdown requirements
  • 5Output signal type compatible with the controller's input cards
  • 6Response speed required for the process or machine cycle time
  • 7Hazardous area classification, if the sensor is installed in a classified zone

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓Dirty or fogged photoelectric lenses, causing missed or false detections
  • ✓Capacitive sensors falsely triggering from humidity, condensation, or nearby metal
  • ✓Loose or corroded terminal connections producing an erratic analog signal
  • ✓Thermocouple junction drift or RTD lead-wire resistance errors on long cable runs
  • ✓Ultrasonic sensors misreading due to foam, vapor, or turbulence at the target surface
  • ✓Mechanical damage or misalignment shifting a sensor's detection point over time
A 4–20 mA reading of exactly 0 mA almost always means a wiring fault, not a real zero — that's precisely why the standard uses a live 4 mA floor instead of starting at zero: it keeps a broken wire distinguishable from a genuine low reading.

Common applications

Machine part & end-of-travel detection Conveyor & packaging line control Tank level & inventory monitoring Process temperature & pressure control loops Motor and shaft speed feedback Safety interlocks & presence detection

Key takeaways

  • ✓Start sensor selection from the target material and whether you need a switch or a measurement — not from a brand or a spec sheet.
  • ✓Inductive sees metal, capacitive sees nearly anything, photoelectric sees surfaces at range, ultrasonic sees through dust and fog.
  • ✓4–20 mA remains the standard analog output because it survives long cable runs and self-flags a broken wire.
  • ✓Dirty lenses and loose terminals cause more real-world sensor failures than the sensing element itself.
