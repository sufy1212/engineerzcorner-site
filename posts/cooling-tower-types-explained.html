Cooling Tower Types Explained

Every cooling tower does the same job — reject heat from condenser water to the atmosphere mostly through evaporation — but the airflow path, the draft mechanism, and whether the process water ever touches outside air all change with the type.

Engineerz CorneRReference · HVAC / Cooling Towers
01
A cooling tower rejects heat mainly by evaporation, not by simple convective cooling — a small amount of the water evaporates and carries away a disproportionate amount of heat, which is why towers can cool water below the surrounding air's dry-bulb temperature.
02
Draft mechanism (natural, forced, or induced) and airflow path (counterflow or crossflow) are independent choices — a tower is specified as a combination of both, not one or the other.
03
Open circuit towers cool the process water directly by contact with air; closed circuit towers keep the process fluid in a sealed coil, trading some thermal performance for a clean, contaminant-free loop.
04
Approach and range are the two numbers that actually describe a tower's performance — range is how much the water cools, approach is how close it gets to the ambient wet-bulb temperature, and a smaller approach always costs more tower.

What a cooling tower actually does

A cooling tower rejects heat picked up by condenser or process water back to the atmosphere, mainly through evaporative cooling. Warm water is broken into droplets or a thin film over fill material while air is drawn or blown through it; a small fraction of the water evaporates, and because evaporation consumes a large amount of latent heat, the remaining water cools significantly in the process. This is why a tower can cool water to within a few degrees of the ambient wet-bulb temperature — a lower, more demanding target than the dry-bulb temperature a simple air-cooled radiator is limited to.

Types of cooling towers

Towers are classified along two independent axes — how air moves through them, and what drives that airflow — plus whether the process water is exposed to air at all.

Counterflow Tower
Air rises opposite to water fall
Air is drawn straight up through the fill while water falls straight down, meeting the coolest air at the point of coolest water. Slightly more thermally efficient, but generally taller and needs more fan power than crossflow.
Crossflow Tower
Air moves horizontally through fill
Water falls vertically through the fill while air moves horizontally across it. Lower static pressure drop and easier access for maintenance, at a small trade-off in thermal performance versus counterflow of the same footprint.
Natural Draft Tower
Buoyancy-driven airflow
A tall hyperbolic shell relies on the density difference between warm moist air inside and cooler air outside to draw airflow through, with no fans at all. Used at very large scale, mainly power plant cooling.
Induced Draft Tower
Fan pulls air at the top
A fan mounted at the discharge draws air up through the fill and expels it well clear of the tower, reducing the chance of recirculating warm, humid discharge air back into the intake. The most common mechanical-draft arrangement.
Forced Draft Tower
Fan pushes air at the bottom
A fan at the air inlet pushes air through the fill under positive pressure. Runs quieter and handles higher static resistance, but is more prone to recirculating its own warm discharge air back into the intake.
Open Circuit Tower
Process water contacts air directly
Process water is sprayed or filmed directly over fill in contact with the air stream, then collected in a basin and pumped back. Most thermally efficient and compact, but the water is exposed to airborne dirt and needs treatment.
Closed Circuit Tower
Process fluid stays in a sealed coil
Process fluid circulates through a closed coil bathed in a separately sprayed and evaporated cooling water circuit, so it never contacts outside air. Keeps the process loop clean, at the cost of a lower approach and more surface area.

Main parts of a cooling tower

  1. 1
    Fill (packing)Splash or film-type material that maximizes water surface area and contact time with the air stream.
  2. 2
    Fan & motorDrives mechanical airflow through the tower; absent entirely on natural draft designs.
  3. 3
    Water distribution systemSpray nozzles or a gravity distribution deck that spreads incoming hot water evenly over the fill.
  4. 4
    Drift eliminatorsBaffles that catch entrained water droplets from the air stream before it exits, limiting water loss and drift carryover.
  5. 5
    Cold water basinCollects cooled water at the base of the tower before it's pumped back to the process or condenser.
  6. 6
    LouversControl air entry at the intake and help contain water splash within the tower.
  7. 7
    Casing / shellStructural enclosure housing all internal components, in FRP, galvanized steel, or concrete depending on size.
  8. 8
    Makeup & blowdown connectionsReplace evaporated and bled-off water and control the buildup of dissolved solids in the recirculating water.

Draft type vs airflow path

CombinationTypical use
Induced draft, counterflowMost common mechanical-draft configuration for commercial/industrial HVAC
Induced draft, crossflowEasier fill/nozzle access, common on larger field-erected towers
Forced draft, counterflowCompact packaged units, tolerant of higher external ductwork resistance
Natural draft, counterflowVery large scale, mainly power generation cooling

Key performance terms

TermWhat it means
RangeTemperature drop of the water across the tower (inlet minus outlet)
ApproachDifference between the tower's outlet water temperature and the ambient wet-bulb temperature
Wet-bulb temperatureThe theoretical performance limit a tower's outlet water can approach but never reach
Drift lossWater lost as entrained droplets in the discharge air stream, minimized by drift eliminators
Cycles of concentrationHow many times dissolved solids concentrate in recirculating water before blowdown, relative to makeup water

Selection checklist

  • 1Required heat rejection duty, design range, and design approach
  • 2Local design wet-bulb temperature for the site
  • 3Open vs closed circuit, based on whether the process loop must stay clean
  • 4Available footprint, height restrictions, and plume/recirculation clearances
  • 5Noise limits — forced draft is generally quieter than induced draft
  • 6Water treatment program and makeup water quality
  • 7Materials of construction for the local water chemistry and climate

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓Fill scaling or fouling, restricting airflow and reducing surface area for heat transfer
  • ✓Nozzle clogging or wear causing uneven water distribution across the fill
  • ✓Drift eliminator damage, allowing excessive water loss and increasing Legionella risk in the surrounding area
  • ✓Fan/motor vibration or bearing wear from continuous outdoor duty and moisture exposure
  • ✓Recirculation of warm discharge air back into the intake, especially on forced draft or tightly spaced towers
  • ✓Basin sediment buildup and corrosion, tracked through routine water treatment and cleaning
  • ✓Inadequate blowdown, letting cycles of concentration run too high and accelerating scale formation
A tower is always sized against the local design wet-bulb temperature, not the dry-bulb air temperature — on a hot, dry day a tower can outperform its rating, while on a hot, humid day it may struggle to hit its design approach even though the air temperature looks the same.

Common applications

Chilled water plant condenser water loops Power plant steam condenser cooling Industrial process heat rejection Refrigeration plant condensers Data center cooling Petrochemical & refinery cooling water systems

Key takeaways

  • ✓A cooling tower rejects heat mainly through evaporation, which lets it cool water close to the ambient wet-bulb, not the dry-bulb, temperature.
  • ✓Draft mechanism and airflow path are independent choices — induced-draft counterflow is the most common combination, but not the only valid one.
  • ✓Open circuit towers are more efficient; closed circuit towers keep the process fluid clean by never exposing it to air.
  • ✓Range and approach, not just "how cold the water gets," are the two numbers that actually describe tower performance.
