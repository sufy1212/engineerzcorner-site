Steam Traps Explained: Types, Failure Modes and What a Leaking Trap Costs

A steam trap lets condensate out and keeps steam in. Here are the three trap families as diagrams, how each fails, what a stuck-open trap really costs and how often to test.

On every steam line there is a quiet device doing three jobs at once, and when it fails nobody sees it. A steam trap sits at the low point of a steam system, lets condensate out and keeps live steam in. A small leaking trap can waste more fuel than most people expect, and a plant can have hundreds of them.

Three jobs, one small device

💧
1. Let condensate outSteam gives up its heat and turns to water. That water must leave, or it blocks the heat transfer surface and causes water hammer.
🔒
2. Keep steam inThe heat in live steam is the whole point. A trap that lets steam through simply wastes fuel.
💨
3. Vent air and CO₂Air and carbon dioxide insulate surfaces and speed corrosion, so the trap has to pass them at start-up and in service.

The three families of trap

Steam traps are grouped by how they tell steam from condensate. Mechanical traps sense density, thermostatic traps sense temperature, and thermodynamic traps sense the speed of the flow. Within each family are the common designs below.

Inverted bucket

Mechanical · senses density
  • Condensate lets the bucket sink and the valve open. Steam fills the bucket, it floats and the valve shuts. A tiny vent hole lets air through.
  • Tolerates water hammer and pressure swings well.
can fail openor closed

Float and thermostatic (F&T)

Mechanical + thermostatic · density and temperature
  • The float rises with condensate and opens the valve, so it discharges continuously. A thermostatic vent at the top removes air.
  • Commonly used on lower-pressure steam systems, such as process heat exchangers and air heaters.
typically fails closed

Thermodynamic (disc)

Kinetic · senses velocity
  • A single moving disc. Fast steam or flash steam creates a low-pressure zone and presses the disc onto its seat. Condensate lifts it again.
  • Compact and robust, and it works at high pressures, but the disc wears and then leaks.
typically fails open

Thermostatic traps (balanced pressure, bimetallic and liquid expansion) open when the temperature drops below the steam temperature, so they hold back the live steam by sensing heat. Bimetallic traps typically fail open.

Why failures matter

Traps fail either open, letting live steam blow through, or closed, holding back condensate. A trap stuck open wastes energy. A trap stuck closed floods the equipment, which cuts heat output and can cause water hammer. Float and thermostatic traps generally fail closed, thermodynamic disc and bimetallic traps generally fail open, and inverted bucket traps can do either.

Putting a price on a leak

The size of the loss follows the size of the opening, so the cost grows fast.

Worked example. A 1/8 inch leak at 100 psig loses 52.8 lb/h, which is about 24 kg/h. Running all year at 10 per 1,000 lb, that is 52.8 × 8,760 × 10 ÷ 1,000 = about 4,625 a year from a single trap. Now take a plant with 500 traps where 15% are leaking and each loses 13.2 lb/h, the loss of a 1/16 inch orifice at 100 psig. Seventy-five traps lose 990 lb/h, which is about 86,700 a year. Bringing the leaking share down to 5% (25 traps) leaves about 28,900 a year, a saving of about 57,800. Real losses depend on the actual orifice, the pressure and the steam price.

Keeping traps healthy

How often to test

Recommended testing intervals by steam pressure (U.S. DOE)

High150 psig and above
Weekly to monthly
Medium30 to 150 psig
Monthly to quarterly
Lowbelow 30 psig
Annually

There are four basic ways to test a trap: temperature, sound, visual inspection and electronic. A “lazy, billowy” plume at the discharge suggests flash steam from a healthy trap, while a sharp, fast plume of live steam suggests failure.

  • Survey and tag. Number every trap, record its type, size and location, and log each test result.
  • Test the way that suits the trap. Temperature, ultrasonic sound and visual checks each have strengths, and electronic monitors can watch critical traps continuously.
  • Match the trap to the job. Size it for the condensate load at the real differential pressure, with the manufacturer’s safety factor, and pick a type that suits the duty, such as a float trap for a heat exchanger that needs continuous drainage.
  • Fix the system as well as the trap. Drain low points, install strainers ahead of traps and keep condensate lines free to drain.

Steam losses also show up in insulation. See pipe insulation thickness and heat loss for the heat lost from the pipes themselves, pipe thermal expansion for what hot steam lines do as they heat up, and relief valve sizing for the protection of the steam system. Corrosion in condensate lines is covered in corrosion basics.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Think of every trap as a valve that must pass condensate and air but stop steam, and test it on a schedule.
  • ✓Know the typical failure mode of each type, since open failures waste energy and closed failures flood equipment.
  • ✓Cost a leak from the orifice size, steam pressure and steam price, because the loss follows orifice area.
  • ✓Match trap type and size to the duty, and fix drainage and strainers as well as the trap.
