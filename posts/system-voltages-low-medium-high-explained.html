System Voltages Explained: Low, Medium & High Voltage Levels and Phases

Every drawing, nameplate, and spec sheet leans on a voltage class — LV, MV, HV — that isn't defined the same way twice. Here's how the bands actually break down, the standard values that recur worldwide, and how single-phase and three-phase fit into all of it.

Why "low, medium, high voltage" isn't one fixed line

Ask an IEC-trained engineer where medium voltage ends and high voltage begins, and you'll get a different number than from someone working off IEEE/ANSI documents or a specific national grid code. The underlying idea is the same everywhere — group system voltages into bands so that insulation levels, clearances, switchgear design, and safety procedures can be standardized within each band — but the exact cut-off numbers shift depending on which document, country, or utility is being referenced. Treat the ranges below as the commonly used bands, not a single universal law.

The voltage classes, ELV to UHV

Typical ranges seen across IEC and IEEE/ANSI practice, with standard values that show up repeatedly on real projects
ELV — Extra Low Voltage

≤ 50V AC / ≤ 120V DC. Not usually classed as a power voltage at all — it's the band used for control and signal circuits.

12V / 24V DC 48V DC < 50V AC
Typical use
  • PLC / control I/O
  • Instrumentation loops
  • Fire & security systems
LV — Low Voltage

Roughly 50V–1000V AC. Covers almost every utilization voltage a building, home, or plant actually runs equipment on.

230/400V 120/208V 277/480V
Typical use
  • Lighting & socket outlets
  • Small & medium motors
  • Distribution boards
MV — Medium Voltage

From 1kV up to roughly 35kV, though some utilities and IEEE references push the upper limit as far as 69kV.

3.3 / 6.6kV 11 / 13.8kV 22 / 33kV
Typical use
  • Primary distribution
  • Large motor feeders
  • Substation infeed
HV — High Voltage

From the top of MV up to roughly 230kV — the workhorse range for sub-transmission and regional transmission networks.

66kV 132kV 220 / 230kV
Typical use
  • Sub-transmission
  • Grid interconnection
  • Large substations
EHV — Extra High Voltage

Roughly 230kV–800kV, used for bulk power transmission over long distances to keep line losses manageable.

400kV 500kV 765kV
Typical use
  • Bulk long-distance transmission
  • National grid backbones
UHV — Ultra High Voltage

Above 800kV. A small group of countries with very large grids run at this level to move huge blocks of power efficiently.

800kV 1100kV
Typical use
  • Cross-country bulk transmission
  • China, India-scale grids

IEC vs IEEE/ANSI: where the lines are drawn

The concept of ELV, LV, MV, and HV is shared across standards bodies, but the exact boundary voltages differ — and national codes often diverge from both. A commonly cited example: the US NEC treated 600V as the ceiling for "low voltage" for decades before more recent code cycles raised that figure to 1000V, bringing US practice closer to the IEC line.

Phases available: single-phase vs three-phase

Whatever the voltage class, the electricity itself only arrives in one of a few phase configurations
Single-phase (2-wire)

One live conductor plus neutral, carrying a single sinusoidal voltage. The default for small residential and light commercial loads.

Domestic lighting Small appliances Single-phase motors
Key point
  • Supplies one usable voltage only
Split-phase (3-wire)

Two 120V legs, 180° apart, from a center-tapped LV transformer — the standard residential service in North America.

120V leg-to-neutral 240V leg-to-leg
Key point
  • 120V for lighting/outlets, 240V for dryers, ranges & AC from one service
Three-phase delta (3-wire)

Three lines with no neutral conductor (aside from high-leg or corner-grounded variants). Common through MV networks and industrial power.

No neutral Balanced line-to-line
Key point
  • Feeds three-phase loads such as motors and MV networks
Three-phase wye / star (4-wire)

Three lines plus a neutral from a star-connected source, giving two usable voltages from the same service.

230/400V 120/208V 277/480V
Key point
  • The most common LV utility arrangement — feeds single-phase and three-phase loads together

What this means when you're reading a drawing or spec

  • 1Confirm which standard the "LV/MV/HV" label on a drawing is referencing — IEC, IEEE/ANSI, or a specific utility grid code — before assuming a boundary.
  • 2Check whether a quoted voltage is line-to-line or line-to-neutral before it goes into a load or cable calculation.
  • 3Match cable, switchgear, and transformer insulation ratings to the actual system voltage class, not just the nameplate utilization voltage.
  • 4Verify delta vs wye connection on three-phase equipment before sizing protection devices and neutral conductors.
  • 5Cross-check local standard voltages before specifying imported equipment for an unfamiliar country or utility.
  • 6Remember that split-phase, delta, and wye services deliver different usable voltage pairs from the same nominal system — don't assume every "three-phase" service exposes the same numbers.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Voltage classes — ELV, LV, MV, HV, EHV, UHV — are defined differently by IEC, IEEE/ANSI, and individual utilities, so the boundary numbers shift depending on the source document.
  • ✓LV runs up to roughly 1kV, MV picks up from there to about 35kV (sometimes 69kV), HV continues to about 230kV, and EHV/UHV cover bulk transmission above that.
  • ✓Only two phase families exist in practice: single-phase (including split-phase) for light loads, and three-phase (delta or wye) for balanced power distribution and motors.
  • ✓Standard LV utilization voltages cluster around 230/400V and 120/208–277/480V worldwide; standard MV distribution voltages cluster around 11kV, 22kV, and 33kV.
