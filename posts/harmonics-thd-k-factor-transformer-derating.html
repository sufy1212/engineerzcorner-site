Current THD and K-Factor: Why Harmonic Distortion Derates Your Transformer

A transformer rated fine for its load in kVA can still overheat, because harmonic current causes losses a standard nameplate rating never accounted for.

What THD actually measures

THDI  =  √(∑ Ih²)  /  I1  ×  100%
Ih = RMS magnitude of each harmonic (h = 2, 3, 5, 7…) · I1 = RMS magnitude of the fundamental (50/60 Hz) current

THD rolls every harmonic's contribution into one number: the total RMS of all the harmonic currents, expressed as a percentage of the fundamental. Nonlinear loads — VFDs, UPS input rectifiers, switch-mode power supplies — are the usual source, because they draw current in pulses rather than a smooth sinusoid, and that pulsed waveform decomposes into a fundamental plus a series of odd harmonics.

K-factor: why not all harmonics cost the same

K  =  ∑ (Ih/I1)² × h²  ÷  ∑ (Ih/I1)²
Weights each harmonic's contribution by the square of its harmonic order h

THD treats every harmonic equally per unit of current. Real transformer losses don't: eddy-current losses in the windings and core rise with the square of frequency, so a 7th harmonic does roughly 49 times the eddy-current heating of the same-magnitude fundamental current. K-factor captures that by weighting each harmonic's contribution by h² before combining them — two loads with identical THD can have very different K-factors depending on which harmonics dominate, and the one with higher-order harmonics runs a standard transformer hotter for the same THD reading.

Why this matters for transformer selection

A standard dry-type or oil-filled transformer's kVA rating assumes a clean sinusoidal load current. Feed it a load with meaningful harmonic content and the extra eddy-current heating from K-factor effects can push winding temperature past its insulation class rating, even while total apparent power stays within the nameplate kVA. The practical response is either derating a standard transformer below its nameplate kVA for that load, or specifying a K-rated transformer designed with the extra eddy-current losses built into its thermal design from the start.

Why the IEEE 519 limit isn't one number

It's common to check THD against a flat reference limit — 5% is a widely used rule of thumb — but the actual IEEE 519 standard doesn't set a single fixed percentage. Its limits vary with the ratio of available short-circuit current to maximum load current (Isc/IL) at the point of common coupling: a stronger supply (higher Isc/IL) tolerates more distortion than a weaker one before it becomes a system-wide power-quality problem. A generic 5% check is a reasonable first screen, but the real limit for a specific installation depends on how strong the supply actually is at that connection point.

Key takeaways

  • ✓THD combines every harmonic's RMS magnitude into one distortion figure relative to the fundamental.
  • ✓K-factor weights harmonics by the square of their order, because transformer eddy-current losses scale with frequency squared.
  • ✓Two loads with the same THD can need very different transformer derating if their harmonic spectra differ.
  • ✓IEEE 519's real limit scales with supply strength (Isc/IL) at the connection point — a flat 5% is a screening rule, not the actual standard.
