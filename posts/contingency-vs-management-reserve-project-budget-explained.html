Contingency vs Management Reserve

Both are money held back for things going wrong, but they cover different kinds of wrong and sit in different places in the budget. Mixing them up either hides risk inside the baseline or lets the project quietly spend its safety net.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Project Management
01
Contingency covers identified risks, the known-unknowns from the risk register, and is included in the cost baseline. The project manager can usually spend it.
02
Management reserve covers unforeseen events, the unknown-unknowns. It sits outside the cost baseline, is part of the project budget, and needs management approval to release.
03
A contingency number should come from the risks, not a flat percentage: expected value from the register gives a defensible starting point.
04
An estimate's accuracy range depends on how far the design is defined. A Class 5 concept estimate can swing from −50% to +100%.

When management reserve is released, the baseline changes through change control. Otherwise the budget ends up showing overruns that look like estimating errors.

From a risk register to a contingency figure

The simplest risk-based method is expected monetary value: probability times cost impact for each risk, summed. Here is a register for a project with a base estimate of 2,000,000.

Expected monetary value per risk

Supplier delivery delay30% × 120,000 = 36,000
Rework from design changes50% × 40,000 = 20,000
Unforeseen ground conditions20% × 250,000 = 50,000
Equipment failure at commissioning10% × 400,000 = 40,000
Permit delay60% × 25,000 = 15,000
Sum = contingency161,000 (8.05% of base)

Feeding this into the stack: cost baseline = 2,000,000 + 161,000 = 2,161,000. With a management reserve of 5% of the baseline (108,050), the total project budget is 2,269,050, which is 269,050 or 13.45% over the base estimate. That is exactly what your cost contingency calculator does when you enter 8.05% contingency and 5% reserve.

What the expected value does not tell you

Expected value is an average, and some risks are all-or-nothing. The equipment failure above is a 10% chance of 400,000, so its 40,000 contribution is never the amount actually spent: it is either nothing or the whole loss. Treating the five risks as independent and each at its stated impact, the chances work out like this:

QuestionAnswer
Chance that none of the five risks occurs10%
Chance that total impact is within the 161,000 contingency66%
Contingency for a 50% chance of being enough (P50)65,000
Contingency for an 80% chance of being enough (P80)290,000
All five occurring together835,000

So the expected-value contingency of 161,000 has about a 66% chance of covering this register (the average sits above P50 because a few large risks skew the distribution), and covering it with 80% confidence takes 290,000. Many owners set funding at a higher confidence than the estimate itself, so the gap between P50 and P80 is a real policy question and not a rounding detail. These numbers assume independent risks, fixed impacts and a complete register; a real analysis uses ranges and correlation, usually through simulation.

Estimate class sets the uncertainty

AACE recommended practice 18R-97 classifies capital project estimates by how much of the project is defined. The expected accuracy range is the typical variation low and high after contingency has been applied (at a 50% level of confidence), so it describes the estimate's uncertainty and is not itself the contingency.

ClassProject definitionTypical purposeLowHigh
50–2%Concept screening−20% to −50%+30% to +100%

310–40%Budget authorization−10% to −20%+10% to +30%
230–70%Control or bid−5% to −15%+5% to +20%
150–100%Check estimate−3% to −10%+3% to +15%

Reading it: a Class 3 estimate of 2,000,000 has an expected actual cost typically between 1,600,000 and 2,600,000 (that is, −20% to +30%, the outer limits of the typical range). The same project at Class 5 could land from 1,000,000 to 4,000,000. The lower the class number, the less contingency the remaining uncertainty needs, which is why contingency percentages should fall as design matures. The class definitions vary slightly between published summaries, so quote them from the document itself.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
A flat 10% on everythingIt ignores the actual risks and the estimate's maturity, so it is too much on some projects and not enough on others
Hiding contingency inside line itemsPadding each activity makes the risk invisible and impossible to release or re-score
Treating management reserve as extra contingencyThe project spends it on identified risks, and nothing is left for the unforeseen
Using expected value as the guaranteeIt is the average, not the amount that is enough for most outcomes
Never releasing unused contingencyContingency tied to risks that have passed should be reviewed and returned as the project proceeds

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cost baseline = base estimate + contingency. Project budget = baseline + management reserve.
  • ✓Contingency is for identified risks and inside the baseline; management reserve is for unknowns and needs approval.
  • ✓Size contingency from the register: probability × impact, summed, then check what confidence level that actually gives.
  • ✓Estimate class (AACE 18R-97) shows how wide the uncertainty is; Class 5 is −50% to +100%, Class 1 about −10% to +15%.
  • ✓Confirm definitions and ranges against your organization's policy and the standards you work to.
