S-Curves Explained: Why Project Progress Isn't a Straight Line

Plot cumulative progress against time on almost any project and you get an S, not a diagonal — slow start, fast middle, slow finish. Knowing why is what lets you read one correctly.

An S-curve plots cumulative progress — work, cost, or hours — against time, and it curves because work rate isn't constant.

Most projects start slow (mobilisation, design), accelerate through the middle (bulk of the work), then taper (finishing, commissioning, punch list).

Why the S-shape, not a straight line

A straight-line progress plan assumes work happens at a constant rate from day one to the last day — which almost never matches reality. Most projects open with mobilisation, procurement lead times, and design work that consumes calendar time without producing much measurable physical progress. The middle of the project is where the bulk of the work packages run in parallel and progress accelerates. The tail end slows again as remaining work packages finish, testing and commissioning take over, and punch-list items get closed one at a time. Plotted cumulatively, that slow–fast–slow pattern draws an S, not a diagonal.

What's actually being plotted

Curve typeY-axis measuresCommon use
Progress S-curve% physical work completeTracking schedule performance against the plan
Cost S-curveCumulative cost spent (often as Planned Value)Cash flow forecasting, ties directly into EVM
Man-hour S-curveCumulative labour hoursResource loading and workforce planning

All three are built the same way: take the planned duration and cost of every work package in the WBS, distribute it across the calendar, and sum cumulatively period by period. The curve is a rollup of the schedule, not a separate estimate drawn on its own.

Reading planned vs actual

The gap tells you there's a problem, not what the problem is Once the actual progress curve is plotted against the planned one, three readings are possible: actual sits on the plan (on schedule), above it (ahead), or below it (behind). What the chart can't tell you on its own is why — whether a single critical-path work package is the cause, or delays are spread thinly across many non-critical packages that individually don't matter. Reading the S-curve is the first flag; finding the cause means going back to the schedule and the WBS work packages behind the number.

Worked example

A six-month installation project, progress measured at each month-end:

Planned vs actual, cumulative %

Month 2Planned 15% · Actual 12% · 3 pts behind
Month 4Planned 55% · Actual 46% · 9 pts behind
Month 6Planned 100% · Actual 88% · 12 pts behind

The gap isn't just growing — it's growing faster through the middle of the project, which is exactly where the planned curve is steepest. A fixed percentage-point gap matters more in the steep section of the curve than the same gap near the flat tail, because it represents more absolute work not done in the same amount of calendar time.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The S-shape comes from work rate genuinely varying — slow mobilisation and design, a fast middle, a slow finishing tail — not from an arbitrary curve-fit.
  • ✓Progress, cost, and man-hour S-curves all roll up from the same underlying WBS and schedule — they're views of the same plan, not separate estimates.
  • ✓A gap between planned and actual flags that something's off; it doesn't identify which work package or which cause on its own.
  • ✓The same point-gap matters more where the curve is steep than where it's flat — read the gap against the slope, not just its size.
