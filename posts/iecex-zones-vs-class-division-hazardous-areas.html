IECEx / ATEX Zones vs NEC Class & Division: Hazardous Area Classification

Two different rulebooks describe the same problem — where explosive gas, vapor or dust might be present, and how equipment has to be built to sit safely inside that space. Mix up which system a site drawing is using and a "correctly rated" panel can be the wrong panel entirely.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Hazardous Area Classification
01
The Zone system (IEC 60079 / ATEX) grades a hazardous area by how often and how long an explosive atmosphere is present — Zone 0/20 is the worst case, Zone 2/22 the mildest.
02
The NEC Class & Division system (traditionally used in North America) instead asks whether the hazard exists under normal operation (Division 1) or only under abnormal/fault conditions (Division 2).
03
They aren't just different names for the same three grades — Division 1 roughly spans what Zones 0 and 1 cover separately, and Division 2 lines up with Zone 2.
04
The NEC has also adopted the Zone system as an alternative (Articles 505/506), so both schemes can legally appear on the same North American site.

The core distinction

Both systems exist to answer the same question for a hazardous-area engineer: given that flammable gas, vapor, or combustible dust could be present here, how likely is that, and how does equipment need to be built to not become the ignition source? The Zone system answers with a probability-graded scale — continuous presence, occasional presence, or rare and brief presence. The Class & Division system answers with a binary split — present in normal operation, or only present if something breaks. They're solving the same design problem from two different angles, which is exactly why converting between them isn't a clean one-to-one swap.

The two systems, side by side

IEC 60079 / ATEX · used across most of the world
Zone System Six zones total — three for gas/vapor (0, 1, 2) and three for combustible dust (20, 21, 22) — graded by frequency and duration of the explosive atmosphere.
NFPA 70 (NEC) Articles 500–506 · North America
Class & Division System Class describes the type of hazard (I gas, II dust, III fibers); Division describes whether it's present in normal operation (1) or only under fault conditions (2).

How the zones are defined

Zone extent around a release source (flange, vent, seal)

The gas/vapor zones and the dust zones use the identical logic, just applied to a cloud of dust instead of a gas mixture — which is why they're numbered as a matching pair (0↔20, 1↔21, 2↔22) rather than as six unrelated categories.

Zone (Gas)Zone (Dust)Presence of explosive atmosphere
Zone 0Zone 20Present continuously, or for long periods, or frequently
Zone 1Zone 21Likely to occur in normal operation occasionally
Zone 2Zone 22Not likely in normal operation, and if it does, only briefly

Class & Division breakdown

Class — what kind of hazard
Class I — flammable gases or vapors Class II — combustible dust Class III — ignitable fibers & flyings
Division — when the hazard is present
  • 1Division 1 — the ignitable concentration exists under normal operating conditions, or frequently due to repair/maintenance, or equipment failure could release it and simultaneously cause the ignition source to fail.
  • 2Division 2 — handled in closed systems and only becomes hazardous through accidental rupture, breakdown, or abnormal equipment failure; normal operation keeps it fully contained.

Zone ↔ Division: the common equivalency

This is the widely used approximate mapping — useful for reading a converted drawing, not a substitute for a proper area classification study.

IEC/ATEX ZoneRoughly equivalent to
Zone 0Division 1 (the most severe portion of it)
Zone 1Division 1
Zone 2Division 2
Division 1 isn't "equal to" either Zone 0 or Zone 1 — it's a single, coarser bucket that covers the ground both of those zones split into two finer grades. That's the main reason a straight zone-to-division relabeling can under- or over-classify an area.

Gas & dust groups compared

Beyond the zone/division grade, equipment is also rated for how easily the specific gas or dust ignites. Both systems subdivide the hazard further — they just use different letters.

IEC Group (gas)NEC Group (gas)Example
IICA / BAcetylene, hydrogen
IIBCEthylene
IIADPropane, most hydrocarbons
IEC Group (dust)NEC Group (dust)Example
IIICEConductive metal dust
IIIBFNon-conductive carbonaceous dust (coal)

Temperature classes

A hot surface can ignite a gas on its own, with no spark involved — so equipment also carries a T-class capping its maximum surface temperature below the gas's autoignition temperature. Both systems use the same T1–T6 scale today.

Protection concepts (the "Ex" methods)

Whichever classification scheme is in use, the equipment inside the area is built using one of a small set of internationally recognized protection techniques — the letter after "Ex" on the nameplate tells you which one.

Ex dFlameproof enclosureContains an internal explosion and cools the escaping gases below ignition temperature before they reach the surrounding atmosphere.
Ex eIncreased safetyNo arcing or sparking parts by design, with extra safety margins on temperature rise — common for terminal boxes and motor windings.
Ex ia / ibIntrinsic safetyEnergy in the circuit is limited so it can never release enough to ignite the atmosphere, even with one (ib) or two (ia) simultaneous faults.
Ex pPressurizationThe enclosure is purged and kept pressurized with clean air or inert gas, physically keeping the hazardous atmosphere out.
Ex nNon-sparkingBuilt so it produces no arcs, sparks or hot surfaces under normal operation — permitted for Zone 2 / Division 2 only, not the more severe zones.
Ex mEncapsulationPotentially sparking parts are fully embedded in a solid compound, sealing them off from any surrounding explosive atmosphere.

Reading an equipment marking

A typical certified nameplate reads something like Ex d IIB T4 Gb. Each block carries its own piece of the classification:

Ex dProtection method
IIBGas group
T4Temperature class
GbEquipment Protection Level

The Equipment Protection Level (EPL) — Ga/Gb/Gc for gas, Da/Db/Dc for dust — states which zone the device is actually rated to sit in: "a" for Zone 0/20, "b" for Zone 1/21, "c" for Zone 2/22. It's this EPL, not the protection method alone, that ultimately confirms whether a given piece of equipment is allowed in a given zone.

Easy way to remember

Zone SystemGrades the odds — how often and how long the hazard is actually there.
Class & DivisionAsks a yes/no — is it there normally, or only if something fails?

Key takeaways

  • ✓Zone (IEC 60079/ATEX) grades an area by frequency/duration of the explosive atmosphere: Zone 0/20 continuous, Zone 1/21 occasional, Zone 2/22 rare and brief.
  • ✓Class & Division (NEC) instead asks whether the hazard is present in normal operation (Division 1) or only under abnormal/fault conditions (Division 2).
  • ✓The common equivalency is Zone 0 & Zone 1 → Division 1, and Zone 2 → Division 2 — but it's an approximation, not a legal substitute for a site-specific area classification.
  • ✓Gas/dust groups (IIC/IIB/IIA vs A/B/C/D, IIIC/IIIB/IIIA vs E/F/G) and T-class ratings apply on top of the zone or division, and must also match the actual hazard present.
  • ✓The protection method (Ex d, Ex e, Ex ia, Ex p, Ex n, Ex m...) tells you how the equipment is built to be safe; the EPL (Ga/Gb/Gc, Da/Db/Dc) tells you which zone it's actually certified for.
