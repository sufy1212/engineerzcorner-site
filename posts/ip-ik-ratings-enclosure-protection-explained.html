IP and IK Ratings Explained: Reading the Codes on Enclosures

Two digits on a nameplate tell you how well an enclosure keeps out solids and water, and a separate IK code covers impacts. Here they are as pictures, with a decoder to try.

Every enclosure, luminaire, junction box and motor has a small code on its plate that says how well it keeps the world out. It is one of the most copied and most misread numbers in electrical work. This post turns the IP and IK codes into pictures, with a decoder you can try.

What the two digits mean

Read it like a code

IP means Ingress Protection. Two digits, and each one answers a different question.

IPingress
protection
6solids
0 to 6
5water
0 to 9K
IP65 = completely dust-tight (6) and protected against low-pressure water jets from any direction (5). An X in a position means that kind of protection was not tested or rated, as in IPX5.

Solids: the first digit

Digits 1 to 4 describe the smallest object that cannot get in, which also covers what a person can touch. Digits 5 and 6 are about dust. Test probes are fixed sizes, so the rating is a pass or fail against that probe.

First digit: how small an object is kept out?

Test probes drawn to relative size. Digits 5 and 6 are about dust, not objects.

Digit 5 is dust protected: a little dust may get in, but not enough to interfere with operation. Digit 6 is dust tight: no dust gets in.

Water: the second digit

The water digits climb from vertical drips to immersion and hot jets. The test conditions are precise, which is why two products with the same digit behave the same way.

Second digit: how much water can it take?

Each step up is a harsher test. Test details from IEC 60529.

Immersion ratings (7, 8) and jet ratings (5, 6) test different things, so one does not imply the other.

Impact: the IK code

IP says nothing about knocks. The IK code covers mechanical impact and follows a separate standard. The energy is simple physics, E = m × g × h. A 5 kg hammer dropped 0.4 m delivers 5 × 9.81 × 0.4 = 19.6 J, which is the 20 J of IK10. A 1.7 kg mass dropped 0.3 m gives 5.0 J, which is IK08.

The IK scale: how hard a knock?

Impact energy in joules, on a logarithmic scale. IEC 62262 defines IK00 (no protection) to IK10.

These are common choices, not rules. The right rating comes from the environment, the specification and the local code.

Mistakes to avoid

!
Ratings are not cumulative. An IPX6 product has not automatically passed the IPX5 or IPX7 tests. Where both apply, makers state both, for example IPX6/IPX7.
!
The rating only holds if it is installed correctly. An open door, a wrong cable gland or a missing blanking plug cancels the enclosure rating. Choose glands to match, as in cable gland selection.
ⓘ
IP is not NEMA. NEMA type numbers cover more than IP does, such as corrosion and icing, and the two systems do not convert exactly.
ⓘ
Sealed means hotter. A higher IP rating leaves less room for air to cool the contents, so check the heat load and the allowed temperature rise.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Read the first digit for solids and dust and the second for water, and remember an X means not rated.
  • ✓Do not assume a higher water digit covers the lower ones, since jets and immersion are different tests.
  • ✓Match glands, doors and installation to the rating, because one open gap cancels it.
  • ✓Check IK separately for impact and consider heat build-up in sealed enclosures.
