Star (Y) and Delta (Δ) Connections Explained

Two ways to wire three windings together, and they behave completely differently — different voltage, different current, different starting torque. Here's what actually changes between them, and why a starter switches from one to the other.

Two ways to connect three windings

Any three-phase winding set — in a motor, a transformer, or a generator — has six ends that need to go somewhere: two per winding, three windings. There are exactly two standard ways to join them. In a Star (Y) connection, one end of each winding is tied together at a common point, forming a neutral; the other three ends go out to the three phases. In a Delta (Δ) connection, the windings are joined end-to-end in a closed loop, and the three junctions between them go out to the three phases — there's no neutral point at all.

Everything else that differs between Star and Delta — voltage relationships, current relationships, starting behavior — follows directly from that one structural difference in how the windings are joined.

Star (Y) Connection

One end of every winding meets at a common neutral point (N); the free ends go to R, Y, B. A neutral wire can be taken from the common point if the load needs it.

Delta (Δ) Connection

The three windings are joined end-to-end, closing into a triangle. The three junctions connect to R, Y, B directly — there's no neutral point to take a wire from.

The line/phase relationship that drives everything

"Phase" values describe what a single winding actually sees; "line" values describe what's measured between two incoming supply terminals. In Star, a winding only sees part of the line voltage, because two windings are effectively in series between any two lines — that's what produces the √3 factor. In Delta, each winding is connected directly across a line-to-line voltage, so phase and line voltage are simply equal. Current works the opposite way: in Star, the same current that flows in a winding flows straight out to the line; in Delta, line current is the sum of two winding currents combining out of phase, which is where Delta's √3 factor on current comes from.

Terminal connections in practice

A standard three-phase motor brings out six terminals — U1, V1, W1 and U2, V2, W2 — specifically so the connection can be made either way at the terminal box, without rewiring the motor internally. For Star, W2, U2 and V2 are bridged together to form the neutral point, and U1, V1, W1 take the three supply lines. For Delta, each terminal is bridged to the next in sequence (W2–U1, U2–V1, V2–W1), closing the loop, and the supply connects across those same junctions. It's the same motor either way — only the jumper arrangement on the terminal block changes.

Why a Star-Delta starter exists

A motor's windings are rated for a specific voltage per winding. A dual-voltage motor (say 400V line) is built so its windings are individually rated for 400V — meaning it can run in Delta directly across a 400V line, or it can start in Star, where each winding only sees 400V ÷ √3 ≈ 230V. Starting in Star cuts the starting current to roughly a third of what Delta would draw directly across the line, at the cost of roughly a third of the torque. A Star-Delta starter uses that tradeoff deliberately: start in Star to limit inrush, then switch to Delta once the motor's up near its rated speed and doesn't need the extra torque to keep accelerating.

Start — Star Low current, low torque. Used only for acceleration, never as the running connection for a motor rated to run in Delta.
Run — Delta Full line voltage per winding, full rated torque and current. The motor switches here automatically once it's reached roughly 80–90% of rated speed.

What switching to Delta actually buys

Reduces inrush current on the supply during starting
Minimizes the voltage dip that inrush current would otherwise cause on the supply system
Reduces mechanical and thermal shock during the transition to full running torque
Lets the same motor both start gently and run at full rated capability, without oversizing anything

Key takeaways

  • ✓Star and Delta are the only two ways to join three windings — a common neutral point, or a closed loop with no neutral.
  • ✓The √3 factor between line and phase values flips depending on connection: it applies to voltage in Star, to current in Delta.
  • ✓Same motor, same windings — only the terminal-box jumpers change between a Star and a Delta connection.
  • ✓Star trades torque for lower starting current; Delta gives full torque and current straight away.
  • ✓A Star-Delta starter isn't a fixed choice — it starts in Star and switches to Delta once the motor is near rated speed.
