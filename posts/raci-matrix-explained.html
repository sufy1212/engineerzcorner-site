RACI Matrix Explained: Who's Responsible vs. Who's Accountable

Most project delays aren't a skills problem — they're an ownership problem. A RACI matrix exists to make sure every decision and deliverable has exactly one owner.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Project Management
01
RACI assigns one of four roles — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — to every person, for every task.
02
The most common failure is having zero or two people marked Accountable for the same task.
03
Responsible and Accountable are not the same thing, even though they're often held by the same person.
04
A matrix is only useful if it's built before the confusion starts, not after a deadline is missed.

The four roles

RACI is a grid: tasks or deliverables down one side, people or roles across the top. Every cell gets one letter. The letters stand for four distinct relationships to the work, and mixing them up is where most matrices go wrong.

R — ResponsibleThe person who actually does the work. There can be more than one Responsible on a task.
A — AccountableThe person who owns the outcome and signs off on it. Answerable if it doesn't get done — and there must be exactly one.
C — ConsultedPeople whose input is sought before the work is finalized — two-way communication.
I — InformedPeople kept up to date on progress or the outcome — one-way communication, after the fact.

The rule that actually matters

Exactly one Accountable per task — no exceptions Responsible can be shared across several people doing the work together. Accountable cannot. The moment two people are both marked "A" on the same task, you've rebuilt the exact ambiguity RACI was supposed to remove — each will assume the other is covering it. Zero people marked "A" is just as bad: the task quietly becomes nobody's problem the first time it gets difficult.

Why R and A get confused

On small teams, the same person is often both Responsible and Accountable for a task, which makes the distinction feel unnecessary. It stops feeling unnecessary the moment a task is delegated. A site engineer might be Responsible for producing a cable schedule, while the lead engineer is Accountable for it being correct and issued on time — even though the lead engineer never opens the drawing themselves. If a client asks who's answerable for a late deliverable, "Accountable" is the name you give. "Responsible" is the name of whoever was doing the drafting.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat it causes
Two or more "A" on one taskEach accountable party assumes the other is driving it — the task stalls silently
No "A" on a taskNobody escalates when it slips, because escalation implies ownership nobody has
Everyone marked "C"Consultation becomes a formality; too many voices slow decisions without adding value
Marking people "I" who need to be "C"Stakeholders find out about a decision after it's made, when their input would have changed it
Building the matrix after the project startsRoles get assigned to match who's already doing the work, not who should be — existing gaps get baked in

Worked example

A control panel submittal needs review before fabrication. Four people touch it: the design engineer, the lead engineer, the client's representative, and the site supervisor.

Assignment

Design engineerR — drafts the submittal
Lead engineerA — owns sign-off before it's issued
Client representativeC — consulted on spec compliance before issue
Site supervisorI — informed once it's approved, for scheduling

Four people, four different relationships to the same deliverable, and only one of them — the lead engineer — is on the hook if it's wrong or late. That's the entire point of the exercise.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Assign exactly one Accountable per task, always — that single rule prevents most RACI failures.
  • ✓Responsible does the work; Accountable owns whether it's right and on time — they can be the same person or different people.
  • ✓Consulted means their input shapes the outcome; Informed means they hear about it afterward — don't mix the two up.
  • ✓Build the matrix at project kickoff, not after the first missed deadline exposes the gap.
