Insulation Resistance Testing Explained

A megger reading is one number, and whether it is a pass depends on the test voltage, the circuit, the winding temperature and how the reading changes over time. This is how to choose the voltage, read the result and avoid the usual false passes.

Engineerz CorneRReference · IEC 60364-6 / BS 7671 / IEEE 43
01
For LV installations the standard sets the test voltage by circuit voltage: 250 V DC for SELV/PELV, 500 V DC up to 500 V, 1000 V DC above 500 V.
02
Minimum values are 0.5 MΩ for SELV/PELV and 1 MΩ for the other two bands. A new, healthy installation reads far above that.
03
For motors and generators a single reading is not enough. The polarization index (PI = R10min / R1min) shows whether the winding is dry and clean, and IR must be corrected to 40 °C.
04
Most bad results are test setup errors: live circuits, wrong voltage, electronics and SPDs left connected, or readings compared across different temperatures.

What the test measures

An insulation resistance test applies a known DC voltage between conductors, or between conductors and earth, and measures the small current that flows. Ohm's law gives the resistance. The circuit must be dead and isolated, and DC is used because it measures leakage through and over the insulation without the capacitive current an AC test would add. It is different from the protective conductor continuity test and from RCD testing: it says something about the quality of the insulation itself.

Current through insulation is the sum of a charging current that dies out in seconds, an absorption current that decays over minutes, and a steady conduction (leakage) current. That is why the reading keeps rising during a test on a clean, dry winding, and why the time of the reading matters.

LV installations: voltage and minimum values

IEC 60364-6, and BS 7671 in its Table 64, set the test voltage and the minimum acceptable value by nominal circuit voltage.

Nominal circuit voltageTest voltage (DC)Minimum insulation resistance
SELV and PELV250 V0.5 MΩ
Up to and including 500 V (including 230/400 V)500 V1 MΩ
Above 500 V1000 V1 MΩ

Two details catch people out. First, the lower 0.5 MΩ limit belongs to SELV/PELV circuits; if a 230 V circuit is tested at a reduced 250 V (for example because surge protective devices cannot be removed), the result must still reach 1 MΩ. Second, these are pass/fail minimums, not typical values. New installations normally read tens or hundreds of megohms, so a reading that scrapes over 1 MΩ is a warning to look for moisture, damage or a nail through a cable. Example: a 230 V circuit reading 0.7 MΩ at 500 V fails.

Pairs to test: live conductors (L and N tied together) to earth, and, where the installation allows, between live conductors. Test the whole board first; if the board reads low, split it into circuits to find the one pulling the value down.

Set-up checklist before pressing TEST

  • ✓Isolate and prove dead. Insulation testing on a live circuit is dangerous and gives meaningless readings.
  • ✓Disconnect or bridge voltage-sensitive equipment: dimmers, electronic drives, control units and surge protective devices. A 500 V test can damage them or read the SPD instead of the cable.
  • ✓Select the voltage for the circuit, not the highest the tester offers. Testing a SELV circuit at 500 V can damage its insulation.
  • ✓Discharge afterward. The cable dielectric stores energy at 500 or 1000 V, so let the tester discharge or short the conductors to earth before touching them.

Motors and generators: spot reading, PI and temperature

For rotating machines IEEE 43 is the reference. It uses a spot insulation resistance reading at 1 minute and the polarization index, and it expects both.

PI = R10min / R1min
Both readings from one continuous test at the same voltage and temperature

IEEE 43 gives a recommended minimum PI of 2.0 for thermal class B, F and H insulation, which covers most motors in service, and 1.5 for older class A insulation. A PI close to 1 means the reading is flat: leakage current dominates, which points to moisture or contamination. Dielectric absorption ratio (DAR = R60s / R30s) is a shorter version of the same idea when a 10-minute test is impractical.

For the spot value, commonly quoted minimums at 40 °C are the rated voltage in kV plus 1 MΩ for older or field-wound windings, 100 MΩ for modern form-wound AC coils, and 5 MΩ for random-wound and low-voltage stators. The test voltage rises with the machine's rating. For windings rated below 1000 V IEEE 43 calls for 500 V DC; for higher ratings use the standard's table, which steps up to several kilovolts.

Correct to 40 °C

Insulation resistance falls as temperature rises, roughly halving for every 10 °C. Readings are corrected to 40 °C before comparing with the minimum:

R40 = Kt × R(T), with Kt = 0.5(40 − T)/10

Worked example, a 415 V random-wound motor tested at a winding temperature of 28 °C. R1min = 60 MΩ and R10min = 150 MΩ.

  • ✓Kt = 0.51.2 = 0.435, so R40 = 0.435 × 60 = 26 MΩ. That is above the 5 MΩ minimum for a random-wound stator.
  • ✓PI = 150 / 60 = 2.5, above the class B/F/H minimum of 2.0.
  • ✓A cold winding reads high, so an uncorrected 60 MΩ flatters it; the same winding at 20 °C would show 80 MΩ raw, which corrects to 20 MΩ.

For a 6.6 kV older winding the kV + 1 rule gives 7.6 MΩ; a modern form-wound machine at the same voltage is held to 100 MΩ. PI is largely independent of temperature because both readings shift together, which is why it is a useful cross-check.

Reading the result

High and risingDry, clean insulation. IR climbs through the test and PI is comfortably above the minimum.
Low but risingContamination or moisture that may dry out. Clean or bake out, then retest and trend.
Low and flatLeakage dominates. A PI near or below 1.0 means a winding with conductive moisture or damage; do not energize until investigated.

A single reading tells you little by itself. Log readings with the temperature and humidity, and compare against the previous test on the same equipment. A steady downward trend over years is the real warning, even when each value is above the minimum.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓Comparing readings at different temperatures without correcting to 40 °C.
  • ✓Testing at 250 V and applying the 0.5 MΩ limit to a 230 V circuit. The limit follows the circuit's voltage class, not the test voltage you chose.
  • ✓Leaving electronics or SPDs connected and either damaging them or measuring them instead of the wiring.
  • ✓Testing a cold motor below the dew point. Condensation on the windings gives a poor reading that a short warm-up would fix.
  • ✓Not discharging the cable or winding afterward.

Key takeaways

  • ✓LV: 250 V / 0.5 MΩ for SELV and PELV, 500 V / 1 MΩ up to 500 V, 1000 V / 1 MΩ above 500 V.
  • ✓The minimum is a floor, not a target. Investigate readings that are low compared with similar circuits or earlier tests.
  • ✓Motors: use the 1-minute spot reading and PI. Minimum PI is 2.0 for class B, F and H.
  • ✓Correct to 40 °C with Kt = 0.5(40−T)/10 before comparing.
  • ✓Confirm limits and test voltages in the standard edition and equipment manufacturer's instructions for your project.
