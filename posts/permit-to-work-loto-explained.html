Permit to Work & LOTO: Why the Lock Belongs to the Person, Not the Job

A permit to work authorizes the task. Lockout/tagout protects the individual doing it — and those are two different guarantees that both have to hold.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Project Management
01
A permit to work authorizes a specific task, at a specific location, for a specific time window — nothing broader.
02
Lockout/tagout physically isolates equipment so it cannot be energized while someone is working on it.
03
Each person applies their own lock — a lock removed on someone else's behalf defeats the entire system.
04
Isolation has to be verified, not assumed — a locked switch and a de-energized circuit aren't automatically the same thing.

Two different guarantees

It's easy to treat "permit to work" and "lockout/tagout" (LOTO) as the same paperwork, but they answer different questions. The permit answers: is this work authorized, by whom, where, and for how long? LOTO answers a narrower and more physical question: is the equipment actually incapable of harming the person working on it right now? A signed permit with no isolation is just permission to approach a live hazard. Isolation with no permit means work is happening outside any authorized, tracked scope. Both have to be true at once.

What a permit records

FieldWhat it pins down
Work descriptionExactly what task is authorized — not a general area of activity
Permit typeThe category of hazard involved (electrical, confined space, hot work, height, etc.)
Location / equipment tagThe specific asset, so there's no ambiguity about which equipment is isolated
Issued by / received byWho authorized the work and who's accountable for the crew doing it

None of these fields say anything about whether the equipment is actually safe to touch — that's what the isolation record covers separately.

One lock per person

Never remove someone else's lock The entire point of personal lockout is that each worker controls their own exposure. If Worker A steps away and Worker B removes A's lock to finish the job faster, the system has silently failed — the equipment could now be re-energized while A believes they're still protected. A lock only comes off when the person who applied it confirms they're clear. On multi-person jobs, a single isolation point often carries several locks on a shared hasp, and the equipment can't be restored until every single one is removed by its owner.

Isolate, then verify

Locking a switch or breaker in the off position isolates the control — it doesn't, by itself, confirm the equipment is de-energized. Verification is a separate step: testing at the point of work with a meter or equivalent method, after isolation and before work begins, to confirm there's genuinely no residual voltage, pressure, or stored energy. Skipping this step because "the breaker's locked" is one of the more common ways LOTO fails in practice — wiring mistakes, back-feed from another source, or stored energy in capacitors and springs can all leave a "locked-out" point still hazardous.

Worked example

A crew needs to service a pump fed from a motor control center, with a second technician working nearby on the associated piping.

Sequence

Permit issuedScope: pump service, MCC feeder 4B, 2-hour window
Isolation pointBreaker locked open, tagged, hasp fitted
Locks appliedTwo — one per technician
VerificationZero-energy check at motor terminals before work starts
RestorationOnly after both locks are removed by their owners

Key takeaways

  • ✓A permit authorizes the task; LOTO isolates the hazard — a project needs both, not one standing in for the other.
  • ✓Each worker applies and removes their own lock — removing another person's lock on their behalf breaks the system.
  • ✓Isolation must be verified at the point of work, not assumed from a locked switch alone.
  • ✓On multi-person jobs, equipment stays isolated until every applied lock is removed — not just the first one.
