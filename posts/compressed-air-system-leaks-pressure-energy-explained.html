Compressed Air Systems: Where the Energy Goes and How to Cut It

Only 10 to 15% of the electricity going into a compressor does useful work at the tool. Here is where the rest goes, how to cost leaks and pressure, and the practical steps that cut the bill.

Compressed air is often called the fourth utility, but unlike electricity, water and gas it is rarely metered or billed to a department. It is also one of the least efficient energy carriers in a plant. Only a small fraction of the electricity that goes into the compressor comes out as useful work at the tool, so every wasted cubic metre carries a large hidden cost.

Why it is so expensive

A compressor turns most of its input energy into heat, and the U.S. Department of Energy puts the overall wire-to-work efficiency of a typical system as low as 10 to 15%. To run a 1 hp air motor at 100 psig, roughly 7 to 8 hp of electrical power goes into the compressor. A rotary screw compressor commonly needs about 18 kW for every 100 cfm, which is about 6.4 kW for each cubic metre per minute, at around 7 bar. Treat that as a planning figure and use the measured value for your own machine.

Leaks: the biggest single loss

Leakage in a poorly maintained plant commonly takes 20 to 30% of compressor output, and DOE guidance treats under 10% as the mark of a well-maintained system. Leaks are invisible and silent in a noisy plant, so they persist. The cost of a leak is simple:

Cost per year = leak flow × kW per unit flow × hours × price per kWh

Worked example. A 1/32 inch leak at 100 psig passes about 1.55 cfm. Ten of them lose 15.5 cfm. At 0.18 kW per cfm, that is 2.79 kW, and over 7,500 hours it wastes 20,925 kWh, about 2,090 a year at 0.10 per kWh. A 1/4 inch leak loses far more, since flow rises with the square of the hole diameter, which is why even small holes are worth fixing.

  • Find them. An ultrasonic leak detector locates leaks in noisy areas, and soapy water works on accessible fittings. Tag each leak and record its size.
  • Measure the total. With the system charged and only leakage drawing air, the time the compressor takes to load and unload shows the leak share of output.
  • Fix and recheck. Savings only appear if the compressor controls are adjusted afterwards. A modulating compressor saves only a fraction of the demand cut, while a variable speed drive compressor follows demand more closely.

Pressure: the free saving

Compressor energy rises with discharge pressure, by roughly 1% for every 2 psi (about 0.14 bar) in the 100 psig range. Excess header pressure also pushes more air through every leak. Many systems run higher than they need because one distant user once had a problem.

Worked example. Lowering the discharge pressure by 1 bar, about 14.5 psi, saves roughly 14.5 ÷ 2 = 7%. On a compressor drawing 100 kW, that is about 7 kW, or 42,000 kWh over 6,000 hours. Check the pressure at the farthest user first, and cut pressure drop in filters, dryers and pipework so the band can come down. An unnecessary drop of 1 bar can raise generating costs by up to 7%.

Storage and demand

A receiver supplies short peaks so the compressor does not have to start and stop. For an intermittent demand taken from the receiver, the volume needed is approximately V = Q × t × p0 ÷ ΔP, where Q is the free air demand, t the time the demand lasts, p0 atmospheric pressure and ΔP the allowed pressure drop. For 2 m³/min of demand lasting 2 minutes with 1 bar allowed drop, the receiver needs about 2 × 2 × 1.013 ÷ 1 = 4.05 m³. This is a quick estimate that ignores the compressor’s own supply during the event.

Other habits that pay

  • Use air only where it is needed. Blow-off, cooling and agitation done with compressed air can often be done with a blower or fan at a fraction of the energy.
  • Regulate at the point of use. Tools should run at their rated pressure, not at the main pressure.
  • Recover the heat. Most of the input energy leaves as heat, and a well-designed system can capture much of it for space or water heating.
  • Match the control to the load. Sequence multiple compressors so they do not fight, and use variable speed on the trim machine.

For the electrical side of the cost, see the running cost of a load, and for how speed control saves energy, VFD speed scaling. Friction in the distribution pipework follows the same principles as in pipe head loss.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cost leaks with flow × kW per unit flow × hours × tariff, then find and fix them.
  • ✓Lower the discharge pressure to what the farthest user really needs.
  • ✓Size receivers from demand, duration and allowed pressure drop.
  • ✓Use compressed air only where nothing cheaper will do the job, and recover the heat where possible.
