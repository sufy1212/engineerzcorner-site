Incoterms 2020 Explained: Who Pays, and Where the Risk Passes

Three letters in a sales contract decide who arranges transport, who insures it and who bears the loss if something goes wrong. Here are the eleven 2020 rules in one place, with the mistakes to avoid.

Incoterms are the standard trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce. A short three-letter code in a sales contract settles who arranges and pays for transport, insurance and customs, and the exact point where the risk of loss or damage passes from the seller to the buyer. The current edition, Incoterms 2020, came into force on 1 January 2020 and has eleven rules.

What Incoterms do, and what they do not

They settle

Delivery, the transfer of risk, who pays for carriage and insurance, and who handles export and import clearance.

They do not settle

Who owns the goods, the price, the payment terms, or what happens in a dispute. Those belong elsewhere in the sales contract.

The eleven rules in two families

Seven rules work for any mode of transport, including road, rail, air and multimodal shipments. Four rules are for sea and inland waterway only. For containerised cargo that is handed to a carrier at a terminal, the any-mode rules usually fit better than the sea-only ones.

The trap in the C-terms

In CPT, CIP, CFR and CIF the seller pays for the main carriage, which makes it look as if the seller carries the risk all the way. It does not. Risk still passes to the buyer early, at handover to the first carrier or on loading, and the buyer carries the risk during the sea or road journey. Cost and risk are separate questions in Incoterms, so read both columns.

Insurance under CIF and CIP

CIF and CIP oblige the seller to buy cargo insurance for the buyer, but at different levels. CIF requires at least Institute Cargo Clauses (C), a minimum cover for listed risks. Since 2020, CIP requires Institute Cargo Clauses (A), which is all-risks cover subject to exclusions. The cover is normally set at the contract price plus 10%. A buyer who wants more than the minimum should agree it in the contract.

What changed in 2020

  • DAT became DPU. The place of delivery, with unloading, can now be any place, not only a terminal.
  • CIP insurance went up from clauses (C) to clauses (A). CIF stayed at (C).
  • FCA gained an option. The buyer can instruct its carrier to issue an on-board bill of lading to the seller, which helps with letters of credit.
  • Own transport is allowed, where the seller or buyer moves the goods with its own vehicles, and security obligations are stated in each rule.

Using them well in a contract

Always write the rule, the named place and the version together, for example “FCA Hamburg, Incoterms 2020”. The named place matters, because it fixes where delivery and risk transfer happen. Use a sea-only rule only for port-to-port bulk or break-bulk shipments, and match the rule to the real transport route. For a live contract, work from the ICC rules themselves. The Incoterms reference on this site gives a plain-language summary for quick checks, and the commercial terms reference covers related contract terms.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cost and risk are separate: check where risk passes, not only who pays freight.
  • ✓Name the rule, the place and the version, such as FCA Hamburg, Incoterms 2020.
  • ✓Use any-mode rules for containers and multimodal freight, and sea-only rules for port-to-port shipments.
  • ✓Incoterms do not cover title, price, payment or disputes, so state those separately in the contract.
