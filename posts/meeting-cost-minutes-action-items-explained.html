The Real Cost of Meetings, and How to Make Minutes and Actions Pay Off

A recurring meeting is a standing cost in people’s time. Here is the simple formula, a worked weekly example, the levers that reduce it and how minutes with owned actions turn the time into results.

A one-hour meeting with eight people is not one hour of cost. It is eight hours of salaried time, and if it repeats every week it becomes a standing line item in the project budget that nobody approved. Putting a number on it does not mean meetings are wasteful. It means they should earn what they cost.

The simple cost model

Cost = attendees × hourly rate × (minutes ÷ 60)

Use a fully loaded hourly rate, which covers salary, benefits and overhead, and a rough blended average across the group is enough. For a recurring meeting, multiply by the occurrences per year, 52 for weekly and 12 for monthly. This is a gut-check for comparing meeting formats, not a payroll figure.

Worked example

Eight people at a blended rate of 60 per hour, meeting for 90 minutes, cost 8 × 60 × 1.5 = 720. Held weekly that is 720 × 52 = 37,440 a year.

Now trim it: only the five people who need to decide attend, and the meeting is held to 45 minutes. The cost becomes 5 × 60 × 0.75 = 225 each time, or 11,700 a year. The saving is 25,740 a year in people’s time, from changing nothing but the guest list and the length.

The three levers

Who attends

Invite people who decide or do the work. Others can read the minutes. The cost scales directly with headcount.

How long and how often

Set the length from the agenda, not from a calendar default, and ask whether a weekly meeting could be fortnightly or monthly.

Make the meeting pay for itself

  • Send an agenda with decisions needed. A meeting that exists to decide something is easy to justify. One that exists to report can often be an email.
  • Record decisions and actions, not a transcript. Good minutes list the attendees, the decisions made, and each action with an owner and a due date. The meeting minutes tool generates that structure in a consistent order.
  • Follow up on actions. Start the next meeting by checking last time’s actions. If they are not done, the cost of the first meeting bought nothing.
  • Review standing meetings. If the annual figure looks large against the value delivered, shorten it, cut the list or drop the cadence.

Cost is not the only measure. A short meeting that avoids a costly mistake is cheap at any price, and some meetings carry value that does not show in the figure, such as safety briefings or client relationship. The point is to decide deliberately. To check your own numbers, use the meeting cost calculator. For tracking the actions that come out of a meeting, see the task tracker, and for how risks and late answers get raised, RFIs and the RFI log.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Treat a recurring meeting as a standing cost and review it from time to time.
  • ✓Invite the people who decide or do, and let others read the minutes.
  • ✓Write minutes as decisions plus owned, dated actions, and check the actions at the next meeting.
  • ✓The cost figure is a gut-check, not a verdict on whether the meeting is worthwhile.
