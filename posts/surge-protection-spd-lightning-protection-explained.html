SPD & Lightning Protection Explained

Lightning protection keeps the strike off the structure; surge protection keeps what it leaves behind off the equipment. They are two halves of one design, and an SPD with a good datasheet still fails when the lightning level, the Type, the voltage rating and the lead length are not chosen together.

Engineerz CorneRReference · IEC 62305 / IEC 61643-11 / IEC 60364-5-53
01
A lightning protection system (LPS) catches the strike and carries the current to earth. It does not stop surges entering through power and data lines, which is the job of SPDs.
02
Type 1 SPDs are tested with the 10/350 µs lightning current (Iimp). Type 2 use 8/20 µs (In, Imax). Type 3 use a 1.2/50 µs, 8/20 µs combination wave. Their kA values are not interchangeable.
03
Uc must sit above the highest normal system voltage, and Up must sit below what the protected equipment can withstand. For 230/400 V equipment that is 1.5 to 6 kV depending on category.
04
Wiring matters as much as the device. Every metre of lead adds roughly 1 kV for each kA/µs of surge current, which can wipe out the protection level you paid for.

Two problems, two layers

A direct strike to a building is a high-energy event. The LPS (air terminals, down conductors, earth termination) gives the current a controlled path so it does not go through the structure. Even with a perfect LPS, a large part of the current ends up on earthing and on incoming services, and nearby strikes induce voltages in cables without touching the building at all.

Surge protective devices handle that second problem. They are voltage-limiting or voltage-switching components that sit between conductors and earth, stay out of the way at normal voltage, and divert the surge when the voltage rises. The standards treat the two layers together: IEC 62305 for the lightning risk and protection measures, IEC 61643-11 for the SPD product, IEC 60364-5-53 for selecting and installing SPDs in low-voltage installations.

Lightning protection levels

IEC 62305-1 defines four lightning protection levels (LPL I to IV). Each fixes a maximum lightning current, used to design components such as LPS conductors and Type 1 SPDs, and a minimum current, which sets the rolling sphere radius and therefore how many strikes the air termination is expected to intercept.

LPLMax peak currentMin peak currentInterception efficiencyRolling sphere radius
I200 kA3 kA0.9920 m
II150 kA5 kA0.9730 m
III100 kA10 kA0.9145 m
IV100 kA16 kA0.8460 m

The same classes set the other air-termination geometry in IEC 62305-3: mesh size of 5×5, 10×10, 15×15 and 20×20 m, and typical down conductor spacing of 10, 10, 15 and 20 m for classes I to IV. Which level a structure needs is not a free choice. IEC 62305-2 requires a risk assessment covering loss of human life, loss of public services, loss of cultural heritage and economic loss, and the chosen level must bring the calculated risk below the tolerable value.

Protection level vs. strike current: a higher efficiency does not mean the system survives a bigger strike, it means it intercepts a larger share of strikes, including weaker ones that a coarser sphere would let through to the roof or wall.

SPD Types and what they are tested with

IEC 61643-11 classifies low-voltage power SPDs by test class, which maps to the Types used on datasheets and in IEC 60364.

Type (Class)Test waveform and ratingTypical locationWhat it is for
Type 1 (Class I)10/350 µs impulse current, IimpService entrance, main board of a building with an LPS or exposed overhead supplyTakes partial lightning current conducted along the incoming conductors
Type 2 (Class II)8/20 µs current, In and ImaxDistribution and sub-distribution boardsLimits residual and induced surges; the workhorse device
Type 3 (Class III)Combination wave: 1.2/50 µs open-circuit voltage, 8/20 µs short-circuit currentClose to sensitive equipment, socket outletsFinal voltage trimming at the load

The 10/350 µs wave has a long tail and carries far more charge and energy than an 8/20 µs wave of the same peak. That is why a Type 1 rated 12.5 kA (10/350) and a Type 2 rated 40 kA Imax (8/20) are different duties, and why comparing the two kA numbers on a datasheet tells you nothing. Combined Type 1+2 devices are tested to both.

The ratings that matter on a datasheet

Uc and Up
Maximum continuous operating voltage and voltage protection level

Uc is the highest voltage the SPD can sit across continuously without conducting. It has to clear the highest voltage the system will actually see, including the usual 10% supply tolerance: for line-to-neutral connection on a 230/400 V TN or TT system that means at least 1.1 × 230 = 253 V, and datasheets commonly show 275 V or 320 V. IT systems and neutral-to-earth connections in TT systems have their own, higher requirements in IEC 60364-5-53, so check the selection table rather than reusing the TN number. Up is the peak voltage that appears across the SPD terminals while it is diverting its rated surge. Lower is better for the equipment, but only if Uc, Type and installation are also right.

  • ✓Iimp (Type 1): peak current of the 10/350 µs impulse the SPD must withstand, rated per protection mode.
  • ✓In (Type 2): nominal 8/20 µs discharge current the device survives repeatedly through its life. This is the number to design with.
  • ✓Imax (Type 2): the 8/20 µs current the device survives once. It is not a reliable basis for choosing between products.
  • ✓Short-circuit and follow-current ratings, and backup protection: the SPD needs the upstream fuse or breaker its manufacturer specifies, and a rating that matches the prospective fault level at its terminals.

What Up has to beat: impulse withstand

IEC 60664-1 groups equipment into overvoltage categories, each with a rated impulse withstand voltage. The values below are for 230/400 V supplies. The SPD at a board only protects what is connected downstream of it if its Up (plus lead effects) stays below the withstand of the equipment actually connected.

CategoryTypical equipmentImpulse withstand, 230/400 V
IVAt the origin of the installation: meters, main incoming equipment6 kV
IIIFixed installation: distribution boards, switchgear, fixed motors4 kV
IIAppliances and portable tools connected to socket outlets2.5 kV
ISensitive electronics protected by external means1.5 kV

A common design target is Up at or below about 80% of the withstand of the equipment being protected, to leave margin for ageing and wiring effects. That margin is a design convention, not a clause of the standard.

Sizing the Type 1 duty

For a structure with an LPS, a simplified way to estimate the lightning current each Type 1 pole may see starts from the maximum current for the chosen level and the usual assumption that half of it flows into the earth termination and half is shared among the services entering the building.

Illustrative case: LPL III (100 kA), one three-phase TN-S supply as the only incoming service, protected conductors L1, L2, L3 and N. Current into services = 100 kA × 0.5 = 50 kA. Shared equally across four conductors, that is 12.5 kA (10/350 µs) per pole, which is why 12.5 kA is a widely used Type 1 rating. More services entering (data, water pipes, a second feeder) lowers the share per conductor, and a lower LPL reduces the starting current. Treat this as a screening estimate; IEC 62305-4 and IEC 60364-5-53 give the full method.

Coordination and lead length

Cascade the stagesType 1 at the entrance takes the bulk of the energy with a higher Up. Type 2 downstream trims what is left. The two must be coordinated so the downstream device is not overloaded.
Separation or decouplingMany manufacturers require around 10 m of cable between a Type 1 and a Type 2 for energy coordination, or a decoupling inductor if the run is shorter. Use the figure in the product documentation, not a rule of thumb.
Keep leads shortConnecting conductors have inductance of about 1 µH per metre, so the surge di/dt creates a voltage drop on every metre of lead. Keep total lead length as short as practicable, commonly under 0.5 m.

Worked example of lead effect. Take a Type 2 with Up = 1.5 kV. A surge near the nominal rating of 20 kA rising over 8 µs has an average slope of roughly 2.5 kA/µs. With 1 µH/m: a 0.5 m total lead adds about 1.25 kV, giving about 2.75 kV at the equipment; a 1 m lead adds about 2.5 kV, giving about 4.0 kV. The first fits Category III (4 kV) but not Category II (2.5 kV); the second uses up the entire Category III withstand. The device did not change, only the wiring did. The average slope understates the steepest part of the front, so real peaks can be higher.

Earthing and bonding make it work

An SPD can only divert a surge to a place that can take it. Equipotential bonding of incoming metalwork, a low-impedance earth termination and a short, direct SPD earth connection are what keep voltages between systems under control. An SPD fed by a long, thin earth lead is a decoration. For the earthing side see Earthing & Grounding Systems Explained and Types of Earthing Systems; ground resistance can be estimated with the earthing rod and earthing grid calculators.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓Comparing Iimp with Imax. They come from different waveforms and different duties. Select by Type first, then by Iimp or In, then by Up.
  • ✓Only fitting Type 2 on a building with an LPS. Direct strike current can enter along services; the entrance needs Type 1 or a combined 1+2 device.
  • ✓Choosing the lowest Up and ignoring Uc. An SPD with a Uc below the real system voltage will age quickly or fail on a normal supply swell.
  • ✓Long, looped connecting leads. They add volts per metre and can defeat the protection level before the surge reaches the load.
  • ✓Fit and forget. SPDs wear out. Check status indicators or remote contacts, and inspect after severe storms or known surge events.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The LPL comes from a risk assessment under IEC 62305-2 and fixes the maximum current (200, 150, 100, 100 kA) and sphere radius (20, 30, 45, 60 m).
  • ✓Type 1 handles 10/350 µs lightning current at the entrance, Type 2 handles 8/20 µs surges at boards, Type 3 does final trimming at the load.
  • ✓Choose Uc above the maximum system voltage and Up (including lead drop) below the impulse withstand of the equipment: 6, 4, 2.5 and 1.5 kV for categories IV to I at 230/400 V.
  • ✓Lead length and earthing can cancel a good Up. Roughly 1 kV per metre per kA/µs is the number to remember.
  • ✓Examples here are screening estimates. Confirm the design against IEC 62305, IEC 60364-5-53 and the SPD manufacturer's documentation.
