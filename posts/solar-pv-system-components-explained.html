Solar PV System Components Explained: From Panel to Load

A solar array on the roof is only the first link in the chain. Getting usable power out the other end takes a charge controller, a battery bank, an inverter, and a web of balance-of-system parts that rarely get talked about — here's what each one actually does.

One array, several conversions

It's easy to picture a solar system as "panels plus batteries," but there are several distinct stages between sunlight hitting a cell and a load actually running. The PV array captures energy and turns it into raw DC. The charge controller regulates that DC before it reaches storage. The battery bank holds energy chemically for later use. The inverter converts stored or incoming DC into the AC most loads and the grid expect. And a set of balance-of-system parts — wiring, combiner boxes, disconnects, monitoring — ties the whole thing together safely.

Grid-tied systems without batteries skip the charge controller and battery stages, feeding array output straight to an inverter. But the underlying pattern — generate, regulate, convert, protect — holds for nearly every PV installation.

1PV Modules (Panels)

  • Photovoltaic cells convert sunlight directly into DC electricity through the photovoltaic effect
  • Rated output is measured at Standard Test Conditions (STC) — real-world output is almost always lower
  • Modules are wired in series into strings to build up array voltage, then strings combine to build current

2Charge Controller

  • Sits between the array and battery, regulating current so the battery charges without being overcharged
  • MPPT controllers track the panel's peak power point for higher efficiency; PWM controllers are simpler and cheaper
  • Sized against both array output and battery bank voltage — a mismatch here is one of the most common sizing mistakes

3Battery Bank

  • Stores surplus DC energy chemically so it's available when the array isn't producing — at night or in cloudy weather
  • Usable capacity depends on chemistry and depth of discharge (DOD), not just the nameplate Ah rating
  • Battery voltage sets the DC bus voltage that the charge controller and inverter both have to match

4Inverter

  • Converts DC from the array or battery bank into AC power that standard loads — or the grid — can actually use
  • Grid-tie inverters synchronize output to grid frequency and voltage; off-grid inverters create their own AC reference
  • Hybrid inverters combine both roles, drawing from array or battery and optionally exporting surplus to the grid

5Balance of System (BOS)

  • Combiner boxes bring multiple strings together; disconnects provide safe isolation points for maintenance
  • Mounting and racking hardware sets array tilt and orientation, and has to handle real wind and snow loads
  • Monitoring hardware tracks production and system health, often the only visibility into a fault before it grows

How energy actually flows

1Sunlight strikes the PV array, generating DC current roughly proportional to irradiance.
2The charge controller regulates that DC output, feeding it into the battery bank without overcharging it.
3The battery bank stores surplus energy chemically for use whenever the array isn't producing enough.
4The inverter draws DC from the battery or array and converts it into AC power for loads or the grid.
5Balance-of-system components — combiners, disconnects, monitoring — protect and report on the whole chain throughout.

Why the battery and inverter stay separate

It might seem simpler to build storage and conversion into one box, and some hybrid products do exactly that. But keeping the battery bank and inverter as distinct components has real advantages: a battery chemistry or capacity upgrade doesn't force an inverter replacement, and an inverter fault doesn't strand stored energy behind it. The DC bus voltage is the contract between them — as long as both sides agree on it, either can be swapped, sized up, or serviced independently.

Why this architecture is common

Safety
Efficiency
Monitoring

Splitting a PV system into generation, regulation, storage and conversion stages is what makes it both safe and serviceable. Isolating the battery behind a charge controller protects an expensive asset from a cheap mistake; keeping the inverter separate means a fault there doesn't touch stored energy; and BOS components give installers and monitoring systems a clear point to isolate, measure, or troubleshoot any single stage without shutting down the rest.
