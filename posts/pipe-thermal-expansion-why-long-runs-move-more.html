Pipe Thermal Expansion: Why a 100m Run Moves More Than You'd Guess

Thermal growth scales linearly with length — so a run that seems too long to matter is exactly where it matters most.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Thermal Expansion
01
Linear thermal expansion is a simple direct proportion between material, run length and temperature change — nothing more exotic than that.
02
Because it scales linearly with length, a long straight run in a high-temperature-change service can move tens of millimetres, not a few.
03
Different pipe materials expand at very different rates for the same temperature change — plastic pipe moves several times more than steel.
04
This is the free, unrestrained growth a run would experience — if that growth is instead restrained, it turns into force on anchors and supports instead of movement.

The relationship itself

ΔL  =  α × L × ΔT
ΔL = linear expansion · α = coefficient of thermal expansion (material property) · L = pipe run length · ΔT = temperature change from installation (cold) to operating (hot) condition

This is about as simple as an engineering formula gets — a direct proportion between three quantities. What trips people up isn't the formula, it's underestimating how large ΔL becomes once realistic values for L and ΔT are plugged in for a genuinely long or genuinely hot run.

Why the numbers add up faster than intuition suggests

Steel's coefficient of thermal expansion is around 12×10−6 per °C. That sounds tiny in isolation — and for a 1 m length and a 10°C change, it is (0.12 mm, easily ignored). But expansion scales linearly with both length and temperature change, and neither of those stays small in real piping systems. A 100 m steel run carrying process fluid at 100°C above its installed (cold) temperature grows by roughly 120 mm — more than enough to rack a poorly guided support, overstress a rigidly anchored branch connection, or bind against nearby structure if no allowance was made for it.

Material matters as much as length

MaterialTypical α (per °C)Relative to steel
Steel~12×10−61× (baseline)
Copper~17×10−6~1.4×
PVC / plastic pipe~50–80×10−6~4–6×

A plastic pipe run experiencing the same length and temperature change as a steel run can move several times as much — a detail that's easy to miss when reusing steel-pipe support spacing and anchor practices for a plastic installation without re-checking the expansion allowance for the different material.

Free growth vs restrained growth

This calculation gives the free (unrestrained) expansion — how far the pipe would move if nothing stopped it. In a real installation, growth is deliberately allowed for at some points (loops, expansion joints, or guided sliding supports that let the pipe move along its axis) and deliberately restrained at others (anchors). Where growth is restrained rather than accommodated, it doesn't disappear — it converts into axial force on the anchor and the pipe wall instead, which is a separate stress calculation from the movement figure here, but starts from exactly the same ΔL.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Linear thermal expansion is a direct proportion between material coefficient, run length and temperature change.
  • ✓Because it scales with length, long runs in high-delta-T service can move tens of millimetres, not a few.
  • ✓Different materials expand at very different rates — plastic pipe can move several times more than steel for the same conditions.
  • ✓This is unrestrained growth — where a run is instead anchored rigidly, the same ΔL becomes force on the anchor rather than movement.
