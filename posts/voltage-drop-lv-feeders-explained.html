Voltage Drop in LV Feeders: Why It's a Chain, Not a Single Number

A cable can be perfectly sized for the current it carries and still be the wrong size once you check what it does to voltage at the far end.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Voltage Drop / IEC 60364-5-52
01
Voltage drop is a separate check from ampacity — a cable can carry the load current safely and still drop more voltage than acceptable over a long run.
02
Drop accumulates through a feeder chain: a downstream board inherits whatever voltage was already lost on every upstream segment feeding it.
03
Standards give recommended drop limits from origin to point of use (commonly cited around 3–5% depending on load type and local code) — always confirm the applicable limit for the project.
04
The two levers that reduce drop are shorter cable runs and larger conductor cross-section — current and power factor are usually fixed by the load.

Why ampacity isn't the whole story

Sizing a cable for ampacity answers one question: can it carry the load current continuously without overheating? Voltage drop answers a different question: how much voltage is lost along the conductor's resistance and reactance by the time the current reaches the load. A cable can pass the ampacity check comfortably and still fail voltage drop on a long run, because drop scales with length in a way ampacity does not.

This is why both checks are run independently, and the cable is sized to whichever one demands the larger cross-section.

The core relationship

ΔV  =  √3 × I × L × (R cosφ + X sinφ)  /  1000
I = load current (A) · L = cable length (m) · R, X = cable resistance/reactance per km (Ω/km) · φ = load power factor angle · result in volts for a 3-phase feeder

Every term in that equation except R and X is set by the load. R and X are properties of the cable itself — larger cross-section reduces R, which is why increasing cable size is the main lever available once length and current are fixed.

Why it compounds downstream

In a branching feeder chain, each segment's drop adds to whatever drop already occurred upstream. A distribution board fed through a long sub-main from the main panel starts already "behind" — whatever percentage was lost getting to that board is drop the final circuits downstream of it don't get to spend.

Short, heavily loaded runHigh current but short length can still land within limits — length is often the dominant factor, not current alone.
Long, lightly loaded runEven modest current can breach the limit over a long enough cable run, because ΔV scales directly with length.

Practical implication

Because drop is cumulative, checking only the final circuit in isolation misses the picture — the sub-main feeding that board may have already used up most of the allowable drop budget. A full check walks the same distribution tree used for the short-circuit study, adding drop segment by segment from source to the furthest load, and confirms the running total stays within the applicable limit at every point, not just the end.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Voltage drop and ampacity are two separate checks — passing one doesn't mean the other is satisfied.
  • ✓Drop accumulates through a feeder chain; a downstream board inherits whatever was already lost upstream.
  • ✓Length is often the dominant driver of drop, more than current alone.
  • ✓Always confirm the specific drop limit that applies — it varies by standard, load type, and local code.
