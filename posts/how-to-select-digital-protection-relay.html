How to Select a Digital Protection Relay

Picking the right relay isn't a catalog lookup — it's a sequence: know the equipment, gather the system data, match the inputs, then check everything else fits. Here's the working order engineers actually use.

Why relay selection is a sequence, not a spec sheet

It's tempting to shop for a protection relay the way you'd shop for any other panel component — by price, brand, or a familiar model number. But a relay that's wrong for its application doesn't just underperform, it can fail to trip when it should, or trip when it shouldn't — either one is a real problem on live equipment. The safer approach is to work through the same eight steps every time: identify what's being protected, gather the system data, match the input signals, select the functions, size the I/O and trip circuits, pick the communications, verify the operating environment, and finally run the coordination study. Skipping a step early tends to surface as a mismatch much later, once the relay is already installed.

Step 1 — Identify the protected equipment

Different equipment types need different ANSI protection functions from the start.

1Define equipment & required functions

  • Feeder: 50/51, 50N/51N, 46, 49, 50BF
  • Transformer: 87T, 64REF, 50/51, 49, 24
  • Motor: 49, 48/51LR, 50/51, 46, 37
  • Generator: 87G, 32, 40, 46, 24, 27/59, 81
  • Busbar: 87B, 50BF, arc flash

Step 2 — Collect system information

The data that shapes every downstream decision.

  • ✓Single line diagram
  • ✓System voltage, frequency & grounding
  • ✓Max load current, inrush / starting current
  • ✓Max / min 3-phase & earth fault current
  • ✓CT / VT ratios, classes & burden
  • ✓Breaker details (opening time, trip coil)
  • ✓Coordination requirements
  • ✓DC supply (24/48/110/125/220 VDC)
  • ✓Communication & SCADA requirements

Step 3 — Match input signals

CT and VT inputs have to match the relay, not the other way around.

ACurrent inputs

  • Match relay input with CT secondary (1 A or 5 A)
  • 3-phase CT inputs + 1 earth CT input
  • Check burden, accuracy, saturation, continuous & short-time current

BVoltage inputs (if required)

  • Match VT secondary (e.g. 100 V or 110 V)
  • 3-phase or 3-phase-to-earth connection
  • Residual / open-delta VT if needed
  • Frequency: 50 Hz or 60 Hz

Step 4 — Select protection functions

Start from a basic function set and add on top of it as the application demands — not the reverse. A typical baseline is 50/51, 50N/51N, 46, 49, and 74TCS (trip circuit supervision), with 27/59 and 81U/81O added when undervoltage, overvoltage, or frequency conditions matter, 67/67N added for non-radial or bidirectional systems, and any equipment-specific functions (transformer, motor, generator, busbar, capacitor bank) layered in from Step 1's list.

Step 5 — Check I/O & trip circuits

The part that's easiest to underspec even after the protection functions are right.

  • ✓Sufficient digital inputs (52a, 52b, interlocks, etc.)
  • ✓Trip output for breaker — check contact rating
  • ✓Close output if required
  • ✓Trip circuit supervision (74TCS)
  • ✓Lockout (86) and alarm contacts
  • ✓Arc flash / temperature / Buchholz inputs if required

Step 6 — Select communication

IEC 61850, Modbus TCP/RTU, DNP3, IEC 60870-5-103/104
Redundant Ethernet (PRP/HSR) if required
Time sync: SNTP / PTP / IRIG-B
GOOSE & Sampled Values for digital substations

Step 7 — Verify performance & environment

  • ✓Operating temperature, humidity, vibration, EMC
  • ✓Insulation level, IP protection
  • ✓Accuracy, operating time
  • ✓Event & disturbance recording
  • ✓Auxiliary supply range
  • ✓Compliance with IEC 60255 series

Step 8 — Perform protection coordination

The final step ties every earlier decision together: calculate pickup currents and time settings, coordinate with upstream and downstream devices, and confirm the scheme is sensitive enough to catch real faults, selective enough to isolate only the faulted section, and reliable enough to operate every time it's called on — while allowing for CT, relay, and breaker tolerances in the calculation.

Worked example — 11 kV radial feeder

System data

System11 kV, 50 Hz
Max load current420 A
CT ratio600/1 A
Max fault current12.5 kA
Min earth fault current800 A
Breaker trip supply110 VDC
SCADA protocolIEC 61850

Initial phase overcurrent pickup

I pickup ≈ 1.2 × 420 A = 504 A (primary)
I pickup (secondary) = 504 / 600 = 0.84 A
Note: validate by a full coordination study before committing to final settings.

What "the right relay" actually buys you

Right Protection — protects equipment and people
Right Reliability — fast, selective and secure
Right Integration — seamless with SCADA and substation
Right Investment — optimal cost and long-term value

Key takeaways

  • ✓Start from the protected equipment, not the relay catalog — the equipment determines which ANSI functions are actually required.
  • ✓CT/VT ratio, connection, burden and accuracy class must match the relay's rated inputs, or every downstream setting inherits the error.
  • ✓I/O count, trip contact rating, and trip circuit supervision are as important as the protection functions themselves — and easy to underspec.
  • ✓Selection isn't finished until a coordination study confirms the settings are sensitive, selective, and reliable against neighboring devices.
