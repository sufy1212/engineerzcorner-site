Change Order Cost and Schedule Impact

A change order has two prices: the money for the changed work and the time it takes. They are worked out separately, approved separately in effect, and only an approved change moves the contract baseline.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Project Management
01
Price = direct cost of the changed work plus the overhead and profit the contract allows. Use the contract's method, because the order of the markups changes the number.
02
Only approved change orders move the contract value and the completion date. Pending ones are exposure, and rejected ones are history.
03
Extra work earns a time extension only if it delays completion. A change that uses up float without reaching the critical path moves nothing.
04
A 20% markup on cost is a 16.7% margin on price. The two are not the same number, and mixing them up shorts the price.

Pricing the changed work

A change order is a written agreement that modifies the original contract. Its price starts from the direct cost the change causes: labour, materials, equipment and subcontractors. Contract-specified overhead and profit go on top. Pricing guidance consistently makes two points: charge for a cost only when the change causes it and the contract allows it, and apply the markup method the contract states, because where it is silent each side will argue for its own.

Example: added feeders

Labour: 120 h × $657,800
Materials9,500
Equipment hire1,200
Direct cost18,500
12% combined overhead and profit2,220
Change order price20,720

If the contract instead defines 8% overhead on direct cost, then 4% profit on direct cost plus overhead, the same job prices as 18,500 + 1,480 + 799.20 = 20,779.20. The two methods differ by 59.20 here, which is why the sequence in the contract language matters. Include the impact costs the change really causes, such as remobilisation or extra supervision, and keep them separate from items already covered by the overhead percentage, so nothing is counted twice.

Markup is not margin

Markup(price − cost) / cost
≠
Margin(price − cost) / price

A contract that allows a 20% markup on change cost gives a margin of 0.20 / 1.20 = 16.67% of the price, and it covers overhead as well as profit, so none of it is pure profit. To end up with a 20% margin you need a 25% markup. The detail is in Markup vs Margin Explained.

Only approved changes move the baseline

Until a change is formally agreed, it is a proposal or a claim. Keeping three lists, approved, pending and rejected, shows committed impact separately from what is still being negotiated. Here is a log on a 5,000,000 contract with a 180-day schedule:

Change orderCostDaysStatus
CO-1 Added feeders+20,720+5Approved
CO-2 Scope reduction-35,000+0Approved
CO-3 Added lab fit-out+64,000+12Pending
CO-4 Alternate finish+15,000+3Rejected
Revised contract = original + approved changes only Approved cost: -14,280 so revised value 4,985,720. Approved days: +5 so revised schedule 185 days. Pending exposure: +64,000 and +12 days, which would take the contract to 5,049,720 and 197 days if approved. Pending is 1.3% of the revised value.

This is the same arithmetic as the change order impact calculator. Treating a submitted change as if it were agreed hides real budget and schedule exposure until it is too late to react. Negative values are valid: a scope reduction or a schedule pull-in lowers the totals, as CO-2 does here.

When extra work earns extra time

Additional duration of work is not automatically additional contract time. In critical path terms the completion date only moves if the change delays the critical path. A change on a path with float first uses the float. A change that merely consumes float on a non-critical path does not entitle anyone to a time extension, and concurrent delay (a client-caused change and a contractor-caused delay hitting the critical path together) needs separate treatment under the contract.

Activity affectedTotal floatAdded durationCompletion delay
Non-critical task10 days8 days0 days (2 days of float left)
Non-critical task10 days14 days4 days
Critical task0 days8 days8 days

To prove it, show the change inserted into the current schedule, usually as a time impact analysis, with the updated critical path. If no current CPM schedule is kept, the argument becomes one expert's opinion against another's. The method is explained in Critical Path Method Explained.

Protecting the claim

  • ✓Read how the contract treats cumulative impact. Many forms include waivers, which makes the change order the one chance to price all related impacts.
  • ✓Where the work must proceed before price is agreed (under a change directive, for example), send written notice reserving rights to cost, time and markup.
  • ✓Be aware that in some jurisdictions an agreed overhead markup on a change has been held to cover overhead for the delay it causes. Whether that applies depends on the contract and the law, so take advice before relying on a separate delay claim.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Pricing only the new workRemobilisation, rework and extra supervision caused by the change are left out
Counting pending changes in the revised valueThe budget looks settled when it is not
Asking for time on every changeTime is only due when the critical path is delayed
Mixing markup and marginA 20% markup is not a 20% margin
Applying overhead and profit in the wrong orderThe contract's sequence can change the total
No schedule evidenceThe time claim cannot be demonstrated

Key takeaways

  • ✓Change order price = direct cost + contract-allowed overhead and profit, using the contract's own method.
  • ✓Revised contract value and schedule include approved changes only; track pending exposure on its own.
  • ✓Time follows the critical path: work on a non-critical path that stays within float earns no extension.
  • ✓20% markup = 16.67% margin; 20% margin needs a 25% markup.
  • ✓Confirm all entitlements and notice requirements against your contract and applicable law.
