CAV vs VAV: Constant vs Variable Air Volume Explained

Every HVAC system exists to provide comfort, protect indoor air quality, and save energy. CAV and VAV are the two ways an air system decides how much air to actually deliver — and that one decision shapes cost, comfort, and energy use for the life of the building.

Two ways to answer the same question

Every HVAC system is built to do three things: provide comfort, ensure indoor air quality, and save energy. How much air to actually push into a space is the core decision behind all three — and there are two established strategies for making it. CAV (Constant Air Volume) systems always deliver the same airflow and adjust temperature to match the load. VAV (Variable Air Volume) systems hold temperature closer to constant and instead adjust how much air they deliver.

Neither approach is universally better — the right choice depends on building size, zoning, and how much load varies from one part of the building to another.

1CAV — Constant Air Volume

  • Delivers a fixed, constant airflow to the space at all times, regardless of the actual cooling load
  • Space temperature is controlled by a reheat or cooling coil, not by changing how much air arrives
  • Simple duct layout — a fixed damper position, with the AHU sized for peak airflow at all hours
  • Best suited to small buildings, single-zone areas, and spaces with steady, predictable load

2VAV — Variable Air Volume

  • Varies airflow to each zone based on that zone's real-time cooling load, tracked by a VAV box
  • Each VAV box modulates a damper to throttle volume, with reheat available for zones that need it
  • Zone-by-zone temperature control — different areas of the same building can run different setpoints
  • Best suited to large buildings, multi-zone areas, and spaces with varying occupancy or load conditions

How a VAV zone actually responds

1A zone's cooling load drops as occupancy or solar gain falls, so its space temperature moves toward setpoint.
2The zone thermostat signals the VAV box controller that less cooling is now needed.
3The VAV box damper modulates closed, reducing the airflow delivered to that zone.
4Across the building, the AHU fan senses lower total demand through duct static pressure and slows down.
5Fan energy drops with airflow — the mechanism behind VAV's part-load efficiency advantage over CAV.

Choosing between them

The decision usually comes down to how uniform the building's load is. A small building or single-zone space with a steady, predictable load rarely justifies VAV's added design and commissioning complexity — CAV does the job at a lower first cost. A large, multi-zone building with occupancy and solar gain that vary by floor, orientation, or time of day is where VAV earns back its higher upfront cost, often within a few years of lower fan and reheat energy use.

Key takeaways

  • ✓CAV holds airflow constant and varies temperature through reheat or cooling coils — simple, but energy-hungry at part load.
  • ✓VAV holds temperature closer to constant and varies airflow zone by zone through VAV boxes — efficient, but more complex to design and commission.
  • ✓CAV suits small, single-zone buildings with steady load; VAV suits large, multi-zone buildings with varying occupancy and load.
  • ✓VAV's efficiency advantage comes directly from fan energy dropping as duct static pressure falls with lower total demand.
  • ✓CAV is simple and cost-effective; VAV is smart and energy-efficient — the right pick depends on the building, not a universal rule.
