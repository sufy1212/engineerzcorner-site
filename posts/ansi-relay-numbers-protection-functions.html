ANSI Relay Numbers: Common Protection Relay Functions

Protection schemes are drawn in numbers, not words. Here's what the ANSI device numbers you'll actually run into — 50, 51, 87, 27, 21 and the rest — mean, and where each one shows up.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical / Protection
01
ANSI device numbers are a standardized shorthand — 87 always means differential protection, everywhere, on every drawing.
02
A letter suffix changes the scope: 51 is time-delayed phase overcurrent, 51N is the same logic applied to earth (residual) current.
03
Most protection schemes stack several of these functions together in one multifunction numerical relay, not one device per number.
04
Relay technology has moved from electromechanical, to static, to numerical — each generation faster and more capable than the last.

Why a number instead of a name

ANSI/IEEE device numbers exist so that a protection scheme can be read identically by anyone, in any country, regardless of what language the drawing's labels are written in. A "51" on a single-line diagram means time-delayed overcurrent protection whether the rest of the drawing is in English, German, or Arabic — the number itself carries the meaning, which is exactly why relay numbering has stuck around since long before microprocessor relays existed and is still the standard way schemes are documented today.

Modern numerical relays typically bundle many of these functions into a single physical device, so seeing "50/51/50N/51N" on one relay's nameplate is normal — it just means that one relay is running four separate protection functions at once, each independently configurable, rather than needing four separate electromechanical relays wired together the way older substations did.

50Instantaneous OvercurrentTrips instantly once current exceeds a preset value, with no intentional delayShort-circuit protection

How relay technology got here

The same protection functions above have been implemented by three very different generations of hardware, each replacing the last for the same underlying reason — better speed, accuracy, and reliability for less ongoing maintenance.

1Electromechanical Relays — Older Substations

  • Uses moving parts — discs, coils, armatures, springs
  • Operates on the electromagnetic induction principle
  • Simple and rugged, but bulky, slower to operate, and higher maintenance

2Static Relays — Transition Technology

  • No moving parts — uses transistors, diodes, op-amps and logic circuits
  • Faster and more consistent than electromechanical relays
  • More compact, with lower ongoing maintenance

3Numerical Relays — Modern Plants

  • Microprocessor-based, with very high speed and accuracy
  • Multiple protection functions combined in one device
  • Event recording, fault logs, oscillography, self-diagnostics and communication built in

Key takeaways

  • ✓ANSI device numbers are a universal shorthand — 87 means differential protection on any drawing, in any country.
  • ✓An "N" suffix (50N, 51N) shifts the same overcurrent logic from phase current to earth/residual current.
  • ✓A single modern numerical relay typically implements many of these ANSI functions at once, not one relay per number.
  • ✓Relay hardware evolved electromechanical → static → numerical, each generation trading bulk and maintenance for speed and functionality.
