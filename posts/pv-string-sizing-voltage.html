PV String Sizing: Why Voltage, Not Wattage, Decides Panels Per String

A string that's perfectly sized in the midday sun can push past the inverter's voltage limit on a cold, bright morning — because panel voltage moves the opposite way from temperature.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Solar / PV System Design
01
Panel voltage rises as temperature falls, and falls as temperature rises — the opposite of power output.
02
Too many panels in series on a cold day can push open-circuit voltage past the inverter's absolute maximum.
03
Too few panels on a hot day can drop the string below the inverter's minimum MPPT voltage window.
04
String sizing is really about the coldest and hottest days the site will see, not the average one.

The counter-intuitive part

It feels natural to assume a hotter, sunnier day means more voltage and more output. Power does rise with sunlight — but voltage moves the other way with temperature. A crystalline silicon panel's open-circuit voltage (Voc) climbs as the cell gets colder, and drops as it gets hotter. That relationship is captured in the panel's temperature coefficient of voltage, usually given as a percentage per degree Celsius away from the panel's standard test condition of 25°C.

Wire enough panels in series, and on a cold, bright winter morning — ideal light, low cell temperature — the string's open-circuit voltage can spike well above what it reads at 25°C. If that spike crosses the inverter's absolute maximum input voltage, it's not just inefficient. It can trip the inverter or damage it.

Cold morning riskLow cell temperature pushes Voc up. Too many panels in series risks exceeding the inverter's max DC input voltage.
Hot afternoon riskHigh cell temperature pulls voltage down. Too few panels in series risks falling below the inverter's minimum MPPT voltage window.

The minimum panel count runs the same logic in reverse: string voltage at the hottest expected day, at maximum power point, must stay above the inverter's minimum MPPT voltage.

Key takeaways

  • ✓String sizing is a temperature-extremes problem, not a nameplate-wattage problem.
  • ✓Check the coldest recorded site temperature against the inverter's max voltage, not just standard test conditions.
  • ✓Check the hottest recorded site temperature against the inverter's minimum MPPT voltage too — both ends matter.
  • ✓Every panel model has its own temperature coefficient — pull it from the specific datasheet, not a generic assumption.
