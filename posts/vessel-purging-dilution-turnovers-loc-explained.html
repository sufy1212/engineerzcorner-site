Vessel Purging and Inerting Explained

Purging looks simple: push gas in until the oxygen is low enough. Whether that takes two turnovers or six, and what “low enough” means, depends on the method, the fuel and how well the vessel actually mixes.

Why purge: the limiting oxygen concentration

A flammable mixture cannot propagate a flame below a certain oxygen level, whatever the fuel concentration. That level is the limiting oxygen concentration (LOC), also called the minimum oxygen concentration. Replacing air with an inert gas (usually nitrogen) until the oxygen is under the LOC is inerting. LOC depends on the fuel and the diluent: it is roughly 12 vol% O₂ for methane with nitrogen and about 5 vol% for hydrogen. With carbon dioxide as the diluent the LOC is higher than with nitrogen, so less dilution is needed. LOC also falls as temperature rises, so a handbook value at ambient temperature can be unsafe in a hot vessel.

ASTM E2079 gives the standard test method. Its larger test vessel gives LOC values roughly 1.5 vol% lower than the older flammability-tube data that much of the literature and standards tables rely on, which is a reason to know where your number came from.

How far below the LOC to operate

NFPA 69 requires a safety margin below the LOC. As summarised in the sources I checked: with continuous oxygen monitoring, operate at least 2 volume percentage points below the LOC when the LOC is at least 5%, and at no more than 60% of the LOC when it is lower. Without continuous monitoring the margin is larger, commonly stated as 60% of the LOC (40% where the LOC is below 5%). One reference places the cut-off between the two rules at 10% O₂ instead of 5%, so confirm the wording in the edition you work to.

Dilution purge: how many turnovers

If clean gas enters at flow Q and an equal volume leaves a well-mixed vessel of volume V, the concentration decays exponentially:

1Concentration: C(t) = C₀ × e−Qt/V.
2Turnovers needed: N = ln(C₀ / C₁), where C₁ is the target.
3Time and gas: t = (V / Q) × N, and purge gas used = V × N.

The flow rate changes how long it takes, not how many turnovers or how much gas, in this ideal model. This is exactly what your purge gas sizing calculator does.

Worked example: a 50 m³ vessel purged at 100 m³/h from air (20.9% O₂).

Target O₂Turnovers N = ln(20.9 / target)TimeNitrogen used
10% (methane, monitored)0.7422 min37 m³

A useful benchmark: five turnovers leave e−5 = 0.67% of the original concentration, about 99.3% removed, and ln(100) = 4.6 turnovers removes 99%.

Pressure-swing purge

Instead of flowing continuously, the vessel is pressurised with inert gas, mixed, then vented back down. For ideal gas and perfect mixing each cycle multiplies the concentration by the ratio of low to high absolute pressure.

Cn = C₀ × ( Plow / Phigh )n

With the same vessel taken from 1 bar to 4 bar absolute with nitrogen and vented back, each cycle divides the oxygen by 4: 20.9% → 5.22% → 1.31% → 0.33%. To reach 2% needs ln(20.9/2) / ln(4) = 1.69, so 2 cycles. Each cycle takes (4 − 1) × 50 = 150 m³ of nitrogen at 1 bar, so 2 cycles use 300 m³, against 117 m³ for the dilution purge to the same 2% target.

Dilution uses less gas in this example, but only the pressure-swing model puts a mixing step into every cycle, which is why it is attractive for awkward vessel internals. A vacuum purge uses the same formula with the low pressure below atmospheric.

When the model is too optimistic

Both formulas assume the gas mixes instantly and perfectly. Real vessels have baffles, dead legs and stratification (nitrogen is slightly lighter than air, so it tends to sit on top). The inert gas can also short-circuit from the inlet straight to the vent. The result is that the concentration at the worst point falls more slowly than the calculation says.

PracticeWhy
Measure oxygen at the far point and at the vent, not only at the outletThe calculation gives an average; the dead zone sets the risk
Put the inert inlet and the vent at opposite ends, and at the right heights for the gas densitiesReduces short-circuiting and layering
Add margin to the calculated time or volumeThe ideal model is a minimum
Hold the target for a soak period, then re-measureGas trapped in dead legs can diffuse back

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Purging to the LOCThere is no margin left for oxygen ingress or analyser error
Using an ambient LOC for a hot processThe true LOC is lower at elevated temperature
Assuming a higher flow rate needs fewer turnoversTurnovers depend only on the concentration ratio; flow only sets how fast
Trusting the calculated time without measurementDead zones can stay above the target long after the average has passed
Mixing up gauge and absolute pressure in the cycle formulaThe ratio must use absolute pressures
Purging a space people will enter with nitrogen aloneAn inert atmosphere is oxygen-deficient, so entry also needs the confined-space procedure and ventilation back to a breathable level

Key takeaways

  • ✓Dilution purge: N = ln(C₀/C₁) turnovers, t = (V/Q) × N, gas used = V × N.
  • ✓Pressure swing: Cn = C₀ (Plow/Phigh)n, using absolute pressures.
  • ✓Target the LOC minus a margin (2 vol% below with continuous monitoring when the LOC is 5% or more, per NFPA 69 as summarised), and treat the LOC as temperature dependent.
  • ✓The ideal-mixing models are minimums; verify with oxygen measurements at the worst point.
  • ✓Confirm the LOC, margins and procedure against the NFPA 69 edition and your site's process safety requirements.
