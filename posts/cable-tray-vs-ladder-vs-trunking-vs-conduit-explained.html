Cable Tray vs Cable Ladder vs Cable Trunking vs Conduit

Four different ways to get a cable from A to B, each trading off protection, capacity, and cost differently. Picking the wrong one isn't usually a code violation — it's an installation that's harder to maintain, more expensive than it needed to be, or both.

Four systems, one job done differently

Every cable route has to answer the same questions — how many cables, how heavy, how exposed to damage, and how much does inspection access matter. Cable tray and cable ladder answer with an open system: cables stay visible, and airflow around them helps with heat dissipation. Cable trunking and conduit answer with an enclosed system: better protection and appearance, at the cost of having to open the enclosure to see what's inside.

1Cable Tray

  • An open support system carrying multiple cables in a safe, organized way — perforated, solid-bottom, ladder-type, or wire mesh/basket
  • Sized on number of cables, cable size, and total cable diameter, with spare capacity for future additions — never filled 100%
  • Cable weight and total tray load capacity have to be checked, along with heat dissipation for the fill achieved
  • Material choice — GI, aluminium, or FRP — depends on the environment the tray runs through

2Cable Ladder

  • Rungs and side rails built for large, heavy power cables that need real mechanical strength, not just support
  • Straight sections, horizontal and vertical bends (inside/outside), reducers, tees, and crosses cover most layouts
  • Open rungs give the best ventilation and heat dissipation of any tray-family system, with easy access for maintenance
  • Support spacing is set by the cable weight and size actually being carried, not a fixed default

3Cable Trunking

  • An enclosed channel with a removable cover, routing and protecting cables while keeping the appearance neat
  • PVC, GI/metal, floor, dado, and multi-compartment types separate power, data, and other cable groups as needed
  • Sized on cable quantity following a 40–50% filling rule, with environment and IP rating factored into material choice
  • The removable cover makes it easy to access and modify a run without disturbing the rest of the installation

4Conduit

  • A pipe or tube system — PVC, GI/steel, EMT, flexible, or heavy-duty — routing and protecting wires from mechanical damage
  • Used for individual circuits or a few cables, providing the highest protection level of the four systems
  • Common in exposed or concealed installations, from walls and ceilings to underground and equipment connections
  • Sized on the number of wires and a fill rule — max 40% for PVC, 40–50% for GI conduit

Where each one is actually used

Tray — MDB/SMDB Rooms, Shafts
Ladder — Substations, Generators
Trunking — Offices, Hospitals, Schools
Conduit — Lighting, Socket Circuits

Installation stages

1First fix (rough-in): route planning and support fixing, tray/ladder/trunking/conduit installed and laid before plaster, with pull boxes and earthing in place.
2Second fix (preparation): cable pulling through the installed system, termination at DBs and panels, tightness and connection checks.
3Third fix (final): device installation — switches, sockets, lights — final connections, testing, commissioning, and handover.

How to select

RequirementSelection
Many cables, large & heavyCable Ladder
Many cables, not particularly heavyCable Tray
Few cables, need enclosed protectionCable Trunking
Few cables, no enclosure neededConduit

The decision tree is really just two questions: how many cables, and how much protection does the run need. Many cables push toward tray or ladder depending on weight; few cables push toward trunking or conduit depending on whether enclosure and appearance matter.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cable tray and ladder are open systems for multiple cables; trunking and conduit are enclosed systems trading visibility for protection.
  • ✓Cable ladder is the mechanical-strength option — it exists specifically for large, heavy power cables tray can't support.
  • ✓Conduit offers the highest protection level of the four, which is why it's the default for individual circuits and exposed wiring.
  • ✓Always follow project specifications and relevant standards (IEC/BS/NEC) rather than a single default choice.
  • ✓Don't mix power and communication cables in the same system unless specifically permitted, and provide earth continuity for all metal trays and conduits.
