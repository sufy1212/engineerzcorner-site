P&ID (Piping & Instrumentation Diagram) Explained

A P&ID is the drawing every process plant actually runs on — more detailed than a flow diagram, less abstract than a spec sheet. Here's how to read one: equipment, piping, valves, instrument tags, and the signal lines connecting them.

More detail than a flow diagram, less than a spec sheet

A Process Flow Diagram tells you what a process does — major equipment, main streams, overall material balance. A P&ID tells you how it's actually built and controlled: every vessel, every line size and material, every valve, every instrument, and every signal connecting them into control loops. It's the drawing operators, maintenance techs, and engineers all reference on the same terms, which is exactly why it has to be unambiguous. A missing valve or a mislabeled signal line on a P&ID isn't a cosmetic error — it's a drawing that no longer matches the plant.

Reading one comes down to three layers stacked on top of each other: the equipment fluid actually moves through, the piping and valves that route and isolate it, and the instrumentation and signal lines that measure and control it. Once those three layers are separated in your head, even a dense P&ID stops being intimidating.

1Equipment

  • Vessels, tanks, pumps, heat exchangers, reactors — drawn roughly to shape, each with its own equipment tag (like T-101 or P-101)
  • Shown with real nozzles and connections, not just a symbol — every inlet, outlet, vent, and drain the equipment actually has
  • Tag prefixes are consistent across a project: T for tanks/vessels, P for pumps, E for exchangers, C for compressors, and so on

2Piping

  • Solid lines are the process itself — the actual fluid path, drawn with an arrow showing flow direction
  • A line number strung along the pipe (e.g. 2"-CS-150#) encodes size, material, and pressure class in one code
  • Utility lines — steam, cooling water, instrument air — are drawn the same way but distinguished by their own tag prefix, since they're still process piping, just not the main product stream

3Valves

  • Manual valves (bowtie symbol) isolate, throttle by hand, or protect — gate, globe, ball, and check valves each get their own symbol
  • Control valves add an actuator symbol on top of the valve body, since they're driven by a signal, not a handwheel
  • Relief and safety valves get a distinct symbol too — they're the last line of defense against overpressure, not a routine isolation point

4Instrumentation

  • Circles are the standard ISA symbol for a field instrument — a bare circle is local, a circle in a box is behind a panel or in the control system
  • Letters inside identify what it does: PT (pressure transmitter), FT (flow transmitter), TT (temperature transmitter), LT (level transmitter), and their controller/indicator counterparts (PC, FC, TC, LC)
  • Final control elements — usually a control valve — carry the loop's number too, tying the whole measure-decide-act chain together visually

5Signal Lines

  • Dashed lines carry information, not fluid — electronic signals, pneumatic tubing, or a data link between instruments
  • Following a dashed line from a transmitter to a controller to a control valve traces one complete control loop, end to end
  • Mixing up a signal line for a process line (or the reverse) is one of the fastest ways to misread a P&ID — the line style is the whole distinction

How an instrument tag is built

Instrument tags aren't arbitrary labels — they follow a fixed ISA structure so anyone trained on the standard can read one cold, on any project. Take PT-101: the letters come first and identify function, then a number identifies which system or unit the loop belongs to, then a sequence number tells loops in that unit apart. PT-101 and PT-102 are both pressure transmitters in the same unit, just different loops.

PrefixFunction
PT / PIPressure transmitter / indicator
TT / TITemperature transmitter / indicator
FT / FIFlow transmitter / indicator
LT / LILevel transmitter / indicator
PC / FC / TC / LCController for the matching variable
CVControl valve (the loop's final control element)

Tracing a control loop on the page

1A transmitter (say FT-101) measures a process variable — flow, in this case — and turns it into a signal.
2A dashed signal line carries that measurement to the DCS or PLC, drawn as a shared control-system block above the loop.
3The control logic compares the measurement to a setpoint and calculates an output — still inside that same dashed-line world.
4Another dashed line carries the output signal down to the final control element, usually a control valve tagged with the same loop number.
5The valve acts on the actual process line — the solid pipe — closing the loop between measurement and action.

PFD vs. P&ID

These two get confused constantly because they cover the same process, just at different resolutions. A PFD is drawn early, to establish the overall process and material balance; a P&ID is drawn (and continually updated) for construction, operation, and troubleshooting, and carries far more detail on every line.

PFDP&ID
Major equipment and main streams onlyEvery piece of equipment, every line, every valve
No instrumentation or control loops shownFull instrumentation, tags, and control loops shown
Used for early process design and mass balanceUsed for construction, operation, and troubleshooting
Rarely revised once a process is setA living document, updated through the plant's life

Why this level of detail earns its keep

Standardized Tags
Traceable Loops
One Shared Reference

Because every symbol and tag follows the same conventions project to project, a P&ID transfers knowledge across teams that never talk to each other directly — a design engineer, a construction crew, and an operator years later can all read the same drawing the same way. That's the whole point of standardizing something as basic-looking as a circle with two letters in it: it removes ambiguity from a document that safety, operations, and maintenance all depend on being exactly right.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Separate a P&ID into three layers — equipment, piping/valves, instrumentation/signals — and dense drawings stop being intimidating.
  • ✓Instrument tags follow a fixed structure: function letters, then unit number, then loop sequence — not arbitrary labels.
  • ✓Solid lines are process piping; dashed lines are signals. That distinction alone resolves most misreads.
  • ✓A PFD shows what a process does; a P&ID shows everything needed to build, operate, and troubleshoot it.
  • ✓A P&ID is a living document — it gets revised through the life of the plant, not just drawn once at design time.
