Pipe Routing Guide: 13 Rules Every Layout Should Follow

A well-routed pipe saves cost, time, and trouble — and most routing mistakes trace back to one of a small set of recurring rules getting skipped under schedule pressure.

1. Pipe routing principles

The baseline rules every routing decision gets checked against.

  • ✓Shortest, direct & practical path
  • ✓Minimum number of bends
  • ✓Avoid high points and dead legs
  • ✓Allow for thermal expansion
  • ✓Provide proper support
  • ✓Ensure accessibility & maintainability
  • ✓Follow code and client standards
These principles routinely conflict with each other in practice — the shortest path with the fewest bends isn't automatically the one that allows enough flexibility for thermal expansion. Routing is where those trade-offs get resolved line by line, not a checklist to satisfy all at once.

2. How to route a pump suction line

Avoid air pockets, ensure smooth flow into the pump.

  • ✓Short, straight & downward slope towards the pump
  • ✓Minimum fittings & bends
  • ✓Maintain NPSH — avoid high points
  • ✓Proper support near the pump
  • ✓No reducer directly at the pump suction

3. Pipe routing around equipment

Clearance and access matter as much as the pipe path itself.

  • ✓Provide sufficient clearance around the equipment
  • ✓Avoid nozzle loads from pipe weight or thermal movement
  • ✓Consider maintenance space for the equipment itself
  • ✓Keep access clear to drains and instruments

4. Pipe rack design basics

A well-designed rack means safe, easy access for the life of the plant.

  • ✓Proper height from grade
  • ✓Adequate structural strength & stability
  • ✓Allow for future expansion
  • ✓Proper spacing between pipes
  • ✓Provide ladder, platform & handrail where needed

5. Pipe supports explained

Right support = long pipe life. Selected and placed per code (ASME B31.3).

Shoe Support
Bears pipe weight
A welded saddle carrying the pipe on a beam or rack member, transferring weight straight down while letting the pipe slide axially as it moves.
Guide Support
Controls sideways movement
Restrains the pipe laterally while still allowing it to move axially, keeping long straight runs from wandering sideways under thermal expansion.
Anchor Support
Fixed reference point
Locks the pipe completely at that point — no axial or lateral movement — used to establish a fixed point that thermal expansion is designed to move away from.
Spring Support
Allows vertical thermal movement
A spring-loaded hanger that keeps a near-constant supporting force as the pipe moves up or down thermally — used where a rigid support would overload the pipe or nozzle during expansion.

All four are placed to: support pipe weight, control movement & vibration, allow thermal expansion, and satisfy code — typically ASME B31.3.

6. Dead legs in piping

A dead leg is a branch or section of pipe with no continuous flow through it — typically an unused connection, a spare nozzle, or an isolated instrument tapping. Dead legs cause fluid stagnation, which can lead to corrosion or blockage over time. Keep the dead leg length (L, measured from the main line to the first valve or cap) as short as possible, and provide a drain or remove the dead leg entirely wherever the process allows.

ServiceTypical limit (ASME B31.3)
Liquid serviceL ≤ 3 × NPS
Gas serviceL ≤ 6 × NPS

7. Expansion loop design

Flexibility prevents stress.

  • ✓Absorbs the thermal expansion of a long straight run
  • ✓Common forms: U-loop, L-shape, Z-shape, or offset loop
  • ✓Provide proper guides and anchors on either side of the loop
  • ✓Consider available flexibility and physical space when choosing the loop shape

8. Hot line vs cold line routing

Always account for the temperature effect on material and movement.

  • ✓Follow standard color coding for each utility line
  • ✓Route for reliability and easy access
  • ✓Keep proper separation from process lines
  • ✓Minimize crossings between utility lines

11. Nozzle orientation rules

Correct orientation = efficient, reliable operation.

  • ✓Top: vapor outlet — preferred orientation for venting gas
  • ✓Side: liquid inlet connection
  • ✓Bottom: drain outlet only — avoid routing a general process connection here

12. Flange accessibility rules

Access today, easy maintenance tomorrow.

  • ✓Provide clear space to open and re-tighten the flange
  • ✓Allow room for tool movement around the bolt circle
  • ✓Don't block flange access with structure or other pipes
  • ✓Minimum 100 mm clearance is a common starting reference — confirm against project spec

13. Maintenance clearance requirements

Maintenance access has to be designed in, not squeezed in after the fact — these are typical starting clearances; always confirm against the equipment manufacturer's data and the project's piping spec.

General notes

  • ✓Follow code — ASME B31.3 or the applicable client standard
  • ✓Coordinate routing with other disciplines (process, structural, electrical, instrumentation)
  • ✓Consider construction and erection sequence, not just the finished layout
  • ✓Think maintenance — not just initial design
Every design contractor's own piping and equipment layout spec sits on top of these general principles, and interfaces with process, stress, and other disciplines. Treat this guide as the shared foundation those project-specific specs are built from — always confirm against the actual project spec before finalizing a routing decision.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Routing principles routinely conflict — minimizing bends and allowing for thermal expansion pull against each other, and resolving that trade-off is the actual job, not a rule to apply blindly.
  • ✓Pump suction lines are the least forgiving of the bunch — short, straight, sloped, and free of high points to protect NPSH.
  • ✓Dead legs have hard code limits for a reason — stagnant fluid is a real corrosion and contamination risk, not just an inefficiency.
  • ✓Maintenance clearance is a routing decision made at design time, not something to solve after the layout is otherwise finished.
