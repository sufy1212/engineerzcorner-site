SEER, EER and COP Explained: Reading Cooling Efficiency Ratings Correctly

COP, EER and SEER all measure cooling per unit of electricity, but with different units and test conditions. Here is how they connect, where the conversion is exact, and where it is only an estimate.

A new air conditioner or heat pump quotes an efficiency number, but which one depends on where the equipment is sold and what it does. COP, EER and SEER all answer the same question, how much cooling you get for each unit of electricity, yet they use different units and different test conditions. Mixing them up leads to wrong comparisons and, occasionally, wrong purchases.

Three yardsticks for one ratio

COP and EER: one operating point

COP is cooling output divided by electrical input, with both in the same units, so it has no unit. EER is the same ratio with output in BTU/h and input in watts. Both describe performance at a single fixed test condition, typically full load on a hot day.

SEER: a whole season

SEER divides the total cooling delivered over a season by the total electricity used, across a range of outdoor temperatures and part-load conditions. Equipment spends most hours at milder, part-load conditions, so SEER comes out higher than EER.

A higher COP or EER, or a lower kW/ton, always means better efficiency. For example, a unit with a COP of 3.5 has an EER of about 11.9 and uses roughly 1.0 kW per ton of cooling.

SEER has no exact formula

Because SEER averages performance over many conditions, there is no exact way to convert between it and EER. Rules of thumb put SEER at roughly 1.1 to 1.2 times EER for typical residential equipment, and converters use values in that range. The SEER / EER / COP converter on this site uses 1.2 and labels the result as an approximation. Use the manufacturer's published figures for any real comparison, and treat a converted SEER as a rough guide only.

SEER2: a tougher test, not worse equipment

In the United States, the Department of Energy replaced the older test procedure with the M1 procedure on January 1, 2023, giving the SEER2, EER2 and HSPF2 ratings. The main change is the external static pressure used during testing, raised from 0.1 to 0.5 inches of water column for ducted equipment, which is closer to the resistance real ductwork creates. Because the test is harder, the numbers are lower for the same hardware.

Applying the split-system factor, a 16 SEER unit is roughly 15.2 SEER2 and a 14 SEER unit about 13.3 SEER2, with no change in how the equipment performs. When you compare products, make sure both are quoted on the same scale. Federal minimums are also set in SEER2 and vary by region of the US, for example 13.4 SEER2 for split air conditioners in the northern region and 14.3 SEER2 in the southeast and southwest for units under 45,000 BTU/h.

Which number should you use?

  • Peak load and hot-day performance: EER, or COP at the design condition, shows how the unit performs when it is working hardest.
  • Annual energy and running cost: SEER2 (or HSPF2 for heating) reflects a typical season, so it is the better guide to the bill.
  • Chillers and large plant: COP or kW/ton at full load, plus a part-load figure, since chillers spend most hours below full load.
  • Heat pumps in heating mode: COP or HSPF2. A heat pump's COP includes the heat it moves, so values above 1 are normal.

Whatever you choose, compare like with like: the same metric, the same test standard and the same capacity. Read the efficiency from the manufacturer's rating certificate rather than from a brochure headline.

Key takeaways

  • ✓COP to EER is exact (× 3.412). Anything that involves SEER is an approximation.
  • ✓SEER2 is not a drop in performance. It is the same equipment measured at higher duct static pressure.
  • ✓Use EER or COP for peak behaviour and SEER2 for annual energy cost.
  • ✓Compare products on the same metric and the same test standard, taken from the rating certificate.
