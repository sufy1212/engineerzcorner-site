Pre-Commissioning vs Commissioning: What's the Difference

Both words get used loosely on site, but they check different things at different points in a project. One happens with the system dead. The other happens with it live — and you don't get to the second without passing the first.

Two phases, one switch between them

Pre-commissioning and commissioning get treated as interchangeable words on a lot of sites, but they sit on opposite sides of a single, very specific moment: the point where a system is first energized. Everything before that point — while the panel, cable, or equipment is still dead — is pre-commissioning. Everything after it — once power is actually applied — is commissioning.

That distinction isn't just semantics. Pre-commissioning exists to catch a fault before it can hurt anyone or anything, using tests that don't require the system to be live. Commissioning exists to prove the system does what it was designed to do, and by definition that can only be checked once it's running. Confusing the two — or worse, skipping the first to get to the second faster — is how a wiring fault ends up discovered by a breaker trip instead of a Megger.

Pre-commissioning: before power is applied

Pre-commissioning is a readiness and safety exercise. It checks that what was actually installed matches what was designed and specified, that every connection is sound, and that nothing about the installation itself would make energizing it dangerous.

Why the order can't be reversed

Commissioning tests only mean something if the thing being tested was already confirmed sound. Running a load test on a panel that never had its terminals torqued or its insulation Meggered isn't thorough commissioning — it's an uncontrolled way of finding a pre-commissioning defect, except now the system is energized when it happens. That's the real reason the sequence is fixed: pre-commissioning removes the failure modes that shouldn't exist before the system is ever put under load, so that whatever commissioning finds is genuinely a performance question, not a build-quality one.

This is also where a Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) and Site Acceptance Test (SAT) fit in. A FAT is pre-commissioning done at the factory, against the equipment alone, before it ever ships. Pre-commissioning on site repeats and extends that verification after installation and cable termination — catching anything transit, handling, or field wiring introduced. Only once that passes does commissioning, and eventually a SAT, take the system live.

Pre-CommissioningFocuses on readiness, safety and verification before power is ever applied.
CommissioningFocuses on performance, functionality and reliability once the system is live.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Pre-commissioning is everything checked while the system is dead; commissioning is everything checked once it's live.
  • ✓Pre-commissioning protects people and equipment from an undiscovered installation fault at the moment of energization.
  • ✓Commissioning can only validate real design intent — functional response, protection, load behavior — once power is actually flowing.
  • ✓The sequence is fixed for a reason: skipping pre-commissioning turns a safe offline check into an uncontrolled live one.
