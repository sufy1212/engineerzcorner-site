RFIs and the RFI Log: Raising, Tracking and Closing Requests for Information

Drawings are never complete. Here is when to ask, what a well-formed RFI looks like, how it should be tracked to closure, and the mistakes that turn a simple question into rework.

Raise an RFI for missing, unclear or conflicting information, not for substitutions or scope changes.

A good RFI has a number, a reference, one clear question, a proposed solution and an impact note.

No set of drawings and specifications is complete. When the documents are silent, unclear or contradictory, the contractor has two options: guess and risk rework, or ask. A Request for Information, or RFI, is the formal way to ask, and an RFI log is how a project keeps track of what has been asked, who owes an answer and what is holding up work.

When an RFI is the right tool

Raise an RFI when

A dimension or detail is missing, a drawing disagrees with the specification or another drawing, disciplines clash on site, or an existing condition differs from what the documents show.

Do not raise one when

The answer is already in the documents, you want to propose a substitution, or you want to change scope. Those have their own routes: a submittal, a variation request or a change order.

What a good RFI contains

  • A unique number and the date, so every later conversation can point to exactly one request.
  • Who is asking and who must answer, since an RFI sent to the wrong person just ages.
  • The reference, meaning the drawing number and detail or the specification clause the question is about.
  • One clear question. A bundle of questions tends to get a partial answer and stays open.
  • A proposed solution, which lets the designer answer with a yes or a correction and speeds the reply.
  • An impact note, saying whether the answer could affect cost or schedule, and the date by which the answer is needed.

The life of an RFI

An RFI is Open once it is issued and Answered when the reply is received. It is Closed only when the drawings or the work have been updated to match the reply. Keeping Answered and Closed separate catches a common failure, where a reply arrives, nobody revises the drawing, and the old detail is built. An RFI that passes its response date while still open is Overdue. Many contracts set a response window, but the length varies, so use the one in your own contract and record the due date for each item.

Where RFIs go wrong

  • Asking too late. An RFI raised the day the work is due leaves no time to answer. Raise it when the gap is first noticed.
  • No follow-through. An overdue RFI is a schedule risk, so raise it at the next progress meeting and record it in the meeting minutes.
  • Answers that change scope. If the reply adds work, changes a material or moves a date, it is no longer just clarification. Assess it as a variation using the change order impact calculator, and read change order cost and schedule impact.
  • Answers given verbally. If it is not written and logged, it did not happen. Insist on a written reply.
  • Using the log as a score. A high count of RFIs may reflect weak documents, not a difficult contractor. Look at the causes before drawing conclusions.

Late answers are also a risk worth tracking, so link significant RFIs to the risk register. Start a log with the RFI log tool.

Key takeaways

  • ✓One question per RFI, with a drawing or clause reference and a proposed answer.
  • ✓Record the response due date from your contract and chase overdue items in progress meetings.
  • ✓Close an RFI only when the drawings or work reflect the answer.
  • ✓If an answer affects cost or time, route it into change control and the risk register.
