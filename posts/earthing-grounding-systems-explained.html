Earthing & Grounding Systems Explained: Why Resistance Is the Whole Game

An earth grid isn't a formality on the drawing — it's the path fault current has to take instead of through a person. Here's what it's actually doing, why one number decides whether it works, and what drives that number up or down on a real site.

What earthing is actually for

Every earthing system exists to do one job: give fault current a deliberate, low-resistance path to the ground, so that it flows there instead of through whatever it happens to touch — a metal enclosure, a conduit run, or a person standing nearby. When insulation fails and a live conductor touches an exposed metal part, that metal part should already be bonded to earth. If the earth path has low enough resistance, enough current flows through it to trip the breaker or fuse almost instantly, clearing the fault before the touch voltage on that metal ever becomes dangerous.

Without a low-resistance earth path, the same fault can leave a metal enclosure sitting at a hazardous voltage for a long time — sometimes indefinitely, since the fault current may be too small to trip anything at all.

The one number that decides everything

Earth resistance (Rg), not just having an electrode in the ground

Driving a rod into the soil doesn't automatically make a system "grounded" in any useful sense. What matters is the resistance between that electrode (or grid) and the surrounding earth, measured in ohms. Lower resistance means more fault current flows through the earth path for a given fault voltage, which is exactly what makes protective devices trip fast. Most site and equipment standards set a maximum allowable earth resistance for this reason — commonly in the 1–5 ohm range for substations and sensitive equipment, and up to around 25 ohms for general-purpose rod electrodes, though the exact limit depends on the applicable code and the protection scheme in use.

What actually drives resistance up or down

FactorEffect on earth resistance

Corroded or loose electrode connectionsIncreases resistance

Grid vs. rod: not the same tool

Rod electrodesSimple, cheap, fine for smaller loads and single-building setups. Performance is very sensitive to local soil conditions right at that one point.
Grid/mesh systemsMultiple interconnected electrodes across a wider area — used for substations and larger installations, where even step and touch voltage across the site need to stay controlled, not just overall resistance.

Getting it right on site

  • 1Test soil resistivity before finalizing electrode design — don't assume a rod count from a generic table without checking your actual site.
  • 2Space multiple rods at least their own length apart, ideally more, to avoid overlapping resistance zones.
  • 3Keep all bonding connections mechanically tight and corrosion-protected — a good electrode with a bad connection still fails.
  • 4Re-test earth resistance periodically, especially after long dry seasons, since soil moisture swings the number more than most people expect.
  • 5Coordinate the earthing design with the actual protective device trip settings — a resistance target only means something in the context of what it needs to make trip.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Earthing's job is routing fault current away from people and equipment, fast enough for protection to act.
  • ✓Earth resistance, not electrode presence, is the number that actually determines whether that happens.
  • ✓Soil conditions usually matter more than electrode material or size.
  • ✓Grounding systems need periodic re-testing — they can drift out of spec with the seasons.
