Tank Volume vs Level Explained

A horizontal tank that reads 10% on its level transmitter holds about 5% of its volume, and one that reads 90% holds almost 95%. The tank heads add more on top. Here is the geometry behind both effects.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Process
01
In a vertical tank, level and volume are proportional. In a horizontal tank they are not: the round cross-section is narrow at the bottom and top.
02
Shell volume at level h is the circular segment area times the length: A = R²(θ − sinθ)/2, with θ = 2 acos((R − h)/R).
03
Dished heads add volume that flat-head calculations leave out. A 2:1 elliptical head holds πD³/24, a hemispherical head πD³/12.
04
Alarm and trip levels set as a percentage of level correspond to much smaller volumes near the bottom, which matters for pump suction and holdup.

Vertical is linear, horizontal is not

For a vertical cylinder the cross-section is constant, so V = πR²h and 50% of the level is 50% of the volume. In a horizontal cylinder the liquid surface width changes with height: very narrow near the bottom, widest at the centerline, narrow again near the top. A given rise in level therefore adds little volume at the extremes and a lot around the middle.

Level (% of diameter)Shell volume (% of full)
10%5.2%
25%19.6%
50%50.0%
75%80.4%
90%94.8%

These are for the cylindrical shell only. By symmetry 50% level is exactly 50% volume. Because the curve is symmetric, the volume at 90% level is 100% minus the volume at 10%.

The shell formula

1Get the half-angle from the level: θ = 2 × acos((R − h) / R), in radians, where h is the liquid depth from the bottom and R the inside radius.
2Circular segment area: A = R² (θ − sinθ) / 2.
3Shell liquid volume: V = A × L, with L the length of the cylindrical section.

Use the inside diameter, and include the straight flange of each head in L when the heads have one. This is the calculation your vessel volume and level calculator performs, with flat heads assumed.

What the heads add

Head typeVolume of one headDepth
2:1 semi-ellipticalπ D³ / 24 ≈ 0.1309 D³D / 4
Hemisphericalπ D³ / 12 ≈ 0.2618 D³D / 2
ASME flanged and dished (100% crown, 6% knuckle)≈ 0.0809 D³Shallower than 2:1
FlatNone—

These are volumes inside the head, excluding any straight flange. Two 2:1 heads together make one full ellipsoid. That gives a neat result for the partially filled case: the liquid in a pair of heads at level h is the full ellipsoid volume times the fraction of a sphere filled to that depth, h²(3R − h) / (4R³). The same factor applies to hemispherical heads. A torispherical head is flatter than an ellipsoid, so using the elliptical formula for its partial volume is only an approximation.

Worked example

Horizontal tank, inside diameter 2 m, shell length 6 m, liquid depth 0.5 m (25% level), with 2:1 elliptical heads.

StepWorkingResult
Half-angleθ = 2 acos(0.5 / 1)2.0944 rad
Segment area1² × (2.0944 − 0.8660) / 20.6142 m²
Shell liquid volume0.6142 × 63.685 m³
Heads full (both)π × 2³ / 122.094 m³
Sphere fraction at h = 0.50.5² × (3 − 0.5) / (4 × 1³)0.1562
Liquid in the heads2.094 × 0.15620.327 m³
Total liquid volume3.685 + 0.3274.012 m³
Total tank volumeπ × 1² × 6 + 2.09420.944 m³

Ignoring the heads gives 3.685 m³, which is 8.2% less liquid than the true 4.012 m³. The heads hold 10.0% of the full capacity of this fairly short tank (shell length three times the diameter), so the longer the shell compared with the diameter, the smaller the head effect. For the other head types the full volume of both heads on the same 2 m tank is 4.19 m³ for hemispherical and 1.29 m³ for ASME flanged and dished, against 2.09 m³ for 2:1 elliptical.

Why it matters in the plant

Level transmitters measure level, so alarm and trip points are set in percent of range, but operators and process calculations need volume. In a horizontal drum, a low-level alarm at 10% corresponds to only about 5% of the shell volume, and a high-level alarm at 90% leaves about 5% of the volume above it. Residence time, holdup for pump suction and available surge volume must all be calculated from volume, which is why a strapping table or the formula, not a straight-line scale, is used to convert.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Scaling volume linearly with level in a horizontal tankOver- or under-states the volume at the ends of the range by a large factor
Using outside diameterVolume is based on the inside diameter, and wall thickness shifts the result
Forgetting the headsOn a short tank the heads can be around a tenth of the capacity
Leaving out the straight flangeThe head volume formulas exclude it, so add it to the shell length
Measuring h from the wrong datumLevel must be from the bottom inside surface of the shell, not the nozzle or the tangent line
Applying elliptical formulas exactly to a torispherical headIt is flatter, so the partial volume is only approximated

Key takeaways

  • ✓Vertical tanks are linear in level; horizontal tanks are not. 10% level is about 5% volume, 25% is about 20%, and 50% is exactly 50%.
  • ✓Shell volume = A × L with A = R²(θ − sinθ)/2 and θ = 2 acos((R − h)/R).
  • ✓Head volumes: 2:1 elliptical πD³/24, hemispherical πD³/12, ASME flanged and dished about 0.0809 D³, each excluding the straight flange.
  • ✓Add the liquid in the heads, which is the full head volume times h²(3R − h)/(4R³) for a pair of elliptical or hemispherical heads.
  • ✓Check dimensions against the vessel datasheet; use the vendor strapping table where one exists.
