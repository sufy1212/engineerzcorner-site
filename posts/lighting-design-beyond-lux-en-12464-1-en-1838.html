Lighting Design Beyond Lux

A luminaire count that hits the target average lux can still fail the room: too dark at the edges, glaring on screens, or dark when the power drops. The lumen method answers one question; these are the other three.

Engineerz CorneRReference · EN 12464-1 / EN 1838
01
The target lux comes from the task. EN 12464-1 sets maintained illuminance together with uniformity (Uo), glare (UGR) and colour rendering (Ra), not lux alone.
02
Utilization factor is not a free number. It depends on the room index K, which comes from room size and the mounting height above the work plane.
03
Count is not layout. Luminaires must be spaced within the manufacturer's spacing-to-height ratio, and an awkward count (such as 5 in a 6 × 5 m room) usually becomes the next even grid.
04
Emergency lighting (EN 1838) is a separate design with its own levels, response times and duration. The general lighting does not cover it.

Start from the task, not a round number

The lumen method needs a target illuminance, and a typical mistake is picking 300 lux for a room because the example used 300. EN 12464-1 sets requirements per space and task. Each row gives a maintained illuminance (the value that must still be met at the end of the maintenance cycle, which is why the maintenance factor is in the formula), an overall uniformity Uo (minimum divided by average illuminance on the task area), a colour rendering index Ra and a maximum unified glare rating UGR. Same room, different answer: a 6 × 5 m room at 300 lux needs 5 panels of 3,600 lm (UF 0.7, MF 0.8). The same room as an office at 500 lux needs 500 × 30 / 2016 = 7.44, so 8 panels, giving about 538 lux.

Room index and utilization factor

UF comes from the luminaire manufacturer's utilization table, entered with the room index K and the surface reflectances (ceiling, walls, floor). The room index describes how deep or squat the space is as seen from the luminaires:

K = (L × W) / (Hm × (L + W))
Hm = mounting height above the work plane, not above the floor

Worked example: ceiling 3 m, work plane 0.85 m, so Hm = 2.15 m. Room 6 × 5 m: K = 30 / (2.15 × 11) = 1.27. A low K (small, tall room) loses more light to the walls, so UF is lower. Many infographics assume UF = 0.7 for everything; at K around 1.3 with mid-reflectance surfaces a lower value is common, and the correct one comes from the product's table. If UF were 0.6 instead of 0.7 in this room, an office would need 500 × 30 / (3600 × 0.6 × 0.8) = 8.7, so 9 panels.

From count to layout

The count is only a number until it is placed on a grid. A symmetrical layout for a rectangular room uses n rows by m columns, so 5 luminaires in a 6 × 5 m room does not work evenly; 6 (3 × 2) or 8 (4 × 2) do. Two checks follow.

  • ✓Spacing-to-height ratio. Spacing S between luminaire centres must not exceed the maximum S/Hm stated for the luminaire, or the illuminance between fittings drops and Uo fails. The limit is a manufacturer figure, not a universal constant.
  • ✓Distance to walls. A common starting point is about half the luminaire spacing from the wall to the first row, reduced if work is done at the walls.

Average lux is E = N × F × UF × MF / A. Only the 8-panel layout meets the 500 lux office target. Whether 2.5 m across is acceptable depends on the panel's published S/Hm limit.

Glare and colour rendering

UGR rates discomfort glare from the luminaires as seen in a standard observer position, and the schedule gives a limit, for example 19 for office and screen work and 16 for fine drawing work. It depends on the luminaire's luminance, the layout and the room surfaces, so it comes from the manufacturer's UGR table for the room, not from the lumen method. Ra is a minimum colour rendering index; 80 is the common requirement for occupied rooms, and 40 is accepted in corridors.

Emergency lighting is a separate design

EN 1838 defines emergency escape lighting in three parts: escape routes, open (anti-panic) areas and high-risk task areas. The figures below are the commonly quoted values; confirm against the edition your project references.

Escape routesAt least 1 lux on the floor along the centre line of a route up to 2 m wide, with the central band at least half the route width at 50% of that value.
Open areasAt least 0.5 lux on the floor of the core area, excluding a 0.5 m border, for rooms over about 60 m² without a defined route.
High-risk tasksEnough light to shut a process down safely, with uniformity not below 0.1. The duration is tied to how long people are exposed to the risk.
  • ✓Response: 50% of the required level within 5 seconds of supply failure and 100% within 60 seconds.
  • ✓Duration: at least 1 hour for escape lighting; some regulations and building types require longer.
  • ✓Uniformity on the route: maximum to minimum illuminance on the centre line of no more than 40:1.
Check the edition: at least one guide to the 2025 revision of EN 1838 reports that escape route illuminance is now required across the full route width, not only on the centre line. If the project predates it, confirm which edition the specification calls up.

Power density

Once the layout is fixed, connected load per area is a quick sanity check for the electrical design. Illustrative case: an LED panel of 3,600 lm at an assumed 100 lm/W draws 36 W. Eight panels is 288 W over 30 m², or 9.6 W/m²; six panels is 7.2 W/m². Use the real luminaire wattage from the datasheet, and check the result against any lighting power limit in the energy code that applies to the project.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Pick the lux target from the task (500 for an office, 100 for a corridor), then check Uo, UGR and Ra, not only average lux.
  • ✓Get UF from the room index K and the manufacturer's table; do not carry 0.7 from one room to another.
  • ✓Round the count to a layout that fits the room, then check spacing against the S/Hm limit and recompute the achieved lux.
  • ✓Design emergency lighting separately to EN 1838: 1 lux on routes, 0.5 lux in open areas, 5 s and 60 s response, at least 1 hour.
  • ✓Examples are screening estimates. Use manufacturer photometric data and the standard editions in force for the final design.
