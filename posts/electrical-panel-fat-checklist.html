Electrical Panel Factory Acceptance Test (FAT): The Complete Checklist

Before an LV panel ever leaves the factory floor, it has to prove itself across eight stages — from a tape measure against the GA drawing to a Megger against the busbars. Here's what each stage actually checks, and why.

What a panel FAT is actually verifying

A Factory Acceptance Test is the point where a switchboard, having just come off the build line, is checked against the documents that defined it — the general arrangement drawing, the schematic, the specification — rather than against a generic "does it look okay" standard. It's normally witnessed by the client, their consultant, or a third-party inspector, and it's the last realistic point to catch a wiring error, an undersized busbar, or a mis-set relay before the panel is packed, shipped, and installed somewhere far less convenient to fix it.

The eight stages below follow the order most FAT procedures use in practice: inspection first, then insulation and dielectric tests, then function, then protection and interlocks, and documentation last as the record that ties it all together.

The eight stages

1
Visual Inspection
  • Panel dimensions verified against the GA drawing
  • Paint finish, IP rating and construction checked
  • Nameplates, labels and warning signs confirmed
  • Component make, model and ratings matched to spec
  • Cable routing, wire ferrules and general neatness
2
Mechanical Inspection
  • Door alignment, locking and hinges checked
  • All terminals, busbar and cable connections tightened
  • Earthing continuity and earth bond verified
  • Busbar supports, insulation and clearance checked
  • Terminal torque verified against specification
3
Insulation Resistance Test
  • IR test with Megger, phase-to-phase
  • IR test with Megger, phase-to-earth
  • IR test with Megger on auxiliary circuits
  • Result accepted against IEC or client specification
4
High Voltage (Hi-Pot) Test
  • Dielectric withstand test run per specification
  • Tested phase-to-phase
  • Tested phase-to-earth
  • No breakdown, and leakage current stays within limit
5
Functional Test
  • MCCB and MCB operation verified
  • Contactors, relays, timers and overload relays tested
  • Push buttons and selector switches checked
  • Indicator lamps, meters and buzzer verified
  • Wiring checked against schematic and terminal numbers
6
Protection Test
  • Overload relay settings and trip tested
  • Short-circuit protection verified on MCCB/MCB
  • Earth fault protection checked, where applicable
  • Trip time and current settings verified
  • All readings and observations recorded
7
Control & Interlock Test
  • Emergency Stop (E-Stop) tested
  • Interlocks and permissives verified
  • PLC / controller I/O checked
  • Alarms and indications simulated
  • Communication (Modbus/BACnet/Ethernet) verified
  • VFD operation checked, where applicable
8
Documentation Review
  • General Arrangement (GA) drawing
  • Power and control schematic
  • Bill of Materials (BOM)
  • Component datasheets
  • Test certificates for major components
  • FAT report (as-built) and calibration certificates

Why insulation resistance and hi-pot are separate tests

These two get mixed up, but they're checking different things. The insulation resistance test uses a Megger to apply a modest DC voltage and measure the resistance of the insulation — a low reading flags moisture, contamination, or damaged insulation before anything is energized. The hi-pot (dielectric withstand) test goes further: it applies a much higher voltage, at the level specified for the panel's rating, specifically to confirm the insulation can withstand a transient overvoltage without breaking down. IR testing is a health check; hi-pot is a stress test, and a panel needs to pass both.

Insulation Resistance (IR)Megger test at low DC voltage. Flags moisture, contamination, and degraded insulation. Result is a resistance value (MΩ) against a minimum threshold.
Hi-Pot / Dielectric WithstandHigh-voltage stress test per spec, phase-to-phase and phase-to-earth. Pass means no breakdown and leakage current stays within the allowed limit.

FAT acceptance criteria

  • ✓Panel complies with the approved drawings and specifications.
  • ✓All wiring is correct and properly identified.
  • ✓No insulation or continuity failures recorded.
  • ✓All protection and control functions operate correctly.
  • ✓No physical damage or loose connections found.

Only once every stage above has a recorded pass, and the criteria above are met, does the FAT close out — typically with a signed FAT report marking the panel as approved for dispatch.

A FAT and a SAT check overlapping ground but at different points: the FAT confirms the panel was built correctly, in a controlled factory environment, before it ever moves. The SAT re-verifies functionality after installation, cable termination, and integration on site — catching anything that transit, site conditions, or field wiring introduced that the factory test couldn't have seen.

Key takeaways

  • ✓FAT is a witnessed, documented verification against drawings and spec — not an informal look-over.
  • ✓Eight stages run in sequence: visual, mechanical, insulation resistance, hi-pot, functional, protection, control/interlock, and documentation.
  • ✓IR testing and hi-pot testing are distinct checks — a health check and a stress test — and both need a recorded pass.
  • ✓FAT precedes dispatch; SAT repeats the verification after site installation, before commissioning and handover.
