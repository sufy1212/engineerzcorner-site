Earth Fault Loop Impedance Explained: Why the Breaker Must Trip Fast Enough

Earthing only protects people if the fault current is large enough to trip the device in time. Here is how Zs sets that current, the disconnection times, a worked 32 A example, the 0.8 rule and how cable length changes the answer.

The point of earthing a circuit is not the earth itself. It is to make sure that when a live conductor touches an exposed metal part, enough current flows to trip the protective device quickly, before anyone can be harmed by the voltage on that metal. How much current flows depends on one number, the earth fault loop impedance, written Zs.

What the loop is

In a TN system, a fault from line to an earthed metal part sends current round a loop: from the supply transformer, along the line conductor to the fault, back through the protective conductor and the earthing path, and into the transformer neutral. Zs is the total impedance of that loop, so the prospective fault current is roughly U0 ÷ Zs. The device must operate within a limited time at that current.

Zs ≤ U0 ÷ Ia

U0 is the nominal voltage to earth and Ia the current that makes the device disconnect within the required time. Zs is the sum of the external loop impedance Ze (the supply and its earthing) and the circuit’s own R1 + R2, the resistance of the line conductor plus the protective conductor.

Disconnection times

For a TN system at 230 V to earth, the rules in IEC 60364-4-41 and BS 7671 set these maximum disconnection times.

CircuitMaximum disconnection time (TN, 230 V)
Final circuit up to 63 A with socket outlets, or up to 32 A with fixed equipment only0.4 s
Distribution circuits and other final circuits5 s

For a TT system the equivalent times are 0.2 s for the final circuits above, and 1 s for the rest. The longer 5 s time for distribution circuits is allowed because the protective devices there need time to discriminate with those downstream, and the touch voltages stay lower.

Worked example: a 32 A circuit

A 32 A Type B miniature circuit breaker trips instantaneously between 3 and 5 times its rating, so to guarantee a fast trip the fault current must reach 5 × 32 = 160 A. The simple limit is Zs ≤ 230 ÷ 160 = 1.44 Ω. BS 7671 tabulates 1.37 Ω for this breaker because it also allows for supply voltage tolerance with a factor of 0.95: 0.95 × 230 ÷ 160 = 1.37 Ω. A Type C breaker trips at 5 to 10 times rating, so its limit is half the Type B value, and a Type D breaker’s is a quarter.

The 0.8 rule on site

The tabulated limits assume the conductors are at their working temperature, such as 70 °C for PVC cable. Tests on a cold installation read lower, since copper resistance rises with temperature. A common allowance is to compare the measured value with 80% of the tabulated limit. For the 32 A Type B breaker, that is 0.8 × 1.37 = 1.10 Ω. A measured Zs of 0.95 Ω passes, and 1.2 Ω fails even though it is under 1.37 Ω.

How long can the cable be?

Zs grows with cable length, so a long run can fail even when the cable carries the current without trouble. Take a 16 A Type B circuit in 2.5 mm² live and 1.5 mm² protective conductor, with R1 + R2 about 19.5 mΩ per metre at 20 °C, and an external impedance Ze of 0.35 Ω (illustrative). The tabulated limit is 0.95 × 230 ÷ (5 × 16) = 2.73 Ω, and 80% of that is 2.19 Ω. The room left for the cable is 2.19 − 0.35 = 1.84 Ω, so the longest run is 1.84 ÷ 0.01951 = about 94 m. At 30 m, Zs is 0.35 + 30 × 0.01951 = 0.94 Ω, which gives a fault current of about 246 A, well above the 80 A needed. Long runs call for a larger protective conductor, a lower-rated or faster device, or an RCD.

TT systems use an RCD

In a TT system the earth electrode resistance Ra is far higher than the loop of a TN system, so the fault current is too small for a breaker to trip fast. Protection relies on an RCD, with the condition Ra × IΔn ≤ 50 V. With a 30 mA RCD, Ra can be as high as 50 ÷ 0.03 = 1,667 Ω. A 100 mA device allows 500 Ω and a 300 mA device 167 Ω. See RCD, RCCB and RCBO explained.

What to do about a high Zs

  • Check the connections. A loose protective conductor or a poor joint is the commonest cause, and it also makes the circuit unsafe.
  • Increase the protective conductor or shorten the circuit.
  • Use a device with a lower limit, such as a Type B instead of a Type C, or an RCBO.
  • Reduce Ze, by improving the supply earthing where it is within your control.

For the supply arrangements these rules depend on, see types of earthing systems and earthing and grounding. The device curves behind Type B, C and D are in circuit breaker types, and fault levels in short-circuit current basics. Local rules govern real designs, so confirm the limits in the edition your project uses.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Zs is Ze plus R1 + R2, and it must be low enough for the protective device to trip within the required time.
  • ✓Type C limits are half and Type D limits a quarter of Type B limits for the same rating.
  • ✓Compare a cold reading with 80% of the tabulated limit, and recheck long circuits.
  • ✓On TT systems rely on an RCD with Ra × IΔn ≤ 50 V.
