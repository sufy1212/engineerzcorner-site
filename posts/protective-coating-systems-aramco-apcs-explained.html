Protective Coating Systems Explained: Reading the Aramco APCS Table in Pictures

Every steel surface on a plant is protected by a defined stack of coats over a defined level of clean steel. Here are the 24 Aramco APCS systems turned into pictures: layers, temperature limits, thickness, blast grades and what to choose.

Ask five engineers how a pipe or tank is protected from rust and you will hear one word: paint. In practice it is a system: a defined stack of coats, each with its own job and thickness, applied over steel prepared to a defined cleanliness. Saudi Aramco names these stacks Approved Protective Coating Systems, or APCS, and the numbering is widely used on oil, gas and industrial projects. This post turns the specification table into pictures you can read at a glance.

One system, three jobs

A coating system is built in layers because no single coat does everything. The primer bonds to the steel and, with zinc, protects it electrochemically. The mid coat adds thickness to block moisture and salts. The topcoat faces the environment. Between coats the table asks for light abrading of the previous coat, so the next one grips.

Reading the numbering

The first number is the service family. Atmospheric epoxy is 1, immersion is 2, coal tar epoxy is 3, and so on up to 12 for non-skid floors. The letter is the variant. The atmospheric family is the easiest place to see the logic.

How much paint is that?

Thickness is the most checked number on site. It is measured as dry film thickness (DFT), in micrometres. Hot-service and zinc systems are thin, general epoxies sit between 200 and 400 µm, and the heavy-duty immersion and buried linings run from 500 µm to over 1,000 µm.

Surface preparation comes first

Most coating failures trace back to the surface, not the paint. Each system names a blast grade, and the demanding services need the cleanest steel.

The rule of thumb: the wetter or more demanding the service, the cleaner the steel. All immersion systems need Sa 3. APCS-11C is the exception, specifying solvent cleaning (SSPC-SP 1) and then commercial blast (SSPC-SP 6). The table also lists a separate touch-up grade for each system, often Sa 2½ or power-tool cleaning (St 3).

Rules that catch people out

!
Near anodes, add 30%. On immersion systems 2A, 2B, 2C, 2E, 2G and 2H, the coating within 2 m of anode connections must be 30% thicker than the specified range. For APCS-2A (270–375 µm) that means about 351–488 µm there.
×
Coal tar epoxy has limits. APCS-3 is not for use in pressure vessels or in direct sunlight, and its succeeding coats must be in contrasting colours. A holding primer, if used, counts as an additional coat.
✓
Potable water needs paperwork. APCS-2B requires a valid certificate for potable use, with a maximum service pressure of 3,445 kPa(g), 500 psig. APCS-2C can be used for potable water only with a certificate from an authorised agency.
!
Watch 2G and 2H. The glass flake vinyl ester 2G is not suitable for de-mineralised water, polar solvents or steam-out cleaning. For gas service, 2H total film thickness is 15 to 20 mils.
ⓘ
Concrete needs more. Coal tar epoxy (APCS-3) on concrete must be at least 720 µm (28 mils) thick. For 9 and 10, non-metallic surfaces must be prepared dry and clean.
ⓘ
Performance targets for 5A and 5B. The insulating coat should match R-20 of conventional insulation at 500 µm (ASTM C177). The table also sets solar reflectance of at least 80% (ASTM E903), emittance of at least 90% (ASTM E408) and 1,500 hours of salt fog (ASTM B117) without rust creep, blistering or cracking. Both are applied over an approved APCS-1A, 1B or 1C primer.

What the inspector checks

  • Cleanliness before coating. Blast grade against ISO 8501-1 or SSPC photographs, dust level, and soluble salts by the Bresle method (ISO 8502-6).
  • Profile. The anchor pattern, measured by replica tape under ISO 8503-5. The tables ask for 40–65 µm for most systems, 25–40 µm for the zinc-rich epoxies, and 60–100 µm for the thick linings and coal tar epoxy.
  • Film thickness. Wet film as the work proceeds, dry film afterwards, measured to SSPC-PA 2 and compared with the specified range for every coat and for the total.
  • Cure and recoat. Immersion systems 2A, 2B and 2C list a seven-day cure before service. The thick linings 2E, 2G and 2H are applied wet-on-wet.
  • Holiday testing. Immersion linings are normally checked for pinholes and thin spots to NACE SP0188 before they go into service.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Choose by environment first (air, immersion, buried or hot), then by temperature and special rules.
  • ✓Match the blast grade and profile to the system, since most failures start at the surface.
  • ✓Check total thickness and every coat against the specified range, and add 30% within 2 m of anode connections on immersion systems.
  • ✓Use this summary to learn the logic and the official standard and data sheets to specify.
