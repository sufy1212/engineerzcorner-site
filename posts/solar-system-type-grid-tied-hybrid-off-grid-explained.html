Grid-Tied, Hybrid or Off-Grid Solar

The first decision in a solar design is not the panel count. It is whether the system is allowed to keep running when the grid does not, and that one choice sets the battery, the inverter and most of the cost.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Solar / System Type
01
A standard grid-tied system has no battery and shuts down within a fraction of a second of a grid failure, even in full sun. That is deliberate: it is called anti-islanding.
02
A hybrid system adds a battery and an islanding-capable inverter plus a transfer device, so selected loads keep running through an outage.
03
Off-grid has no utility at all. The battery must cover several cloudy days, so it dominates the cost.
04
Battery energy is sized from the critical load: kWh = load × hours (or days) ÷ usable depth of discharge, with a margin for inverter losses.

Why grid-tied panels go dark in an outage

A grid-tie inverter synchronises with the utility voltage and frequency and feeds power into that waveform. If the grid disappears while the inverter keeps energising the lines, it could back-feed a downed line and endanger the people repairing it. So grid-connected inverters certified to UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 must detect the loss of the grid and stop, typically within a fraction of a second. This applies whether the system uses string inverters or microinverters. After the grid returns, the standard also requires a delay, commonly stated as around five minutes, before reconnecting.

Some products offer limited daytime power to a designated outlet without a battery, but a system that keeps the house running through an outage needs the hybrid arrangement below.

What a hybrid system adds

BatteryStores energy from the array or the grid for use during an outage and, depending on the tariff, at peak times.
Islanding-capable inverterForms its own local grid when the utility fails. Not every hybrid inverter can do this, so check the datasheet.
Transfer device and critical-load panelPhysically separates the house from the grid before the inverter forms the island, and feeds only the circuits you chose to back up.

A battery inverter can also be added to an existing grid-tied solar inverter, which keeps operating inside the island while the battery inverter forms it. The available backup power is limited by the smallest of the inverter's continuous rating, the battery's discharge rate and the critical load itself, so a large battery does not help if the inverter cannot supply the loads, particularly motor starting surges.

Backup hours = battery kWh × DoD ÷ average critical load kW. Add roughly 15–20% for inverter losses.

Worked example: a fridge, router, lighting and a few outlets average 0.6 kW. To ride through a 12-hour outage the system must deliver 0.6 × 12 = 7.2 kWh. With 90% usable depth of discharge, the battery needs 7.2 ÷ 0.9 = 8.0 kWh nominal, before inverter losses. In the other direction, a 10 kWh battery at 90% DoD carries the same load for 10 × 0.9 ÷ 0.6 = 15 hours. If the outage is during the day, the array can recharge the battery and extend the time further, but plan the battery for the worst case.

Sizing an off-grid battery

Off-grid, the battery has to cover the load through days with little sun. The usual planning target is one to three days of autonomy, so the energy needed is the daily load times the days of autonomy, divided by usable depth of discharge. For a 10 kWh/day load with 2 days of autonomy and 80% usable depth of discharge:

StepWorkingResult
Energy to cover10 kWh/day × 2 days20 kWh
Nominal capacity20 ÷ 0.825 kWh
With 15–20% for inverter losses25 × 1.15 to 1.2028.7 to 30.0 kWh

That is about three times the daily load held in storage, and it is why batteries are the largest cost in an off-grid system. A backup generator for the long tail of multi-day bad weather often costs less than adding a third or fourth day of battery. The array and the charge controller are then sized so the system recovers the battery in poor-sun months; see sizing from load to panel count.

Net metering and economics

The value of a grid-tied system depends on what the utility pays for exported energy. Under net metering the export offsets what you import, and under a feed-in tariff it is paid at a set rate. Where export is credited at a low rate, self-consumption, and so storage, becomes more attractive. These rules differ by country, state and utility and change often, so check the current tariff before assuming a payback. The payback side is covered in Solar Payback and ROI Explained.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Assuming solar panels keep the lights on in an outageA standard grid-tied system shuts down by design
Backing up the whole houseCost and battery size jump; most of the benefit comes from the critical loads
Buying a hybrid inverter without checking islandingSome hybrid inverters manage solar and battery but cannot form a grid during an outage
Sizing the battery without the inverter limitBackup power is capped by the inverter and discharge rate
Ignoring motor starting loadsA pump or compressor surge can trip a small inverter
Using nominal battery capacity as usableDepth-of-discharge limits and losses reduce what you can draw

Key takeaways

  • ✓Grid-tied has no backup by design (anti-islanding); hybrid adds battery, an islanding-capable inverter and a transfer device; off-grid has no utility.
  • ✓Backup battery = critical load kW × hours ÷ usable DoD, plus about 15–20% for inverter losses.
  • ✓Off-grid battery = daily load × autonomy days ÷ usable DoD; one to three days is the usual target.
  • ✓Check inverter islanding capability, continuous rating and surge capacity as well as battery capacity.
  • ✓Confirm tariff rules and local interconnection requirements before choosing a system type.
