Chiller Capacity and COP: Where the Compressor's Work Actually Goes

A condenser sized to reject only the cooling load, with no allowance for the compressor's own work, is undersized from day one.

What the cycle is actually doing

A refrigeration cycle doesn't destroy heat — it moves it, from the cooled space to somewhere it can be rejected (outside air, a cooling tower, a condenser water loop). Moving heat against its natural direction (from cold to hot) isn't free: it takes work, supplied by the compressor. That work input is what makes the whole cycle possible, and it shows up again later as extra heat the condenser has to deal with.

COP: cooling delivered per unit of work in

COP  =  Qcooling  /  Wcompressor
Qcooling = cooling capacity delivered at the evaporator · Wcompressor = compressor electrical work input

COP is a straightforward efficiency ratio: how much cooling comes out for each unit of compressor work put in. It isn't a fixed number for a given machine — it depends on the operating conditions, primarily the temperature lift between the evaporator (cold side) and condenser (hot side). A smaller lift (mild condensing conditions, a higher chilled water setpoint) gives a better COP than a large one, which is why chiller efficiency varies with ambient conditions and load, not just nameplate rating.

Why the condenser sees more heat than the cooling load

Qcondenser  =  Qcooling  +  Wcompressor
Simple energy balance across the refrigeration cycle: everything that enters as heat or work has to leave somewhere

This follows directly from conservation of energy across the cycle: the heat absorbed at the evaporator (the cooling load) and the work put in by the compressor both end up as heat that has to be rejected at the condenser. A condenser or cooling tower sized only to match the chiller's rated cooling capacity, with no allowance for the compressor's own work, is undersized — sometimes by 20% or more depending on the COP at design conditions, since a lower COP means the compressor is contributing proportionally more of the total heat the condenser has to reject.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A refrigeration cycle moves heat using compressor work as the input — it doesn't destroy the heat.
  • ✓COP is cooling delivered divided by compressor work input, and it varies with operating conditions, not just equipment rating.
  • ✓By energy balance, condenser heat rejection equals cooling capacity plus compressor work input, not cooling capacity alone.
  • ✓A lower COP means the compressor contributes proportionally more heat to the condenser load, on top of the cooling capacity itself.
