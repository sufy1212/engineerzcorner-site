DC/AC Ratio & Inverter Clipping

A 6.6 kW array on a 5 kW inverter sounds undersized. In practice the array rarely produces its full nameplate power, so the small amount of energy clipped can cost less than the inverter capacity saved.

Why the array rarely hits nameplate

A panel's rating is its output under Standard Test Conditions: 1000 W/m² irradiance at a 25°C cell temperature. Real operation seldom matches both. Cells run hotter than 25°C in sunshine and lose output through the module's negative power temperature coefficient, irradiance is often below 1000 W/m², and soiling, wiring losses and module mismatch take further cuts. The result is that a clear-day peak is typically noticeably below the array's DC nameplate. An inverter sized to the full nameplate would spend almost its whole life below its rated output.

DC/AC ratio  =  Array DC nameplate (kWp)  /  Inverter AC rating (kW)
Also called the inverter loading ratio (ILR). Grid-tie designs commonly sit somewhere above 1.0 and often in the 1.1–1.4 range, but the best value depends on climate, tilt, temperature and inverter cost.

What clipping actually is

An inverter can only push out as much AC power as its rating allows. When the array could produce more than that, the inverter shifts its operating point away from the array's maximum power point, so the array delivers only what the inverter can export. The energy above the AC limit is not stored or diverted — it is simply not harvested (unless the system is DC-coupled to a battery that can absorb it). Clipping is concentrated around midday on clear days, and is worst when irradiance is high and modules are cool.

A worked example

6.6 kWp array, 5 kW inverterDC/AC ratio = 6.6 / 5 = 1.32.
Peak output at ~80% of nameplate6.6 × 0.80 ≈ 5.3 kW, so only about 0.3 kW is clipped and only during the sunniest hours — illustrative figures, not a prediction.

Because the excess above 5 kW occurs for a small fraction of the year, the annual energy lost is usually a small percentage of production. The 5 kW inverter costs less than a 6.6 kW unit, and the array produces more energy in the shoulder hours of morning and evening, when it would otherwise be underusing a large inverter.

Trade-offs of a higher ratio

Higher DC/AC ratioEffect
Lower inverter cost per kW of arraySmaller, cheaper inverter for the same panel count
More energy in low-light hoursInverter operates closer to its efficient range for longer
More clipped energy at middayLosses rise faster as the ratio climbs past what the site supports
Higher DC-side stress on the inverterMust stay inside its maximum DC input voltage and current limits

Limits that override the ratio

  • !Voltage and current limits. Cold-morning Voc must stay below the inverter's maximum DC voltage, and each MPPT input has a maximum current and a usable voltage window. Oversizing the array does not relax these.
  • !Manufacturer and warranty limits. Many inverters specify a maximum allowed DC input power or ratio; exceeding it can void the warranty.
  • !Grid and export rules. Utilities and local rules may cap inverter AC output or export, which sets the AC side of the ratio for you. Check the rules that apply to your site.
  • !Off-grid systems differ. Stand-alone and hybrid inverters are sized to the continuous load plus motor-starting surge, not to a DC/AC ratio.

Key takeaways

  • ✓DC/AC ratio = array DC nameplate ÷ inverter AC rating; ratios above 1.0 are normal for grid-tie.
  • ✓Real peak output sits below nameplate because of temperature, irradiance and system losses, so full-nameplate inverter sizing usually wastes capacity.
  • ✓Clipping is lost energy at the sunniest hours; check the annual loss with a yield simulation before settling on a ratio.
  • ✓Voltage, current, warranty and grid limits cap how far you can oversize the array.
