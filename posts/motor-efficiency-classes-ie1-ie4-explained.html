Motor Efficiency Classes Explained: IE1 to IE4, the EU Rules and What the Savings Look Like

Over its life, a motor uses far more in electricity than it costs to buy. Here is what the IE classes mean, which ones the EU requires, a worked running cost comparison and the points that matter as much as the class.

A motor costs a modest amount to buy and a great deal to run. Over its life, the electricity it uses is typically far larger than its purchase price, so a few percentage points of efficiency matter. Efficiency classes give a common way to compare motors, and in many regions regulation now sets the minimum.

The IE classes

IEC 60034-30-1 defines efficiency classes for single-speed, three-phase induction motors from 0.12 to 1,000 kW, measured at rated output by the method in IEC 60034-2-1. Each class has a minimum efficiency that depends on the power and the number of poles.

ClassNamePosition
IE1Standard efficiencyBeing phased out
IE2High efficiencyNow the minimum for small motors in the EU
IE3Premium efficiencyEU minimum for most three-phase motors
IE4Super premium efficiencyEU minimum for 75 to 200 kW motors

A class is a minimum for a given size, so a 7.5 kW four-pole motor needs about 88.7% to reach IE2 while a smaller or larger motor has a different threshold. Check the nameplate or datasheet for the actual value, which manufacturers also publish at 50%, 75% and 100% load.

What the regulations require in the EU

  • From 1 July 2021: three-phase motors from 0.75 to 1,000 kW must be at least IE3, and those from 0.12 to under 0.75 kW at least IE2. Variable speed drives must meet class IE2.
  • From 1 July 2023: two-, four- and six-pole motors from 75 to 200 kW must be IE4. Single-phase motors from 0.12 kW must be at least IE2.
  • Exclusions apply to some brake and explosion-protected motors, and the rules cover only new motors placed on the market, so an installed motor can stay in service.

Other regions have their own rules and dates, so check the ones that apply to your project.

Worked example: running cost

Compare two 11 kW four-pole motors at full load for 6,000 hours a year. Efficiency values used here are about 89.8% for IE2, 91.4% for IE3 and 93.0% for IE4, which are illustrative, so use the datasheet figures for real motors.

  • IE2 input power: 11 ÷ 0.898 = 12.25 kW. IE3: 11 ÷ 0.914 = 12.04 kW. IE4: 11 ÷ 0.930 = 11.83 kW.
  • Losses: 1.25 kW for IE2, 1.04 kW for IE3 and 0.83 kW for IE4. IE4 losses are about 20% below IE3, which matches the common description of the step between classes.
  • IE2 to IE3 saves 0.21 kW, or about 1,290 kWh a year, worth about 129 at 0.10 per kWh. IE2 to IE4 saves about 2,530 kWh a year, roughly 253 at the same price.

The yearly saving looks modest for one motor, but the whole running cost is the larger picture: 12.04 kW over 6,000 hours is 72,200 kWh, about 7,200 a year at the same tariff, which dwarfs the price of the motor. Across a plant with many motors that run for long hours, the savings add up. The payback on a premium motor is the extra cost divided by the yearly saving, so it is shortest for large motors that run continuously.

Where the losses come from

  • Stator and rotor copper losses, which rise with the square of the current.
  • Iron losses in the core, set by the voltage and frequency.
  • Friction and windage, from the bearings and the cooling fan.
  • Stray load losses, a small extra that depends on the load.

Higher classes cut these losses with more copper, better steel, more careful design and, in IE4 and above, sometimes a different rotor technology.

Practical points beyond the class

  • Sizing matters. Efficiency is highest between about 60 and 100% load and falls off at light load, so a large oversized motor wastes energy. Match the rating to the real load.
  • Speed control. For variable-load fans and pumps, a drive saves far more than a better motor, as in VFD speed scaling. Check that a motor is suitable for use on a drive.
  • Starting. A premium-efficiency motor can have a higher starting current, so recheck protection and the supply. See motor starting methods and overload protection and trip classes.
  • The load itself. Belts, couplings and worn pumps waste more than the motor does. See pump power and compressed air energy.

Work out the running current with the motor full-load current calculator, and put a figure on yearly energy with the running cost of a load.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Efficiency classes are minimums that depend on size and poles, so read the nameplate or datasheet values.
  • ✓The EU rules cover new motors placed on the market, with some exclusions.
  • ✓Estimate payback from the extra cost, the power saved, the hours and the tariff.
  • ✓Right-size motors and use drives on variable loads, since both can save more than a class upgrade.
