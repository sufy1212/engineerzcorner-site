Punch Lists and Snagging: Closing Out a Project Without Loose Ends

The last stretch of a project is mostly small defects. Here is how to write punch list entries that can be assigned and checked, sort them by priority, and estimate how long the close-out will take.

Near the end of a project, the work is mostly done and the remaining problems are small and numerous: a missing label, a door that does not latch, a cable gland not tightened. A punch list, also called a snag list, collects those items in one place so that they are fixed, checked and signed off before the space or system is handed over.

What a good entry says

Each entry needs four things: what is wrong, exactly where it is, which trade owns it, and how urgent it is. “Fix the panel” cannot be assigned or checked, while “MCC-2 door seal missing, plant room B, electrical contractor, must fix before energising” can. A photo or drawing reference helps, and so does a date raised.

Sorting by priority

Projects commonly sort items into three groups. The names and the cut-offs differ between contracts, so use the categories your contract defines.

GroupTypical meaningExample
CriticalBlocks safe use, energising or handoverMissing earth connection, unlabelled isolator
MajorMust be corrected before final completionIncorrect cable tag, damaged enclosure
MinorCosmetic or can follow handoverPaint touch-up, loose cover screw

A worked burn-down

Suppose an inspection raises 120 items: 15 critical, 60 major and 45 minor. If the trades can clear about 10 items a working day, the critical items alone take 1.5 days, so allowing for the first pass they are cleared in 2 working days. All 120 items take about 12 working days. Not every fix passes re-inspection, though. If 10% fail and reopen, that adds 12 items and about 1.2 more days, which brings the total to about 13 working days. This is an illustration with invented numbers, but it shows why the handover date should be set from the burn-down rate, not from hope.

Closing items properly

  • The inspector closes, not the contractor. An item is closed when the person who raised it has verified the fix. Track Open and Closed separately, and re-inspect rather than assume.
  • Watch the critical count. Safety and function items normally have to be cleared before sign-off, so the open critical number is the one to report.
  • Carry leftovers forward with owners and dates. Items that will outlast the handover go into a dated action list, recorded in the meeting minutes.
  • Link it to payment and documents. Contracts often tie retention or final payment to outstanding items, and handover usually also needs test certificates, as-built drawings and operation manuals. Check what yours requires.

Where it fits

A punch list comes at the end of construction and commissioning, after testing and inspection. For the electrical side of that stage, see pre-commissioning vs commissioning and the panel FAT checklist, and for the wider project stages, the PPP project lifecycle. Use the punch list tracker to count open items and the critical backlog as you go.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Write entries that can be assigned and checked, with location and trade.
  • ✓Estimate close-out time from the realistic clearance rate, including items that fail re-inspection.
  • ✓Track Open and Closed separately and re-inspect every fix.
  • ✓Carry leftover items into a dated action list with an owner.
