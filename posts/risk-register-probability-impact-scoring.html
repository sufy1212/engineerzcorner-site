Risk Register Basics: Turning Probability × Impact Into Priority

A list of things that might go wrong isn't a risk register. It becomes one the moment every entry has a score, an owner, and a reason it's ranked where it is.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Project Management
01
A risk register scores every risk on two axes — how likely it is, and how bad it would be if it happened.
02
Multiplying probability by impact turns two subjective judgments into one comparable number.
03
The score decides what gets attention first — not how loudly someone raised it in a meeting.
04
A register only earns its keep if it's revisited, not filed away after the kickoff meeting.

The two inputs

Every risk gets rated on a simple 1–3 scale along two separate questions. Keeping them separate matters — a risk that's very likely but trivial in effect is a completely different problem from one that's rare but severe, and collapsing them into a single "how worried should I be" guess loses that distinction.

RatingProbability meansImpact means
1Unlikely to occur on this projectMinor — absorbed without real disruption
2Plausible, has happened on similar projectsModerate — noticeable cost, schedule, or quality effect
3Likely, or already showing early signsSevere — major cost, schedule slip, or safety consequence

The formula

Probability1–3
×
Impact1–3
=
Score1–9
The score is what gets sorted and compared — not the raw probability or impact rating on its own.

Reading the score

ScorePriority bandWhat it means for the team
1–3LowLog it and monitor — no immediate action needed
4–6MediumAssign an owner and a mitigation plan, review regularly
7–9HighNeeds a response now — the combination of likely and severe

Why raw ranking beats gut feeling

The loudest risk in the room isn't always the biggest one Without scoring, whichever risk was mentioned most recently or most forcefully tends to get the attention — regardless of whether it's actually likely or severe. A scored register forces every risk through the same two questions, so a low-probability, low-impact annoyance can't crowd out a high-probability, high-impact risk that nobody's raised their voice about.

Worked example

Two risks on a build-out: a key supplier missing a delivery date, and a minor finish material being briefly unavailable.

Scoring

Supplier delivery delayProbability 3 × Impact 3 = 9 (High)
Finish material shortageProbability 2 × Impact 1 = 2 (Low)

Both are legitimate risks, but only one demands a mitigation plan and an owner assigned this week. The score makes that obvious at a glance, instead of leaving it to whoever spoke up loudest at the last progress meeting.

Keeping it alive

A register filled in once at kickoff and never reopened is a compliance document, not a management tool. Probability and impact both shift as a project progresses — a supplier risk rated Medium in month one can become High once a delivery is confirmed late, or drop to Low once an alternate source is secured. Revisiting the register at each progress review, not just adding new risks but re-scoring existing ones, is what keeps the priority list matching reality.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Score probability and impact separately — a likely-but-minor risk and a rare-but-severe one need different responses.
  • ✓Multiply, don't average — the product spreads scores across a wider range and separates true high-priority risks more clearly.
  • ✓Let the score set priority, not who raised the risk or how recently it came up.
  • ✓Re-score at every progress review — a static register goes stale within weeks on an active project.
