Heat Pump Sizing Basics: Why Electrification Made This a Core Skill

Heat pumps aren't sized like a furnace or a straight AC unit — their capacity moves with outdoor temperature, which is exactly the part a lot of sizing mistakes come from. Here's the logic that actually drives it.

Engineerz CorneRReference · HVAC / Heat Pumps
01
A heat pump's rated capacity is only true at the outdoor test temperature it was rated at — not at your coldest design day.
02
COP (coefficient of performance) drops as outdoor temperature drops, which is the opposite of how a furnace behaves.
03
The "balance point" is where the heat pump alone can no longer match the building's heat loss — everything colder needs backup.
04
Oversizing for the coldest day wastes efficiency on the 95% of days that aren't that cold.

Why this is suddenly everyone's problem

Electrification has pushed heat pumps from a niche mild-climate product into the default heating choice in a growing share of new installations and retrofits, as utilities and regulators push households and buildings away from combustion heating. That shift means a lot of people sizing heating systems for the first time are sizing a heat pump instead of a furnace or boiler — and the sizing logic isn't the same exercise at all.

A furnace's output barely changes with outdoor temperature; a heat pump's output changes constantly with it, because it's not generating heat by combustion, it's moving heat from outside air (or ground, or water) into the building using a refrigeration cycle running in reverse.

Capacity isn't one number — it's a curve

Rated capacity vs. capacity at your design temperature

A unit's nameplate or brochure capacity is measured at a specific standardized outdoor temperature — often around 47°F/8°C for heating. As outdoor temperature drops, the heat pump has a smaller temperature difference to extract useful heat from, so both its capacity and its efficiency (COP) fall. The capacity that actually matters for sizing is the capacity at your coldest expected design temperature, not the brochure number — and manufacturers publish that as a performance curve or table, not a single figure.

Below the balance point temperature, the heat pump alone can't keep up with heat loss — that's where backup heat (resistance strip, or a hybrid furnace) has to pick up the remainder.

COP: the number that moves the wrong way

Outdoor temperatureTypical COP behavior
Mild (10–15°C / 50–59°F)High COP — often 3.5–4.5, very efficient
Cold (0°C / 32°F)COP drops, often to roughly 2.5–3
Very cold (-10°C / 14°F and below)COP drops further — cold-climate models are specifically engineered to hold up better here
Standard vs. cold-climate heat pump models differ mainly in how well they hold capacity and COP as temperature keeps dropping — not in their mild-weather performance, which is often similar.

Sizing mistakes that actually cost money

Oversizing for the coldest daySizing the whole system around a design-day extreme that occurs a handful of hours a year wastes cost and efficiency across the other 95% of the season.
Ignoring the manufacturer's capacity-at-temperature tableUsing brochure capacity as if it holds at every temperature overstates real cold-weather performance.
Skipping the heat loss calculationSizing off the old furnace's tonnage carries over decades of assumption errors — a proper heat loss calc is the actual starting point.
Undersizing backup heatIf the balance point sits above your coldest design day, backup heat isn't optional — it has to cover the real gap.

A practical sizing sequence

  • 1Calculate the building's actual heat loss at your local winter design temperature — not summer cooling load, a separate calculation.
  • 2Pull the manufacturer's heating capacity table at that same design temperature, not the rated/brochure capacity.
  • 3Find where heat pump output crosses building heat loss — that's your balance point.
  • 4Size backup heat to cover the gap between balance point and your actual coldest design day, not the whole heating load.
  • 5In colder climates, weigh a cold-climate-rated unit against a standard one — the efficiency difference below freezing is often significant.
  • 6Don't forget cooling-mode sizing too — the same unit has to be checked against summer cooling load separately.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A heat pump's usable capacity is a curve against outdoor temperature, not a fixed number.
  • ✓COP falls as it gets colder — the opposite behavior of combustion heating.
  • ✓The balance point, not the coldest day, should drive the primary unit's sizing.
  • ✓Backup heat exists to cover the gap below the balance point, not to double the whole system.
