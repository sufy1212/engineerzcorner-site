ATS, MTS, ECB & Switch Disconnectors: Transfer & Isolation Switching Devices Explained

Nine devices that move or isolate load current without necessarily interrupting a fault — transfer switches, on-load isolation gear, enclosed breakers, bypass switches and interlocks — what each one actually does, and how to tell them apart.

Engineerz CorneRReference · LV Switching & Transfer Gear
01
ATS and MTS do the same job — transferring a load between two sources — the only difference is whether transfer happens automatically on a sensed failure or needs an operator; both are interlocked so both sources can never be connected together.
02
A switch disconnector isn't just an isolator — it's rated to make and break normal load current, so it can be opened safely under full load, unlike a plain off-load isolator.
03
None of the transfer or isolation devices here interrupt fault current on their own — a fused switch disconnector adds that only via its fuses, and an ECB is the exception, because it is a breaker, just in its own standalone enclosure.
04
Interlocking — mechanical (key) or electrical — is what actually stops two live sources from ever closing together; it's arguably the most safety-critical part of any transfer scheme.

Source transfer switches: ATS, MTS & COS

All three move a load between two sources — commonly utility mains and a standby generator — with interlocking that prevents both being connected at once. What separates them is automation, not the underlying switching principle.

ATS
Automatic Transfer Switch
Senses loss of the primary (utility) source and transfers the load to standby — typically a generator — without operator action, after a short time delay to confirm the failure is real. Re-transfers back once the primary is stable, usually after its own delay.
MTS
Manual Transfer Switch
Performs the identical electrical job as an ATS, but needs someone present to operate the handle or a remote control action — no sensing logic or timers. Simpler and cheaper, and often kept as a manual backup even on sites that also have an ATS.
COS
Change-Over Switch
The generic mechanical device behind both: a multi-position (I–0–II) rotary or lever switch connecting the load to one of two sources. An MTS is essentially a COS with a genset-changeover application; the terms are often used interchangeably on site.
ATS vs MTSAn ATS senses loss of the primary source and transfers automatically, with a delay to confirm the failure is real and another to re-transfer once utility power stabilizes. An MTS does the identical electrical job but needs someone present — cheaper, simpler, and still common as a manual backup path.
Switch disconnector vs plain isolatorA plain isolator gives only a visible, off-load air gap — opening it under load can flash over. A switch disconnector is purpose-built to interrupt normal load current safely, so the same isolation can be exercised without killing the load elsewhere first.

On-load isolation: switch disconnector, load break switch & fused switch disconnector

These provide the visible, physical air gap of an isolator, but are additionally rated to make and break normal load current — still not fault current, unless fuses are added.

Switch Disconnector
Load-Break Isolating Switch
An isolator upgraded to IEC 60947-3 on-load duty — it can be safely opened under full normal load current, not just off-load, while still giving the visible air gap needed for safe maintenance isolation.
LBS
Load Break Switch
The medium-voltage equivalent of a switch disconnector, found in ring main units — rated to interrupt normal load current at MV, often with a combined earthing function, but never intended to clear fault current.
Fused Switch Disconnector
Fuse-Switch Combination
A switch disconnector combined with HRC fuses in one unit — on-load switching plus genuine fault protection from the fuses, common in distribution boards and motor circuits where a full breaker isn't justified.

Enclosed breakers, bypass & interlocking: ECB, bypass switch & key interlock

The remaining pieces of a transfer or switching scheme — a standalone protective device, a way to service critical equipment without dropping the load, and the hardware that makes two-source closure physically impossible.

ECB
Enclosed Circuit Breaker
A circuit breaker (typically an MCCB or MCB) pre-mounted in its own weatherproof or IP-rated enclosure so it can be installed standalone — outdoors, on a wall, next to the equipment it feeds — rather than inside a panel. A local, code-required disconnecting means for rooftop AC units, pumps and similar loads, and a real breaker, not just a switch.
Maintenance Bypass Switch
Make-Before-Break Bypass
Lets a critical load fed through a UPS or an ATS be transferred to raw source power while the UPS or ATS itself is isolated for service — without ever dropping the load. Essential wherever downtime during routine maintenance isn't acceptable.
Key Interlock
Kirk Key System
A mechanical interlocking scheme using captive keys that can only be removed or exchanged in a fixed sequence, physically preventing two sources — two ATS positions, or a generator and utility breaker — from ever closing at the same time. A hardware-enforced backup to electrical interlocking, not a replacement for it.

Key takeaways

  • ✓ATS, MTS and COS all perform the same transfer function — the difference is automation, not the underlying switching principle.
  • ✓Switch disconnectors and load break switches add on-load switching to isolation, but stay fault-rated only when paired with fuses — or replaced by an ECB.
  • ✓An ECB is simply a breaker in its own enclosure — a full protective device, not just a switch, wherever standalone disconnection with fault protection is required near the load.
  • ✓A maintenance bypass switch keeps critical loads running during service; key interlocking makes dual-source closure physically impossible, not just electrically prevented.
