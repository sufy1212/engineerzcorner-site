FM-200, Novec 1230, CO2 & Foam: Fire Suppression Systems Explained

Four different ways to put a fire out automatically, each built around a different hazard: clean agent gas that leaves electronics untouched, CO2 that starves the fire of oxygen, and foam that seals off a flammable liquid surface. Same goal, very different components, occupancy limits and governing standards.

Fire suppression systems exist to detect, control and extinguish a fire automatically, before it can spread and before manual intervention would be fast enough to matter. What separates the systems below isn't the goal — it's the extinguishing mechanism, and that mechanism dictates where each one can and can't be used.

1. FM-200 System (HFC-227ea)

FM-200
HFC-227ea · Clean Agent Gaseous System

A clean agent gaseous system that absorbs heat and interrupts the chemical chain reaction of combustion. It discharges as a colorless, electrically non-conductive gas that leaves no residue behind, which is exactly why it's the default choice for spaces full of electronic equipment that can't tolerate a wet or corrosive agent.

Components
  • Storage cylinders
  • Manifold & piping
  • Control panel
  • Detection system
  • Nozzles
  • Manual release station
Applications
  • Data centers
  • Telecommunication rooms
  • Electrical rooms
  • Control rooms
  • Archives & museums

2. Novec 1230 System (FK-5-1-12)

Novec 1230
FK-5-1-12 · Next-Generation Clean Agent

A next-generation clean agent that extinguishes fire primarily by absorbing heat, physically breaking the fire's energy balance rather than displacing oxygen. It carries zero ozone depletion potential and a global warming potential of roughly 1 — orders of magnitude lower than FM-200 — with an atmospheric lifetime measured in days rather than decades, which is why it's increasingly specified for new-build critical facilities.

Components
  • Storage cylinders
  • Manifold & piping
  • Control panel
  • Detection system
  • Nozzles
  • Manual release station
Applications
  • Data centers
  • Server rooms
  • Control rooms
  • Museums & libraries
  • Medical facilities

3. CO2 System (Carbon Dioxide)

CO2 System
Carbon Dioxide · Total-Flooding Agent

Extinguishes fire by flooding the protected space with carbon dioxide, reducing the oxygen concentration below the level combustion can sustain, with a secondary cooling effect from the expanding gas. It's cheap per kilogram and leaves zero residue, but the flooding concentration required is also an asphyxiation hazard — which is the single biggest constraint on where a CO2 system can be applied.

Components
  • Storage cylinders
  • Manifold & piping
  • Control panel
  • Detection system
  • Nozzles / horns
  • Manual release station
Applications
  • Electrical rooms
  • Transformer rooms
  • Boiler rooms
  • Turbine rooms
  • Industrial areas
Why CO2 needs a pre-discharge alarmThe flooding concentration used to suppress fire is also enough to cause loss of consciousness and death in an occupied space. CO2 systems are fitted with an audible/visual pre-discharge alarm and a time delay to allow evacuation before release — and are generally restricted to normally unoccupied areas.
FM-200 / Novec 1230 vs CO2Both clean agents are designed to be used in occupied spaces at their design concentration. CO2's flooding concentration is inherently lethal, which is the core reason clean agents took over in occupied electronics rooms.

4. Foam System

Foam System
AFFF / Protein / Fluoroprotein · Surface Blanketing Agent

Used specifically against flammable liquid fires. Rather than displacing oxygen or absorbing heat throughout the space, foam forms a blanket over the liquid surface that seals off vapor release and separates the fuel from the flame — which is the mechanism a Class B liquid fire actually needs to stay out.

Components
  • Foam tank / bladder tank
  • Foam concentrate
  • Proportioning system
  • Piping & nozzles
  • Detection system
  • Control panel
Applications
  • Fuel storage areas
  • Oil tanks
  • Flammable liquid storage
  • Loading bays
  • Chemical plants

How to select the right system

  1. 1
    Identify the hazardDetermine the type of fire risk and the assets being protected — electronic equipment, flammable liquids, general combustibles or an energized electrical hazard each point toward a different agent.
  2. 2
    Assess the areaEvaluate room volume, normal occupancy and environmental conditions. Occupied spaces rule out CO2 as the primary agent; enclosure integrity affects how long a clean agent's design concentration can hold.
  3. 3
    Choose the suitable agentSelect the system that's both effective against the specific hazard and safe for how the space is actually used — weighing residue, ozone depletion potential and global warming potential where they apply.
  4. 4
    Comply with standardsDesign to the governing NFPA standard for the chosen agent, plus any applicable local fire code and manufacturer installation guidelines.
  5. 5
    Provide for maintenanceBuild in regular inspection, agent-quantity checks and functional testing — a suppression system that hasn't been maintained is a liability, not a safeguard.

Key benefits

  • ✓Automatic detection and actuation trigger suppression within seconds of a confirmed fire signature, without waiting on manual response.
  • ✓Fast discharge and effective knockdown — typically 10–30 seconds from release to design concentration.
  • ✓Minimizes damage to equipment and property compared with sprinklers or handheld extinguishers, especially where a clean agent is used.
  • ✓Configurable for both occupied spaces (clean agent) and unoccupied hazard areas (CO2), matched to how the room is actually used.
  • ✓Complies with NFPA and applicable local fire code requirements when designed and installed to the relevant standard.
  • ✓Enhances life safety and supports business continuity by protecting the assets a fire would otherwise take offline.

Key takeaways

  • ✓FM-200 and Novec 1230 are clean agents — no residue, safe for occupied electronics spaces — but Novec 1230's GWP is a small fraction of FM-200's.
  • ✓CO2 suppresses by oxygen displacement and is highly effective, but the discharge concentration is lethal — restricted to electrical and unoccupied hazard areas with a pre-discharge alarm.
  • ✓Foam is the only one of the four built for flammable liquid fires — it blankets the surface rather than treating the whole enclosure volume.
  • ✓Each system is governed by its own NFPA standard: NFPA 2001 for clean agents, NFPA 12 for CO2, NFPA 11 for foam.
  • ✓Selection follows the hazard and occupancy first — cost and agent availability come after the system has already been narrowed down by what's safe to use in that space.
