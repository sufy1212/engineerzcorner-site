Cable Derating Factors Worked Through

The ampacity in the table is for one cable in one reference condition. Heat from the room and from neighbouring cables take some of it away, and the factors that account for that multiply, they do not add.

Why a cable has to be derated

A conductor's current rating is set by how hot the insulation may get: 70 °C for PVC, 90 °C for XLPE. The current heats the conductor, and the heat has to flow out to the surroundings. If the surroundings are already warm, or other cables are heating the same space, less temperature rise is available, so the permitted current falls. The tabulated ampacity is for a defined reference condition, and every departure from it gets a correction factor.

Ambient temperature factor

The heat a cable can shed is proportional to its temperature rise above ambient, and the heating is proportional to I²R. A cable running at its maximum conductor temperature in a hotter room has a smaller rise available, so the permitted current scales with the square root of the ratio of rises:

Ca = √[ (Tmax − Tamb) / (Tmax − Tref) ] with Tref = 30 °C for cables in air.

These match the IEC 60364-5-52 correction table at 35, 40, 45 and 50 °C (for example 0.87 and 0.91 at 40 °C). Below 30 °C the table values are slightly smoothed, so read them from the standard rather than the formula. XLPE loses less because it has more temperature headroom above 30 °C.

Grouping factor

Cables run together each heat the others. For circuits bunched or enclosed in air, the reduction factors in IEC 60364-5-52 depend on the number of circuits or multicore cables:

These are for the bunched case. Spaced, single-layer arrangements on trays or ladders have their own, less severe factors, and ducts in the ground have separate tables. The factors assume a uniform group of equally sized, equally loaded cables, and they do not apply to multilayer groups; for multilayer routing you need a calculation method, not a table lookup.

Combine them by multiplying

Each correction acts on the same temperature rise, so the effects compound. The combined factor is the product of every applicable factor, including soil resistivity, burial depth or harmonic loading where those apply.

Multiply0.91 × 0.65 = 0.5915. This is the correct combined factor.
Adding reductions1 − 0.09 − 0.35 = 0.56. Lower than the product, so it over-derates and oversizes the cable.
Worst factor only0.65 ignores the ambient effect and under-derates, which is the unsafe mistake.

A smaller case shows the same pattern: 0.87 × 0.80 = 0.696, while adding the reductions (13% and 20%) gives 0.67. Both shortcuts are wrong, but only ignoring a factor leaves the cable running hotter than designed.

Worked example

A circuit with design current Ib = 95 A is protected by a 100 A breaker (In). It is wired in XLPE multicore cable on a perforated tray, in a 40 °C ambient, bunched with three other circuits (four circuits in all).

The same circuit in PVC needs more: Ca = 0.87, so the combined factor is 0.87 × 0.65 = 0.566 and the tabulated current must be at least 177 A, about 5% more than XLPE. The tabulated value comes from your cable table for the installation method; nothing in this calculation replaces that lookup.

The protection check

The conditions in IEC 60364-4-43 for overload protection are Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz and I₂ ≤ 1.45 Iz, where I₂ is the current that makes the device operate within its conventional time. For circuit breakers I₂ is 1.45 In, so once In ≤ Iz is satisfied the second condition holds too. Iz here is the derated current-carrying capacity of the cable in its actual installation, which is why the factors matter: selecting the cable from the underated table can leave In above the real Iz.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓Using the grouping factor from the wrong arrangement. Bunched, single-layer and buried-duct tables differ widely.
  • ✓Counting circuits wrongly. The table works in circuits or multicore cables, and a three-phase circuit of single-core cables counts as one circuit.
  • ✓Forgetting the neutral. With heavy harmonic loads the neutral carries current and may have to be counted as a loaded conductor.
  • ✓Taking the reference ambient from the wrong table. 30 °C is the air reference; buried cables use a 20 °C ground reference.
  • ✓Stopping at current capacity. Voltage drop and fault withstand are separate checks on the same cable.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Ca = √[(Tmax − Tamb) / (Tmax − 30)]: 0.87 (PVC) and 0.91 (XLPE) at 40 °C.
  • ✓Bunched grouping factors fall from 0.80 for two circuits to 0.38 for twenty.
  • ✓Multiply every applicable factor; adding over-derates, and using only one under-derates.
  • ✓Required tabulated current = In / combined factor, and then check Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz.
  • ✓Take final factors from IEC 60364-5-52 or your cable datasheet for the exact installation method.
