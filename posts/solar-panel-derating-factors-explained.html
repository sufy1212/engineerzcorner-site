Solar Panel Derating: Why Nameplate Wattage Isn't What You Get

A 400W panel almost never delivers 400W to the load. Its rating is measured under lab conditions a real rooftop never sees — here's where every percentage point actually goes.

What the nameplate number actually means

Every panel's rated wattage is measured under Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1000 W/m² of irradiance, 25°C cell temperature, and an AM1.5 solar spectrum, in a lab with a solar simulator. None of those three conditions describe a typical operating day — real irradiance is usually well below 1000 W/m², real cell temperature runs well above 25°C once a panel has been in the sun a while, and the spectrum shifts with time of day and atmosphere. The nameplate number is a fair, standardized basis for comparing panels — it was never meant to predict field output directly.

Temperature: usually the biggest single factor

Pactual = PSTC × [1 + γ × (Tcell − 25°C)]
γ = power temperature coefficient (negative), typically −0.30%/°C to −0.45%/°C depending on cell technology · Tcell = actual cell temperature, not ambient air temperature

Crystalline silicon panels lose power as they get hotter — the opposite of what intuition suggests for a "solar" product. On a still, sunny day, cell temperature commonly runs 20–25°C above ambient air temperature because the panel absorbs far more solar energy than it converts to electricity, and most of the rest becomes heat. A 35°C ambient day can put cells at 55–60°C, which at a typical −0.4%/°C coefficient is already a 12–14% power loss from temperature alone — before any other factor is counted.

The other stacking losses

Loss factorTypical rangeCause
Cell temperature5–15%Cells run hotter than STC's 25°C once in sustained sun
Soiling2–7%Dust, pollen, bird droppings reduce light reaching cells
Module mismatch1–3%Panels in a string never perform identically; string output is limited by the weakest
DC wiring1–3%Resistive (I²R) loss in cabling from panel to inverter
Inverter/MPPT efficiency2–4%DC→AC conversion is never 100% efficient
AC wiring<1%Resistive loss from inverter to point of connection
Availability/downtime~1%Brief outages, monitoring/communication faults, grid disconnects

These losses stack multiplicatively, not additively, but the combined effect is similar either way: a well-designed, well-maintained system commonly nets out to roughly 75–85% of its total nameplate DC capacity actually delivered as usable AC energy over a year. That gap isn't a defect to chase down — it's the expected cost of running real hardware outdoors instead of a lab sample under a solar simulator.

Which of these are worth actively managing

Worth managingSoiling in dusty/arid climates (periodic cleaning), string mismatch (module-level power electronics on partially shaded arrays), and DC wiring loss (proper cable sizing at design time).
Hard to avoidTemperature derate is largely a function of climate and mounting — some improvement from rack-mounting with airflow underneath a roof, but the loss can't be engineered away entirely.
Fixed by designInverter efficiency and AC wiring loss are set once at equipment selection and installation — choose a quality inverter and correctly sized conductors, then it's done.

Practical checklist when sizing a system

  • 1Never size a system off pure nameplate wattage — apply a realistic system-loss derate (commonly 14–18%) to estimate actual annual energy production.
  • 2Check the panel datasheet's temperature coefficient (%/°C) when comparing panels for a hot climate — a lower (less negative) coefficient performs better in sustained heat, even at equal STC wattage.
  • 3For roof-mount arrays, leave an air gap under the panels where possible — airflow underneath measurably reduces operating cell temperature versus a flush-mounted array.
  • 4Budget a periodic cleaning schedule in dusty, low-rainfall, or high-pollen environments — soiling loss compounds silently between rain events.
  • 5Size DC conductors for a target voltage drop (commonly ≤2%) rather than just meeting minimum ampacity — the extra copper pays for itself over the system's lifetime.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Nameplate wattage is a lab measurement at STC (1000 W/m², 25°C, AM1.5) — not a field prediction.
  • ✓Cell temperature is usually the largest single derate factor, since rooftop cells commonly run 20–25°C hotter than ambient air.
  • ✓A realistic all-in system derate lands around 75–85% of nameplate DC capacity delivered as AC energy — treat that as the expected baseline, not a fault to troubleshoot.
  • ✓Only some derate factors (soiling, mismatch, DC wiring) are meaningfully within an installer's control after equipment is chosen — the rest is climate and physics.
