Transformer and Generator Sizing: Why Motor Starting Decides the Genset, Not the Running Load

A generator sized to comfortably carry the running load can still stall or trip the moment the largest motor tries to start.

Transformer sizing: a demand-factor roll-up

kVAxfmr  =  (Pconnected × DF)  /  PF
Pconnected = sum of connected load · DF = demand factor (not everything runs at once, or at full nameplate) · PF = system power factor

Connected load is the sum of every nameplate rating on the installation — a figure that's almost never actually drawn all at once. Demand factor knocks that down to a realistic coincident maximum, and dividing by power factor converts real power to apparent power, since the transformer has to be sized in kVA, not kW. The result is rounded up to the next standard transformer size (100, 160, 200, 250, 315… kVA) rather than a bespoke figure.

Generator sizing starts the same way — then diverges

The essential load calculation for a standby generator follows the identical logic: sum the loads that must stay powered during an outage, apply a demand factor, convert to kVA. Where it diverges is that a generator is not a stiff source the way the utility grid effectively is. Its own internal (transient) reactance means that a sudden current draw — like a large motor starting — causes a real, measurable dip in its output voltage while the alternator's excitation system catches up.

Why motor starting is the real sizing driver

A motor starting direct-on-line draws its locked rotor current — typically 5 to 7 times running current — for the brief period before it accelerates to speed. Against a generator's transient reactance Xd′, that surge produces a voltage dip roughly proportional to the ratio of starting kVA to the generator's own kVA rating:

ΔV  ≈  (kVAstart × Xd′)  /  kVAgenset
kVAstart = motor starting kVA (locked rotor) · Xd′ = generator transient reactance (per unit) · kVAgenset = generator's own rating

If that dip exceeds what the rest of the connected equipment can tolerate — commonly 15–20% — contactors can drop out, VFDs can trip on undervoltage, and lighting visibly flickers. The only ways to fix it are a bigger (lower-impedance) generator, a soft starter or VFD to cut the motor's starting current, or both. This is exactly why a genset carrying the same running kW as the transformer it backs up is very often sized noticeably larger — the running load was never the constraint.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Transformer sizing (connected load × demand factor ÷ PF) is a load roll-up with no motor-starting consideration needed — the utility source is effectively stiff.
  • ✓A generator's own internal reactance means large motor starting currents pull its output voltage down, unlike a grid connection.
  • ✓The starting-voltage-dip check, not the running load, is frequently what actually sizes a standby generator.
  • ✓Soft starters or VFDs on the largest motor reduce starting kVA and can let a smaller, cheaper generator do the job.
