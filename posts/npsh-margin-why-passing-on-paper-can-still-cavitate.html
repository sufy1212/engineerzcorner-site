NPSH Margin: Why 'NPSHa > NPSHr' Isn't Enough on Its Own

NPSHr on a pump curve isn't the point where cavitation starts — it's a point where a small amount of it is already tolerated.

Sizing the pump itself

Phydraulic  =  ρ × g × Q × H
ρ = fluid density · g = gravitational acceleration · Q = flow rate · H = total dynamic head

Hydraulic power is the actual work being done on the fluid — lifting it and pushing it against friction losses. Shaft power divides that by the pump's own efficiency (Pshaft = Phydraulic / ηpump), and the motor is then sized with margin above shaft power. That part is a relatively clean calculation once the duty point (flow and head) is known. NPSH is the less intuitive half of pump sizing.

NPSHa vs NPSHr — two different things being compared

NPSH available (NPSHa) is entirely a property of the suction-side installation: atmospheric pressure, the fluid's vapour pressure at its operating temperature, static lift or flooded suction height, and friction losses in the suction piping. NPSH required (NPSHr) is entirely a property of the pump itself, published on its performance curve by the manufacturer, and it rises with flow rate. The basic check is simply NPSHa > NPSHr at the duty point — but that comparison alone understates the real risk.

Why NPSHr on the curve already has some cavitation built in

NPSHr isn't measured at the point where cavitation first begins — it's measured at a defined, small performance degradation, conventionally a 3% drop in developed head, caused by vapour bubbles already forming and collapsing inside the pump. In other words, the published NPSHr value is a point where mild cavitation is already occurring, just not yet enough to noticeably hurt performance. Operating exactly at NPSHa = NPSHr means running right at that threshold, with essentially zero margin against the erosion and noise that come with sustained cavitation — see the companion piece on pump cavitation causes and prevention for what that damage looks like in practice.

This is why standard practice (per the Hydraulic Institute and most pump vendors) calls for a margin beyond the bare comparison — commonly a minimum factor around 1.3× NPSHr, or an absolute margin of roughly 0.6 m, whichever governs. A design that shows NPSHa just barely exceeding NPSHr on paper has effectively no real-world margin at all.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Hydraulic power comes from flow, head and density; shaft power divides by pump efficiency to size the motor.
  • ✓NPSHa depends on the suction-side installation; NPSHr depends on the pump itself and comes from its performance curve.
  • ✓NPSHr is measured at a defined head-drop tolerance, meaning mild cavitation is already occurring right at that published value.
  • ✓Apply a real margin beyond NPSHa > NPSHr — a factor around 1.3× or an absolute margin around 0.6 m — not just the bare comparison.
