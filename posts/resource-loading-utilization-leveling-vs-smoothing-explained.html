Resource Loading and Utilization

Utilization is a simple ratio, but it is easy to read wrongly. A team can average 90% and still have two people drowning, and a person at 100% is not a safe person: they are a queue waiting to happen.

The utilization ratio

Utilizationpercent
=
Allocated hoursassigned work
÷
Available hoursreal capacity

Allocated hours are the work assigned in the period (usually a week). Available hours should be the time the person can really give to the project, not the length of the working week. Someone with 40 contracted hours, 6 of them lost to meetings and admin, has 34 available. Allocate 36 hours and they are at 36 ÷ 34 = 106%, over-allocated, although 36 ÷ 40 = 90% looks comfortable.

Over 100% is a leading indicator of missed dates and burnout, well before status reports show it. A low figure, say under 70%, may be real spare capacity or work that is not tracked at task level, and either way it deserves a look.

Why the team average misleads

Pooling the team gives one number: total allocated divided by total available. Here is a team of four, one of them part-time:

PersonAllocatedAvailableUtilization
A52 h40 h130%
B24 h40 h60%
C26 h40 h65%
D25 h20 h125%
Team127 h140 h90.7%
A comfortable team figure can hide an overloaded person The team shows 90.7%, yet A is at 130% and D at 125%, while B and C sit at 60% and 65%. The simple average of the four percentages is 95%, a different number again, because the part-time person counts equally. Read the individual rows first, and use the pooled figure only to see whether the team has enough capacity in total.

Moving work from A and D to B and C could fix this, but only if the skills match and the tasks can be done by someone else. Total capacity is not the same as the right capacity in the right place.

Look week by week

One snapshot is not a plan, because allocation changes as tasks start and finish. The same person over four weeks, with 40 hours of capacity a week:

WeekAllocatedUtilization
Week 130 h75%
Week 252 h130%
Week 348 h120%
Week 420 h50%
Total150 h93.8% of 160 h

The four-week average is 93.8%, which looks fine, but weeks 2 and 3 are overloaded by 12 and 8 hours. Total overload is 20 hours, and weeks 1 and 4 have 10 and 20 hours spare, so the work fits if some of it can move. That is what leveling and smoothing do.

Smoothing vs leveling

Resource smoothingResource leveling
GoalReduce peaks and troughs in demandStay within resource limits, removing over-allocation
MethodMove work within free and total floatDelay tasks, split them, or reassign until the limit holds
End dateFixedMay move later
Critical pathUnchangedMay change or lengthen
Fully fixes over-allocation?Not always, only where float existsYes, by definition, at a cost in time

For the weekly example, smoothing could move 10 hours of week 2 work into week 1 and 10 hours of weeks 2 and 3 work into week 4, giving 40, 40, 40 and 30 hours (100%, 100%, 100%, 75%) with the same end date, provided those tasks have float. If the weeks 2 and 3 tasks are critical, there is no float to use, and the only way to reach 100% is leveling, which means accepting a later finish. A common approach is to smooth everything that has float and then level what remains. Smoothing consumes float, so recalculate the critical path afterwards: a path that used up all its float is now critical.

Why 100% is not the target

Work arrives unevenly and takes varying lengths of time. In the simplest queueing model (one server, random arrivals and service times), the average wait before a task starts, as a multiple of the average task time, is ρ / (1 − ρ), where ρ is utilization:

UtilizationAverage wait, in task lengths
50%1.0 ×
70%2.3 ×
80%4.0 ×
90%9.0 ×
95%19.0 ×

Real projects are not a textbook queue, but the shape is the point: delays grow slowly up to about 70–80% and then explode towards 100%. A person planned at 100% has no room for interruptions, rework or a late dependency, so the schedule slips. This is the reason behind flagging both ends of the range.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Using 40 hours as available capacityMeetings, leave and admin are ignored, so everyone looks under-loaded
Reading only the team averageIndividual overloads are hidden by spare capacity elsewhere
Planning anyone at 100%There is no slack for variation, so small delays build into large ones
Treating low utilization as free capacityIt may be untracked work, or the wrong skills for the tasks that need people
Smoothing without checking floatUsing up float can turn a non-critical path critical
One snapshot instead of a timelinePeaks in individual weeks are missed

Key takeaways

  • ✓Utilization = allocated ÷ available hours, where available means real project time, not the working week.
  • ✓Check each person and each week. A team figure of 90% can sit on top of 130% and 125% rows.
  • ✓Smoothing works within float and keeps the end date; leveling enforces limits and may extend it.
  • ✓Do not plan to 100%: in a simple queue, waits grow as ρ/(1 − ρ), which is 9 task lengths at 90%.
  • ✓Use the flag thresholds (over 100%, under 70%) as prompts to investigate, not as fixed rules.
