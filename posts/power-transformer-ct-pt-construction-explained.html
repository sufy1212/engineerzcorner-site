Power Transformer, CT & PT: Internal Construction

A power transformer, a current transformer, and a potential transformer all rely on the same core-and-winding principle — but what's built around that core, and what job each one is actually doing, is completely different.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical / Transformers
01
A power transformer moves real power — a CT and a PT don't; they only reproduce a scaled-down, isolated copy of the current or voltage for meters and protection relays to read.
02
CTs and PTs are built for opposite failure modes: never open-circuit a CT secondary, and never short-circuit a PT secondary — each one is dangerous in the way the other one isn't.
03
Accuracy class (0.2 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 5P / 10P etc.) tells you how far the instrument's reading can drift from the true value — metering and protection duties use different classes for a reason.
04
The Buchholz relay and conservator tank exist only on oil-filled power transformers — CTs and PTs are small enough to be dry-type or resin-cast and skip that entire cooling/protection stack.

Why these three are grouped together

A power transformer, a current transformer (CT), and a potential transformer (PT, also called a VT) all work on the same principle — mutual induction between a primary and a secondary winding wound around a shared magnetic core, with no direct electrical connection between the two sides. Where they diverge is purpose. A power transformer's job is to actually transfer bulk electrical power, stepping voltage up for transmission or down for distribution. A CT and a PT transfer no meaningful power at all — their only job is to take a high current or high voltage that's too large and too dangerous to measure directly, and reproduce a small, standardized, electrically isolated version of it for meters and protection relays to safely read.

1. Power transformer — internal construction

Steps voltage up or down while providing electrical isolation between circuits at the same frequency.

  1. 1
    Silica Gel BreatherDries the air drawn into the conservator tank as oil level changes, keeping moisture out of the insulating oil.
  2. 2
    Conservator TankAn expansion reservoir mounted above the main tank that accommodates oil volume changes as the transformer heats and cools.
  3. 3
    Buchholz RelaySits in the pipe between the main tank and conservator; detects gas from minor internal faults and trips the breaker on major ones.
  4. 4
    HV BushingInsulated porcelain/composite terminal that brings the high-voltage winding connection out through the grounded tank wall.
  5. 5
    LV BushingSame function as the HV bushing, sized and rated for the lower-voltage winding side instead.
  6. 6
    Tap Changer (OLTC)Adjusts the effective turns ratio to regulate output voltage; an on-load tap changer does this without de-energizing the unit.
  7. 7
    Main TankThe grounded steel enclosure that holds the core, windings, and insulating oil, and contains any internal fault.
  8. 8
    Cooling RadiatorsFinned panels that circulate and cool the oil, rejecting heat generated by core and winding losses to the air.
  9. 9
    Transformer OilInsulates and cools simultaneously — fills the tank, surrounds the windings, and carries heat out to the radiators.
  10. 10
    Insulation PaperWraps the winding conductors; combined with oil this forms the primary dielectric barrier inside the transformer.
  11. 11
    LV WindingsThe low-voltage coil, usually wound closest to the core for shorter insulation distance and easier cooling.
  12. 12
    HV WindingsThe high-voltage coil, wound outside the LV winding where more insulation clearance is available.
  13. 13
    Laminated Steel CoreThin, insulated steel laminations that carry the magnetic flux linking the windings while limiting eddy current losses.

2. Current transformer (CT) — internal construction

Reduces high current to a standard value (1 A or 5 A) for protection relays and metering.

  1. 1
    Primary ConductorThe bus bar or cable carrying full system current, usually passed straight through the CT's core window.
  2. 2
    Magnetic CoreA toroidal laminated core that links the primary conductor's field to the secondary winding.
  3. 3
    Secondary WindingMany turns wound around the core, producing a scaled-down current (1 A or 5 A) proportional to the primary.
  4. 4
    InsulationSeparates primary and secondary sides electrically, rated for the full system voltage even though the CT only measures current.
  5. 5
    Terminal BoxHouses the secondary winding connections where meters, relays, or a shorting link are wired in.
  6. 6
    Mounting BaseFixes the CT to switchgear, a bus duct, or a support structure.
ParameterTypical value
Typical primary current50 A – 5000 A
Typical secondary current1 A or 5 A
Accuracy class0.2 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 5P / 10P
Frequency50 / 60 Hz

3. Potential transformer (PT / VT) — internal construction

Steps high voltage down to standard values (110 V or 63.5 V) for meters and protective relays.

  1. 1
    Primary WindingConnected across the high-voltage system it's measuring, with many turns to handle the full system voltage.
  2. 2
    Secondary WindingFewer turns than the primary, producing a standardized low voltage (110 V or 63.5 V) proportional to the system voltage.
  3. 3
    Magnetic CoreLinks flux between primary and secondary windings, similar in principle to a small power transformer core.
  4. 4
    InsulationProvides the internal dielectric barrier between the high-voltage primary and the low-voltage secondary.
  5. 5
    Porcelain / Composite InsulatorThe external housing that provides creepage distance and insulation against the surrounding air and weather.
  6. 6
    Terminal BoxHouses the secondary connections to meters, relays, and protective devices.
  7. 7
    Mounting BaseFixes the PT to a support structure, switchyard stand, or switchgear panel.
ParameterTypical value
Typical primary voltage3.3 kV – 132 kV
Typical secondary voltage110 V or 63.5 V
Accuracy class0.2 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 3P
Frequency50 / 60 Hz

Why CT & PT are essential

  • ✓Protects people and equipment by keeping meters and relays fully isolated from high-voltage, high-current primary circuits
  • ✓Enables accurate metering at safe, standardized secondary values regardless of the primary system's actual size
  • ✓Essential for protective relays and control — relays act on the scaled-down signal, not the raw system current or voltage
  • ✓Cost-effective and standardized — meters and relays can be built to common 1 A / 5 A / 110 V input ranges across any system size
Never operate a CT or PT on open circuit (secondary open) or beyond its rated burden. An open CT secondary can develop a dangerously high voltage across its terminals; an overloaded or shorted PT secondary loses accuracy and can overheat. Always short a CT's secondary before disconnecting a meter or relay from it.

Key takeaways

  • ✓All three share the same core-and-winding principle, but only the power transformer actually transfers bulk power — CTs and PTs just reproduce a safe, scaled copy for instruments.
  • ✓A power transformer's oil, conservator, Buchholz relay, and tap changer exist to handle and protect real power flow — CTs and PTs are small enough to skip most of that.
  • ✓CT secondaries must never be left open; PT secondaries must never be shorted — the two failure modes are opposite and both are dangerous.
  • ✓Accuracy class selection differs by duty — metering and protection use different classes because they need different things from the same measurement.
