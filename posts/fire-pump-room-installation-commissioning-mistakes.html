Fire Pump Room: 10 Common Installation & Commissioning Mistakes

A properly selected pump is not enough — the complete system around it has to be installed, tested and commissioned correctly. These are the ten mistakes that keep showing up in fire pump rooms, from valve position and suction arrangement through to housekeeping and accessibility.

A fire pump is selected against a duty point on a curve, but the room it sits in is a complete system — suction piping, isolation and check valves, pressure sensing lines, three separate pumps working together, controllers, and a discharge header feeding the building's fire network. Getting the pump right and getting the room right are two different jobs, and it's the second one where most commissioning problems actually show up. Here are ten mistakes worth checking for before any fire pump room is handed over.

The 10 mistakes

1

Incorrect valve position

Suction and discharge isolation valves must be left in the correct operating position. A valve that's part-closed or shut when it should be open quietly throttles the pump before it ever gets tested.

2

Improper suction arrangement

Undersized pipe, poor routing or the wrong fittings on the suction side create flow restrictions that show up as lost performance right at the pump — long after the piping itself is buried or boxed in.

3

Air in the suction line

Air pockets trapped in the suction piping cause serious operational problems once the pump starts. Correct piping arrangement and approved installation details are what keep the line fully primed.

4

Incorrect pressure settings

Jockey, main electric and diesel pump pressure settings all need to follow the approved design and hydraulic calculations — not a generic default. Wrong cut-in/cut-out points mean the wrong pump responds first, or none of them do.

5

Missing or incorrect check valves

Non-return valves prevent unwanted reverse flow and keep the intended system operation intact. Leaving one out — or installing it backwards — lets flow drift back where it shouldn't.

6

Pressure & sensing line issues

Blocked, damaged, leaking or wrongly connected sensing lines feed bad information to the pressure switches — and that breaks the automatic pump-start sequence the whole room depends on.

7

Diesel pump readiness

For diesel-driven pumps, fuel level, batteries, battery charger, engine condition, controller status, cooling system and exhaust arrangement all need verifying — not assuming — before the room can be signed off.

8

Poor test arrangement

Flow testing and pump performance verification need the correct approved test arrangement — a proper test header, with test equipment and gauges installed and calibrated as required, not an improvised setup.

9

Electrical & controller problems

Power supply, controller indications, alarms, and automatic/manual operation all have to be checked during commissioning, including how the system behaves under emergency arrangements — not just under normal power.

10

Poor housekeeping & accessibility

Fire pump rooms need to stay clean and accessible. Valves, controllers, gauges and equipment should be clearly identifiable, and nothing should block the access needed for operation and maintenance.

The key lesson

Every fire pump room follows the same chain, and every one of the ten mistakes above breaks a different link in it. Trace the water from the tank to the network and the whole system — and everything that can go wrong with it — falls into place.

FIRE WATER TANK→ SUCTION→ PUMPS→ CONTROLLERS→ DISCHARGE HEADER→ FIRE PROTECTION NETWORK

Every component matters — exact installation requirements, pump settings and testing procedures should always be based on the approved project design, hydraulic calculations, manufacturer requirements and applicable standards or codes.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A properly selected pump is not enough — the complete system around it must be installed, tested and commissioned correctly before handover.
  • ✓Valve position, suction arrangement and trapped air are installation issues that only surface once the pump is actually run — not while it sits on a datasheet.
  • ✓Pressure settings, sensing lines and check valves all exist to make the automatic jockey → electric → diesel start sequence work as designed.
  • ✓A diesel standby pump's reliability comes from fuel, batteries, charger, cooling and exhaust checks — not from assuming it will start.
  • ✓As fire protection / MEP engineers, the responsibility doesn't end at installing the equipment — it includes inspecting, testing, commissioning and verifying the complete system before handover.
