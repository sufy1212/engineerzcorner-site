Reading a Centrifugal Pump Curve: Head, Flow, Efficiency & the BEP

A pump's datasheet flow rate isn't a promise — it's one point on a curve. Where the pump actually operates is decided by where that curve crosses the system it's connected to.

The head-capacity curve

The main curve on any pump datasheet plots total head (usually in meters or feet) on the vertical axis against flow rate (capacity, in L/s, m³/h, or GPM) on the horizontal axis. For a fixed-speed centrifugal pump, this curve falls from left to right: at zero flow (a fully closed valve, "shutoff head") the pump produces its maximum head, and head drops as flow increases, because more of the pump's energy goes into moving fluid rather than building pressure.

Manufacturers often print several curves on one chart for a pump family — different impeller diameters (trims) at the same casing and speed, each shifted up or down. Selecting a pump usually means picking the trim whose curve passes closest to the required duty point.

Where the pump actually operates: the system curve

Hsystem = Hstatic + k · Q²
Hstatic = fixed elevation/pressure the pump must overcome regardless of flow · k = system resistance coefficient (pipe friction, fittings, valves) · Q = flow rate

The system curve represents the head the piping network demands at each flow rate — it rises with the square of flow because friction losses grow with velocity squared. The pump's actual operating point is wherever the pump curve and the system curve cross; neither curve alone determines the answer. Change a valve position, add a strainer, or resize a pipe, and the system curve shifts, moving the operating point to a different spot on the same, unchanged pump curve.

Efficiency islands and the Best Efficiency Point

At the BEPLowest radial shaft loading, quietest operation, longest bearing/seal life, and the pump's rated efficiency is achieved — the ideal long-term operating flow.
Right of BEPHigher flow than optimal; increases NPSH required and risk of cavitation, plus higher motor loading — watch for motor overload on pumps selected too small.
Left of BEPLower flow than optimal; increases radial thrust on the shaft and can cause internal recirculation damage over time, even though power draw is lower.

Efficiency is shown as closed contour lines ("islands") overlaid on the head-capacity curve, each ring representing a band of efficiency (e.g. 70%, 75%, 80%). The BEP sits at the center of the highest-efficiency island. A good selection lands the design operating point inside or very close to that island — not just anywhere the head-capacity curve happens to satisfy the duty requirement.

Reading the curve: a practical checklist

  • 1Plot the actual system curve (static head + friction losses vs. flow) before picking a pump — a pump selected against a guessed duty point often lands well off its BEP once installed.
  • 2Check where the operating point falls relative to the efficiency islands, not just whether the head-capacity curve passes through the required duty point.
  • 3Cross-reference the NPSH-required curve at the actual operating flow against the system's NPSH available — both change with flow, so check them at the real operating point, not just at rated flow.
  • 4For multi-trim pump families, prefer a trim that lands the duty point near the BEP over one that merely "fits" — oversized trims commonly get throttled, wasting the affinity-law energy savings a smaller trim would have kept.
  • 5Recheck the operating point any time the system changes — a new strainer, a partially closed valve, or pipe fouling all shift the system curve and move the pump off its original design point.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A pump curve is a range of possible operating points, not a single guaranteed flow — the actual operating point is set by where it crosses the system curve.
  • ✓The Best Efficiency Point isn't just about energy cost — operating far from it increases mechanical wear even when flow and head both look acceptable.
  • ✓NPSH required rises with flow, so cavitation risk from insufficient suction head concentrates at the high-flow end of the curve.
  • ✓Any change to the piping system moves the operating point along the same pump curve — re-verify against the curve whenever the system changes.
