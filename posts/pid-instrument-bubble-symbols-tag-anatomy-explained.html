P&ID Instrument Bubbles: Line Types & Tag Anatomy Explained

Every circle on a P&ID is doing two jobs at once — the line underneath tells you where the instrument physically lives, and the letters and numbers inside tell you what it measures and does. Miss either one and a walkdown turns into guesswork.

Engineerz CorneRReference · ISA S5.1
01
The bubble's border line — none, solid, or dashed — tells you where the instrument is physically mounted, not what it measures.
02
The letters inside the bubble follow ISA S5.1: the first letter is the measured variable, the following letters are the function.
03
The number under the letters is the loop number — every instrument tied to the same control loop shares it.
04
Four transmitters on one column read as TT01, TT02, TT03, TT04 — same function letters, different loop numbers.

A P&ID doesn't waste space explaining itself — every symbol is compressed into a small circle, and that circle is carrying more information than it looks like at first glance. Read it wrong during a field walk and you'll go looking for a transmitter in the control room that's actually sitting on the column, or vice versa. This is the part of P&ID literacy that's easy to skim past and expensive to get wrong on site.

The instrument bubble: three line types

Same tag, three different circles — each one says something different about where the instrument is.

No line
In the field
The instrument is located in the field, near the process & the operator.
Solid line
In a control room
The instrument is located in a control room, accessible to the operator.
Dotted line
Not directly accessible
The instrument is not directly accessible — e.g. embedded in a DCS or PLC, not a physical panel-mounted device.

This distinction matters most when you're planning a commissioning walkdown or troubleshooting a loop under time pressure. A no-line tag means someone has to physically walk to the field to read, calibrate or bypass it — budget the travel time. A solid-line tag means it lives on a hardwired panel face in the control room, so it's checkable without leaving the desk. A dashed-line tag is the trickiest of the three: it exists logically inside the DCS or PLC configuration rather than as a discrete physical device, so "accessing" it means opening the right graphic or faceplate on the HMI, not walking to a panel at all.

Some references also use a square, or a square inside a circle, for shared-display DCS points and computer-function blocks — the logic is the same: the enclosure shape and line style describe location and accessibility, never the measured variable.

Anatomy of an instrument tag

A tag like TT01 packs three separate pieces of information into five characters.

First letter — T (Temperature)The parameter being measured or controlled. Examples: F = Flow, P = Pressure, L = Level, T = Temperature.
Second letter — T (Transmitter)The type of control device being used. Examples: T = Transmitter, C = Controller, V = Valve, I = Indicator.
01 — Loop numberThe logical numerator. A system of four temperature transmitters on the same equipment is classified as TT01, TT02, TT03 & TT04.

Stack a variable letter with a function letter and the tag reads itself: FT is a flow transmitter, PT is a pressure transmitter, TC is a temperature controller, FV is a flow-actuated control valve. An I/P tag is a slight exception — it's a current-to-pneumatic converter, taking a 4–20 mA electrical signal from a controller and converting it into a 3–15 psi pneumatic signal that a spring-diaphragm valve actuator can actually use.

Reading a typical control loop

Put the bubble convention and the tag anatomy together and a control loop on a P&ID reads as one continuous sentence rather than a set of disconnected symbols:

1A field-mounted sensor (no line under the tag, e.g. TT02) measures the process variable directly on the equipment.
2A dashed line carries that signal to a controller (TC02) sitting logically inside the DCS/PLC — not a physical panel instrument.
3The controller's output signal passes through an I/P converter (I/P02) to translate it into a pneumatic signal.
4That pneumatic signal drives the field-mounted actuator (FV01) on the control valve, closing the loop back to the process.

The same reading logic applies regardless of how many instruments are on the drawing. A feed tank with a flow controller and control valve (FC01 driving FV01), a set of column temperature transmitters (TT01–TT04) feeding a shared control system, and an independent pressure transmitter on a rotary compressor (PT44) are all just repeats of this same four-step pattern — sense, transmit, compute, actuate — at different points in the process.

Key takeaways

  • ✓No line = field-mounted, solid line = control-room panel-mounted, dashed line = logical/DCS point that isn't a discrete physical device.
  • ✓The first letter in a tag is the measured variable; the letters after it describe the device function — transmitter, controller, indicator, valve, switch or alarm.
  • ✓The loop number underneath is what ties multiple instruments together into one control loop, even when they sit on different symbols across the drawing.
  • ✓An I/P converter bridges the electrical and pneumatic worlds — expect one wherever a DCS/PLC output needs to drive a pneumatic actuator.
  • ✓Always confirm tag conventions against the project's own instrument index or legend sheet — ISA S5.1 is the common baseline, but plants adapt it.
