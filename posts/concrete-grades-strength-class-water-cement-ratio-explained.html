Concrete Grades Explained: What C25/30 Means and Why the Water-Cement Ratio Matters

C25/30 is a strength promise measured at 28 days. Here is how to read the grade, how cylinder and cube values differ, and why the water in the mix matters more than almost anything else.

Concrete is ordered by grade, and the grade is a promise about strength. The labels look cryptic, with C25/30, C30/37 and C35/45, but each one follows a simple rule, and the single biggest factor in whether a pour meets its grade is the amount of water in it.

Reading a grade such as C25/30

The first number: cylinder

The characteristic compressive strength, in MPa (N/mm²), measured on a 150 mm by 300 mm cylinder at 28 days. In C25/30, that is 25 MPa.

The second number: cube

The characteristic strength on a 150 mm cube at 28 days. In C25/30 that is 30 MPa. A cube reads higher because the shape restrains the sample more than a tall cylinder does.

The cylinder strength is roughly 80% of the cube strength. Design to Eurocode 2 uses the cylinder figure, while some older standards and national practice quote the cube figure, so check which one a drawing means. Characteristic strength is a statistical value: it is the level that results are expected to exceed in all but a small fraction of cases, not an average.

The water-cement ratio rules strength

w/c ratio = mass of water ÷ mass of cement

Cement paste needs water to hydrate and to make the mix workable. Abrams’ law says that for the same materials, a lower water-cement ratio gives a stronger and more durable concrete, and a higher ratio gives a weaker one. Typical mixes use ratios between about 0.45 and 0.60, balancing strength and workability, while ratios of 0.30 to 0.45 are used where high strength and durability matter, with admixtures to keep the mix workable.

A worked case: a mix with 320 kg of cement per cubic metre and a w/c ratio of 0.55 contains 320 × 0.55 = 176 litres of water per cubic metre, and is the sort of mix used for a C25/30 grade with CEM I cement. If a gang adds 10 litres of water per cubic metre to make the pour easier, the ratio rises to 186 ÷ 320 = 0.58. The concrete flows better and ends up weaker, and it is also more porous, so it resists weather and chlorides less well. The fix for stiff concrete is an admixture or a revised mix, not site water.

Choosing a grade

  • Structure. Foundations, slabs and stairs commonly use mid-range grades such as C25/30, with higher grades for heavily loaded beams and columns, and lean mixes for blinding. The design and the drawings set the figure.
  • Environment. Exposure to freezing, chlorides or sulphates calls for a lower maximum w/c ratio, a higher minimum cement content and sometimes a higher grade, as set by the exposure classes in EN 206 or the equivalent code.
  • Placement. Workability, usually measured by slump, has to suit the formwork and the reinforcement. Superplasticizers deliver it without extra water.

Why the 28-day test

Concrete keeps gaining strength after it is placed, and 28 days is the agreed age for testing and grading. The samples are cured under controlled conditions, so a good result on cubes or cylinders only counts if the real structure is cured well too. Poor curing in hot or windy weather can leave the surface weaker than the test specimens. Keep the concrete moist and protected during the first days.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Check whether a drawing quotes cylinder or cube strength before comparing grades.
  • ✓Control water carefully, since the w/c ratio governs strength and durability.
  • ✓Use admixtures, not site water, to improve workability.
  • ✓Cure the structure properly, because test specimens are cured better than most real pours.
