Transformer Turn Ratio (TTR) Test Explained

A TTR meter applies a small AC voltage to the HV winding and measures what comes out the LV side — a five-minute test that catches wrong connections, reversed polarity and shorted turns before a transformer is ever energized.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Transformer Commissioning
01
TTR applies a known AC voltage to the HV winding and measures the induced voltage on the LV winding — the ratio between the two should match the transformer's nameplate turns ratio.
02
The same test that confirms the ratio also confirms polarity and correct terminal connections, since a reversed or miswired winding shows up immediately as a wrong or unstable reading.
03
A shorted turn is one of the hardest transformer faults to see any other way — it pulls the measured ratio measurably off nameplate even though insulation resistance can look fine.
04
Acceptance is normally ±0.5% of nameplate ratio per phase, with the three phases balanced within 0.5% of each other — tighter, and a deviation almost always means investigate before energizing.

What TTR verifies

The turn ratio test exists to catch problems a transformer can have before it's ever put into service: winding ratios that don't match the nameplate, terminals wired to the wrong phase, reversed polarity, or turns shorted together inside a winding. None of these are things you can see from the outside, and several of them — a shorted turn especially — won't necessarily show up on an insulation resistance test either. TTR is comparative by nature: it measures the actual ratio and checks it against what the nameplate says it should be, for every phase and every tap position the transformer has.

Transformer terminals and polarity

A typical distribution transformer example — 11 kV / 433 V, vector group Dyn11 — has three HV terminals (U, V, W) and three LV terminals (u, v, w), each pair sharing a common core leg. Terminals of the same polarity are marked with a dot or matching terminal numbers: 1U pairs with 2u, 1V with 2v, 1W with 2w. That pairing is exactly what the TTR meter connects across — H (high) lead to the HV terminal, X (low) lead to the corresponding LV terminal — for each of the three phases in turn.

HV Winding

Test setup and connections

1
De-energize and isolateConfirm the transformer is fully de-energized and isolated from the system before any leads are connected.
2
Verify polarity marksCheck the nameplate and terminal markings so the H and X leads go on the correct, same-polarity pair.
3
Connect H to HV terminalH (High) lead to the HV terminal being tested first, e.g. 1U.
4
Connect X to LV terminalX (Low) lead to the corresponding LV terminal, e.g. 2u — same phase, same polarity pairing.
5
Select voltage ratio rangeSet the meter's range to suit the expected ratio so the reading resolves cleanly.
6
Take and record the readingThe meter applies AC to the HV winding, measures the induced LV voltage, and displays the calculated ratio.
7
Repeat for V–v and W–wMove the leads to the next phase pair and repeat, then repeat again for any other tap positions the transformer has.

The neutral point on the LV side (N) stays grounded throughout, the same way it would in service — the test doesn't disturb the transformer's normal earthing arrangement, only its energization state.

Example reading

Y1V – 2v11,000 / 433 = 25.40425.39−0.06%
B1W – 2w11,000 / 433 = 25.40425.40−0.02%

All three phases here land comfortably inside a ±0.5% band and stay within 0.5% of each other, which is exactly what a healthy transformer should show — small, consistent deviation attributable to instrument accuracy rather than a winding fault.

Acceptance and rejection criteria

✓ Acceptance criteria
  1. Measured turn ratio for each phase is within ±0.5% of the nameplate ratio.
  2. The ratio across all three phases is balanced — difference between any two phases ≤ 0.5%.
  3. Correct polarity is confirmed at every terminal pair.
⚠ Rejection criteria
  • Deviation greater than ±0.5% from the nameplate ratio.
  • Reversed or incorrect polarity.
  • Unbalanced ratio between phases greater than 0.5%.

Any deviation beyond these limits points to possible shorted turns, wrong connections, or an internal fault — none of which should be dismissed as instrument noise without investigating further.

Practical notes

  • Test all three phase pairs — U–u, V–v and W–w — not just one, since a fault can be isolated to a single phase.
  • Repeat the test at other tap positions if the transformer has an off-load or on-load tap changer.
  • Record ambient temperature and test conditions alongside the readings.
  • Ensure all connections are tight and clean before applying test voltage — a loose lead reads as a bad ratio that isn't actually there.
  • Use a calibrated TTR meter, and keep the calibration record with the test report.

Key takeaways

  • ✓TTR compares measured turns ratio against nameplate ratio for each phase and tap — it's a comparison test, not an absolute measurement standing alone.
  • ✓The same test simultaneously confirms winding ratio, polarity, and correct terminal connections.
  • ✓A shorted turn is one of the few transformer faults that TTR catches reliably where an insulation resistance test might not.
  • ✓±0.5% of nameplate per phase, and ≤0.5% between phases, is the standard bar — anything outside it gets investigated before the transformer is energized.
