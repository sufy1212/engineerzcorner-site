Orifice Plate Flow Sizing Explained

An orifice plate does not measure flow. It makes a pressure drop that grows with the square of the flow, and a transmitter and an equation turn that back into a flow reading. Sizing is choosing how big that pressure drop should be.

01
Flow is proportional to the square root of differential pressure. Doubling the flow needs four times the DP, and half the flow gives a quarter of the signal.
02
The beta ratio, bore diameter over pipe diameter, sets the trade-off: a smaller beta gives a bigger signal and more permanent pressure loss, a larger beta the opposite.
03
Q = Cd × (π/4)d² × √[2ΔP / (ρ(1 − β⁴))], with Cd around 0.6 for a sharp-edged plate. For gases an expansibility factor also applies.
04
Because of the square-root law, accuracy falls off fast at low flow. Typical turndown for an orifice meter is only about 3:1 to 5:1.

How an orifice plate measures flow

An orifice plate is a thin plate with a precisely machined hole (the bore) clamped between pipe flanges. The fluid accelerates through the bore, so its pressure falls. A differential pressure (DP) transmitter measures the drop between a tapping upstream and one at or just downstream of the plate, and the flow follows from the energy balance, with a discharge coefficient Cd to cover the real behaviour of the jet.

Q = Cd × (π/4) d² × √[ 2ΔP / ( ρ (1 − β⁴) ) ] where β = d / D. The (1 − β⁴) term is the velocity-of-approach correction for the fluid already moving in the pipe.

Cd for a sharp-edged plate is about 0.6 and depends on β, Reynolds number and the tap arrangement. The standard (ISO 5167-2) gives Cd as an equation of those quantities, and a simple fixed 0.6 is only good for rough sizing.

Worked example: flow from a bore and a DP

Water (998 kg/m³) in a 100 mm pipe through a 50 mm bore, measuring 25 kPa, with Cd = 0.6.

Sizing the other way: bore for a design flow

To size a plate, fix the flow you need to measure at its maximum and the DP you are willing to have there, then solve for the bore. For 50 m³/h of water at 40 kPa in the same 100 mm pipe, iterating the equation gives d ≈ 55.9 mm, β ≈ 0.56. The solution cannot be written in closed form because β appears inside the equation, which is why tools solve it by iteration.

The choice of design DP is a compromise. A bigger DP at full flow gives a stronger signal and better low-flow performance, but it also means more permanent pressure loss, which is lost pumping energy. In practice the design DP is chosen from the available transmitter ranges and the pressure loss the process can tolerate.

Why turndown is limited

Since DP follows the square of the flow, the signal collapses at low flow. Suppose the transmitter's error is ±0.1% of its span, and the flow range is set so full flow equals the full DP span. The flow error is roughly half the relative DP error:

FlowDP as % of spanApprox. flow error (reading)
100%100.00%±0.05%
50%25.00%±0.20%
25%6.25%±0.80%
10%1.00%±5.00%

The error stays small down to about 50% flow, then grows quickly, and at 10% flow the same transmitter gives around ±5%. That is the reason orifice meters are normally quoted with a turndown of about 3:1 to 5:1 (flow range of about 20–33% to 100%). The error values are for illustration; real uncertainty also includes the discharge coefficient, the plate condition and installation effects.

Limits of the standard and of a simple calculation

ItemTypical requirement
Bored greater than 12.5 mm
Pipe diameterD between 50 mm and 1000 mm (ISO 5167-2)
Beta ratio0.1 to 0.75 in the standard; 0.2 to 0.75 in normal practice
Reynolds numberA minimum applies, for example above 5,000 for β of 0.2 to 0.45 and above 10,000 for larger β (one manufacturer's limits for corner-tap plates)
FluidSingle-phase, subsonic, non-pulsating flow
InstallationLong straight pipe upstream and downstream, with lengths depending on the fittings and β

Outside these limits, or for gas, steam, fiscal metering and custody transfer, use a full calculation to ISO 5167 (or AGA Report 3 for natural gas). For compressible fluids an expansibility factor below 1 corrects for the gas expanding as its pressure falls across the plate. A fixed Cd, as in the quick sizing above and in the calculator, is for preliminary work and field checks.

Mistakes that catch people out

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Using flow-condition density incorrectlyDensity must be at actual line pressure and temperature, especially for gases
Ignoring expansibility on gasThe calculated flow is too high when the pressure drop is significant
Choosing β outside 0.2–0.75The constant Cd assumption stops holding
Sizing the transmitter for full flow onlyLow-flow operation then falls in the noisy bottom of the span
Worn or damaged upstream edgeCd shifts, and the meter reads wrong with no visible fault
Short straight runsSwirl and distorted profiles change the reading

Key takeaways

  • ✓Q = Cd × (π/4)d² × √[2ΔP / (ρ(1 − β⁴))]; flow goes with √ΔP.
  • ✓β trades signal strength against permanent pressure loss; keep it between about 0.2 and 0.75.
  • ✓Turndown is limited to about 3:1 to 5:1 because DP falls as the square of the flow.
  • ✓Use the full ISO 5167 or AGA-3 calculation for gas, fiscal and high-accuracy work.
  • ✓Confirm limits and Cd equations against the standard edition and the plate manufacturer.
