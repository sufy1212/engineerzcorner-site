Ingress Protection (IP) Ratings Explained

Two digits after "IP" decide whether an enclosure survives its environment or fails in it. Here's exactly what each digit means, how to read one, and how IP ratings line up against NEMA types.

02
Each digit is a floor, not a bonus — a rating of 6 in a category means every level below it is also satisfied, nothing more.

Two digits, two completely separate tests

An IP rating looks like a single number, but it's really two independent test results stapled together. The first digit answers "how well does this keep solid objects and dust out?" and the second digit answers "how well does this keep water out?" — they're tested separately, rated separately, and neither one implies anything about the other. An enclosure can be excellent against dust and mediocre against water, or the reverse; the two-digit code is what lets you see that at a glance instead of it being buried in a datasheet.

This matters because it's tempting to treat "IP66" as one abstract quality score. It isn't. It's shorthand for two concrete, standardized test conditions defined in IEC 60529 — and reading it correctly means reading both digits on their own terms.

1First Digit — Solid Protection (0–6)

  • Rates protection against solid foreign objects and dust, from large tools and body parts (low digits) down to fine dust (digit 5–6)
  • 6 is "dust tight" — no ingress of dust at all under the standard test, not just a reduced amount
  • A 6K rating (used mainly in automotive and severe-duty work) goes further still: completely dust tight under a stricter test

2Second Digit — Liquid Protection (0–9K)

  • Rates protection against water, from vertical drips (digit 1) through spray, jets, and immersion, up to high-pressure/high-temperature jets (9K)
  • The jump from 6 to 7 is a real change in test type: 6 is a powerful jet from outside, 7 is temporary immersion — direction and duration of exposure both change
  • A higher digit doesn't automatically imply protection against every lower-numbered scenario in every real installation — but by the standard's structure, each digit does certify all levels below it

3Optional Letters

  • Appended after the two digits to note extra conditions the base test doesn't cover — like access protection against hazardous parts (C) or a high-voltage test (H)
  • Supplementary information only — they add detail on top of the two-digit rating, they never substitute for a missing digit
  • Not every enclosure carries optional letters; most everyday ratings are stated as just the two digits (IP66, IP54, IP20)

Decoding an example: IP66

Splitting the code into its parts makes any rating easy to check against a datasheet.

IPIdentifies this as an Ingress Protection code under IEC 60529 — not a separate numbering system.
6First digit: dust tight — no ingress of dust under the standard test.
6Second digit: protected against powerful water jets (12.5 mm nozzle) from any direction.

IP vs. NEMA — not quite the same scale

NEMA types and IP ratings get treated as interchangeable, and they're close enough for a rough cross-check — but NEMA also folds in things IP doesn't test for at all, like corrosion resistance (NEMA 4X) and hazardous-location construction (NEMA 7/9), which have no direct IP equivalent. Treat any IP-to-NEMA mapping as an approximation for comparison, not a certification substitute.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Read an IP rating as two separate certifications, not one combined score — solid protection first, liquid protection second.
  • ✓Each digit is tested and specified against IEC 60529, so a rating means something checkable, not a marketing label.
  • ✓Optional letters after the digits add detail; they never replace or override the two core digits.
  • ✓Match the rating to the actual installation environment — over-specifying costs money, under-specifying costs reliability.
  • ✓NEMA and IP overlap but aren't identical — check both if a spec calls out one and the datasheet only gives the other.
