Essential Protection Settings of Air Circuit Breakers (ACB) Explained

Every ACB relay unit has the same handful of dials — long-time, short-time, instantaneous and ground fault. Proper configuration of these settings is what turns a breaker into a coordinated protection device rather than just an on/off switch for a big current.

01
ACB protection is built from four families of settings — long-time, short-time, instantaneous and ground fault — each targeting a different current level and time band.
02
Long-time settings (Ir, tr) protect against sustained overload; short-time (Isd, tsd) and instantaneous (Ii) settings handle short-circuit currents at progressively faster response.
03
Ground fault settings (Ig, tg) run on a separate, lower-threshold path to catch leakage and ground faults that phase overcurrent protection alone would miss.
04
None of these settings should be picked in isolation — they're derived from equipment ratings, system design and a coordination study with upstream and downstream devices.

An ACB's protection relay is really just a current-vs-time trip curve, built out of a handful of adjustable breakpoints. Each setting below moves one segment of that curve — get the segments right and devices trip in the right order; get one wrong and you either nuisance-trip on normal load swings or fail to clear a fault fast enough.

The eight settings

Grouped by protection family — long-time (overload), short-time (short-circuit), instantaneous (extreme fault) and ground fault.

1
Long-Time PickupIr

Defines the current level, as a multiple of the breaker's rated current (In), at which overload protection begins to act. Set too low and normal load fluctuations trip the breaker; set too high and sustained overload goes unprotected.

Typical range: 0.60–1.0 × In
2
Long-Time Delaytr

Determines how long the breaker tolerates a current above Ir before tripping, following an inverse-time (I²t) characteristic — longer delay at currents just above pickup, faster trip as the overload gets larger. This is what lets a breaker ride through a motor's normal starting inrush without tripping.

Typical range: 3–30 sec @ 6×Ir
3
Short-Time PickupIsd

Sets the threshold, as a multiple of Ir, above which the breaker switches from long-time overload logic into short-time short-circuit logic. This is the boundary between "overload" and "fault" as far as the relay is concerned.

Typical range: 2–10 × Ir
4
Short-Time Delaytsd

Adds an intentional, adjustable delay before tripping on a short-time overcurrent. This delay is the main tool for selective coordination — a delay long enough lets a downstream breaker clear a fault on its own circuit first, before the upstream ACB trips.

Typical range: 100–400 ms
5
Instantaneous PickupIi

Trips with no intentional delay once current exceeds this threshold — reserved for extremely high fault currents where waiting even a few hundred milliseconds risks equipment damage. On some coordinated systems this function is deliberately disabled on upstream breakers to preserve selectivity.

Typical range: 2–24 × In
6
Ground Fault PickupIg

Detects ground faults or earth-leakage currents at a threshold well below the phase overcurrent settings, since a ground fault often draws far less current than a phase-to-phase short but is no less dangerous.

Typical range: 0.1–0.8 × In
7
Ground Fault Delaytg

Introduces a delay before tripping on a ground fault, mirroring what tsd does for short-time protection — it exists so ground fault protection at different levels of the distribution system can be coordinated selectively instead of all tripping together.

Typical range: 0.1–0.4 sec
Long-time vs short-time vs instantaneousThese three sit on the same trip curve at increasing current and decreasing time — long-time protects against overload over seconds, short-time against faults over hundreds of milliseconds, instantaneous against extreme faults with no intentional delay at all.
Why ground fault runs separatelyGround fault protection doesn't sum the same way phase overcurrent does — it typically monitors the vector sum of the phase currents (or a dedicated CT), which is why Ig and tg are set and coordinated independently of Ir/tr and Isd/tsd.

Why these settings matter

Reliable protection
Protects against overloads, short circuits and ground faults across their respective current and time bands.
System stability
Ensures continuity of supply and minimizes downtime by tripping only the affected section.
Selective coordination
Works with other protective devices upstream and downstream so only the nearest breaker to a fault opens.
Equipment safety
Protects downstream equipment and personnel by clearing faults before thermal or mechanical damage occurs.

Settings should always be based on system design, equipment ratings and a proper coordination study — not left at factory defaults.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Long-time settings (Ir, tr) handle sustained overload with an inverse-time characteristic that rides through normal starting transients.
  • ✓Short-time settings (Isd, tsd) and instantaneous pickup (Ii) handle short-circuit currents at progressively faster response, with tsd doing most of the work for selective coordination.
  • ✓Ground fault settings (Ig, tg) run on their own lower-threshold path, separate from phase overcurrent protection.
  • ✓Instantaneous pickup is sometimes intentionally disabled on upstream breakers specifically to preserve selectivity with downstream devices.
  • ✓Every value here should come from the coordination study and equipment ratings for the specific installation — not from a typical-values table.
