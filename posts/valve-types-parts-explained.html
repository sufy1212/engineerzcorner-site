Industrial Valves Explained: Types & Parts

Every valve does the same basic job — start, stop, or throttle flow — but the way it does that job, and what it costs you in pressure drop, leak-tightness, and maintenance, changes completely with the type.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Mechanical / Valves
01
Every valve either isolates (fully open/shut) or throttles (controls flow at partial positions) — using an isolation valve to throttle is a common way to wreck its seat prematurely.
02
Valve type is chosen by what it needs to do to the flow, not just by pipe size — a gate valve and a globe valve can share a line size and pressure class and still be completely wrong for each other's job.
03
The stem and packing, not the disc or seat, are usually where a valve first starts leaking — external leakage past the stem is a maintenance issue long before internal seat leakage becomes one.
04
A check valve is the odd one out here — it has no handwheel or actuator at all, because flow itself is what opens and closes it.

What a valve actually does

A valve is a mechanical device inserted into a pipeline to start, stop, regulate, or redirect the flow of a fluid or gas. Underneath that one description sit two very different jobs: on/off isolation, where the valve just needs to seal tightly in two positions, and throttling control, where the valve needs a flow characteristic that behaves predictably somewhere in between. Picking a valve family that's built for the wrong one of those two jobs is the single most common valve-selection mistake on a project.

Types of valves

Different closure mechanisms trade off flow resistance, shutoff tightness, response speed, and how well they throttle — no single type wins at everything.

Gate Valve
Full isolation, straight bore
A flat gate slides across the flow path perpendicular to it. Gives a nearly unobstructed, low-pressure-drop bore when fully open. Built for on/off isolation — not meant to throttle, since a partly-open gate erodes and chatters.
Globe Valve
Throttling & frequent operation
Flow takes an S-shaped path through a movable disc and a fixed seat, which is exactly why it's good at throttling — that geometry gives a predictable, controllable flow characteristic, at the cost of higher pressure drop than a gate valve.
Ball Valve
Quick quarter-turn shutoff
A bored sphere rotates a quarter turn to align or block the flow path. Fast operation, tight bubble-tight shutoff, and low pressure drop when open — a default choice for isolation on gas, liquid, and utility lines alike.
Butterfly Valve
Compact, large-diameter lines
A disc rotates on a central shaft within the flow path itself. Compact and light for its size, quick quarter-turn operation, and economical at large diameters — common on HVAC water and low-to-medium pressure process lines.
Check Valve
One-directional flow only
No handwheel, no actuator — flow itself pushes the disc, flap, or ball open, and reverse flow (or its absence) closes it. Purely there to stop backflow, protecting pumps and upstream equipment from reversed process fluid.
Plug Valve
Quarter-turn, abrasive service
A tapered or cylindrical plug with a bore rotates to open or block flow — mechanically similar in concept to a ball valve, but the plug's larger sealing surface holds up better in slurry, abrasive, or particulate-laden service.
Needle Valve
Precise, low-flow metering
A slender tapered stem seats into a small, precisely machined orifice, giving very fine control over low flow rates — common on instrumentation lines, sample points, and gauge isolation where exact metering matters more than capacity.
Diaphragm Valve
Corrosive & hygienic service
A flexible diaphragm presses down onto a weir or seat to shut off flow, with no packed stem in contact with the process fluid — ideal for corrosive, abrasive, or sanitary/hygienic service where a stem leak path can't be tolerated.
Pressure Relief Valve
Overpressure protection
Spring-loaded to stay shut under normal pressure and pop open automatically once system pressure exceeds its set point, dumping excess pressure to protect equipment — not an operator-controlled valve at all.

Main parts of a valve

  1. 1
    BodyThe pressure-containing shell that holds all internal parts and connects into the pipeline.
  2. 2
    BonnetCloses the top of the body and houses the stem, typically bolted or threaded on for access to internals.
  3. 3
    StemTransmits motion from the handwheel or actuator down to the closure element inside.
  4. 4
    Disc / ball / plug / wedgeThe actual closure element that blocks or permits flow, named differently by valve type.
  5. 5
    SeatThe surface the closure element presses against to form a tight seal when closed.
  6. 6
    Packing / glandSeals around the stem where it passes through the bonnet, preventing external leakage.
  7. 7
    Actuator / handwheelProvides the operating force — manual handwheel, or pneumatic, electric, or hydraulic actuator for automated valves.
  8. 8
    GasketsSeal the body-to-bonnet joint and the pipeline flange connections against the process fluid.

Actuation methods

1Manual — handwheel or lever, operated directly by a person, no external power needed.
2Pneumatic — compressed air drives a piston or diaphragm actuator; fast, common where instrument air is already available.
3Electric — a motor-driven actuator turns or lifts the stem; suited to remote or infrequent operation without a compressed-air supply.
4Hydraulic — fluid pressure drives the actuator; used where very high operating force is needed, such as large or high-pressure valves.
5Self-actuated — check and pressure relief valves are moved directly by process conditions, with no external actuator at all.

Selection checklist

  • 1Duty — on/off isolation or throttling control
  • 2Fluid type — clean, corrosive, abrasive, or hygienic service
  • 3Pressure and temperature rating required
  • 4Line size and available space for the valve and actuator
  • 5Required shutoff class / allowable leakage rate
  • 6Actuation method — manual, pneumatic, electric, or hydraulic
  • 7Body and trim material compatibility with the process fluid

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓External leakage at the stem packing — often the first sign of wear, well before the seat itself leaks
  • ✓Seat wear or erosion from being used to throttle a valve that wasn't designed for it
  • ✓Actuator response lag or loss of full travel, indicating linkage or air-supply issues on pneumatic types
  • ✓Check valve slam or chatter caused by an oversized valve for the actual flow velocity
  • ✓Corrosion or erosion of body and trim in aggressive or abrasive service
  • ✓Handwheel or actuator not fully seating the closure element at end of travel
  • ✓Flange and body-to-bonnet gasket condition during any valve that's opened for maintenance
Most valve problems trace back to using the wrong family for the job — a gate valve throttled for years, or a globe valve expected to give a gate valve's low pressure drop, both fail slowly and predictably.

Common applications

Process isolation HVAC chilled/hot water systems Pump discharge backflow protection Instrumentation & sample lines Steam and condensate systems Boiler and pressure vessel overpressure protection Water and wastewater treatment Corrosive chemical transfer

Key takeaways

  • ✓Every valve isolates or throttles — match the family to the job, since using an isolation valve to throttle wears it out prematurely.
  • ✓Gate and ball give low pressure drop and tight shutoff; globe and needle give controllable, predictable throttling.
  • ✓Check and pressure relief valves are self-actuated — process conditions operate them, not a handwheel or actuator.
  • ✓Stem packing is usually the first thing to leak — inspect it before assuming the seat itself has failed.
