What a Load Really Costs to Run

Nameplate kW times hours is where an estimate starts, not where it ends. The real cost depends on how hard the load actually runs, how efficient it is, and which part of the tariff the last kilowatt-hour lands in.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Energy
01
Energy = input power × hours. Input power is shaft or output power divided by efficiency, not the nameplate rating.
02
Most loads run below nameplate. A load factor of 0.75 and a 93% efficient motor turn a 30 kW rating into about 24 kW drawn from the supply.
03
With tiered tariffs, the rate that matters for savings is the marginal rate of the top block, not the average (effective) rate on the bill.
04
Demand charges, fixed fees, taxes and time-of-use periods are billed on top of energy and are not in a simple kWh × rate estimate.

From nameplate to metered power

A motor nameplate gives the rated shaft output. The utility meter sees the electrical input, which is larger because of losses, and smaller than nameplate whenever the load is below full output:

Input kW = Rated kW × load factor ÷ efficiency, then kWh = input kW × hours.

Worked example: a 30 kW motor driving a pump at 75% load, with an assumed efficiency of 93% at that load (take the real figure from the motor datasheet), running 16 hours a day for 26 days a month.

StepWorkingResult
Input power30 × 0.75 ÷ 0.9324.19 kW
Daily energy24.19 × 16 h387.1 kWh
Monthly energy387.1 × 26 days10,065 kWh
Naive nameplate estimate30 × 16 × 2612,480 kWh (24% too high)

Using the nameplate overstates this motor's energy by about 24%. For a more accurate input, use measured current and voltage with the power factor, or a power meter, instead of assuming a load factor.

Flat rate vs tiered rate

At a flat 0.12 per kWh the month costs 10,065 × 0.12 = 1,207.74. A two-block tariff charges a lower rate up to a monthly limit and a higher rate above it. With the first 5,000 kWh at 0.10 and everything above at 0.14:

BlockkWhRateCost
Tier 15,0000.10500.00
Tier 25,0650.14709.03
Total10,0650.1201 effective1,209.03

The effective rate on the bill is 0.1201, but the next kWh saved or used is charged at the marginal rate of 0.14. That is the number to use when valuing an efficiency measure.

Valuing a saving: use the marginal rate

Cut 2 kW of input2 kW × 16 h × 26 days = 832 kWh a month. At the marginal 0.14 that is 116.48 a month, or 1,398 a year.
Valued at the average rateThe same saving at 0.1201 would read as 99.95 a month, understating it while the usage sits in the top block.
Premium motorEfficiency 93% to 95% at the same load: input falls from 24.19 to 23.68 kW, saving 212 kWh a month, 29.66 a month, 356 a year. A cost premium of 800 pays back in about 2.2 years (assumed figures).

What a kWh × rate estimate leaves out

ItemHow it is billedEffect
Demand chargePer kW of peak demand in the billing periodDepends on when the load runs, not only how long
Time of useDifferent rates by time of day or seasonMoving a load can change the rate it pays
Power factorPenalty or kVA-based charges in some tariffsLow power factor raises the bill without raising kWh
Fixed charges and taxesPer month or as a percentageRaise the cost per kWh at low usage

For scale, if the tariff above also had a demand charge of 12 per kW-month and this motor's 24.2 kW were the site peak, that adds 290.32 a month, about 24% on top of the 1,209.03 energy cost. That is an illustrative rate; real values vary widely by utility and region. Power factor correction can reduce the kVA a tariff bills, as described in Power Factor Correction Explained.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓Using nameplate kW as the running load. Most equipment runs below its rating.
  • ✓Forgetting efficiency. The meter sees input power, which is higher than shaft output.
  • ✓Valuing savings at the average rate. Reductions come off the top block first.
  • ✓Multiplying one month by 12. Seasonal loads and tariffs make that wrong; sum the months instead.
  • ✓Ignoring demand and fixed charges. They can be a large share of a small industrial bill.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Input kW = rated kW × load factor ÷ efficiency; kWh = input kW × hours.
  • ✓Compute tiered bills block by block; value savings at the marginal rate.
  • ✓Demand, time-of-use, power factor, fixed charges and taxes sit outside a simple kWh × rate estimate.
  • ✓Replace assumed load factors and efficiencies with measured or datasheet values before making a decision.
  • ✓Confirm tariff details against the utility's current rate schedule.
