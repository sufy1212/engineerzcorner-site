Row Spacing & Inter-Row Shading: Sizing the Gap Between Solar Rows

Pack rows too tight and the back rows lose winter production to the shadow of the row in front; space them too wide and you waste land or roof area you already paid for — the gap is set by one worst-case sun angle, not a rule of thumb.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Ground-Mount & Rooftop Layout
01
Row spacing is normally sized against the lowest sun angle the array needs to stay unshaded for — typically solar noon on the winter solstice, the worst case of the year.
02
Steeper tilt throws a longer shadow at the same sun angle, so tilt and pitch (row-to-row spacing) are coupled — you can't optimize one without the other.
03
Ground coverage ratio (GCR) — panel area divided by land area — is the single number that trades off energy density against shading loss.
04
Most utility-scale fixed-tilt arrays accept a small, bounded amount of winter self-shading rather than spacing rows far enough apart to eliminate it entirely.

Why rows shade each other at all

A row of tilted panels casts a shadow behind it whose length depends on the panel height and how low the sun sits in the sky. On a clear summer day near solar noon the sun is high enough that the shadow barely reaches the next row. In winter, at the same time of day, the sun sits much lower and the same row throws a shadow several times longer — long enough to darken the bottom edge, or more, of the row behind it.

Because a shaded string can drag down the output of an entire series string (not just the shaded cells), even a small strip of winter shadow across the bottom row of modules can cost a disproportionate amount of energy if it isn't accounted for at the layout stage.

The governing geometry

d = h × ( cosβ + sinβ / tanα )
d = minimum row pitch (front edge to front edge) · h = collector slant height · β = tilt angle · α = sun elevation angle at the worst-case hour — this is the classic no-shade spacing equation, sized against solar noon on the winter solstice for the site's latitude

The lower the design sun elevation α (higher latitude, or a stricter design hour), the larger tanα shrinks and the more pitch the equation demands. This is why a fixed-tilt array in a high-latitude location needs proportionally more land per kW than the same design near the equator.

Ground coverage ratio: the trade-off in one number

High GCR (tight rows)More panel area per unit of land or roof, lower land/racking cost per watt — but more winter self-shading and a lower capacity factor per panel.
Low GCR (wide rows)Little to no inter-row shading, each panel performs closer to its unshaded potential — but more land, more DC wiring run, and lower total array size for a given site.
Typical fixed-tilt GCRUtility-scale fixed-tilt arrays commonly land around 0.3–0.45 GCR, deliberately accepting a bounded, modeled amount of winter shading loss.

GCR is defined as total module area divided by total ground area the array occupies. It's a design lever, not a fixed constant — the "right" GCR is the one where the marginal land cost saved by tightening the rows still exceeds the marginal energy lost to shading, which is a site-specific financial answer, not a universal geometry answer.

How the design sun angle is usually chosen

Design criterionWhat it means for spacing
Zero-shade at winter solstice noonMost conservative — largest pitch, lowest GCR, used where every winter kWh matters (e.g. peak winter demand offset)
Limited shading window (e.g. ±2 hours around solar noon)Common utility-scale compromise — accepts brief shading right at sunrise/sunset-adjacent hours when output is low anyway
Fixed GCR target (e.g. 0.35)Land- or budget-driven — spacing set first, resulting winter shading loss then modeled and accepted

Practical constraints beyond the pure geometry

  • 1Site slope changes the effective row-to-row height difference, which shifts the required pitch from the flat-ground formula.
  • 2Tracker arrays (single-axis) need extra pitch beyond the fixed-tilt formula to avoid rows shading each other as they rotate through the morning and evening.
  • 3Access roads, maintenance clearance, and firebreak requirements can force wider pitch than the shading math alone would require.
  • 4Rooftop arrays are constrained by the roof's finite area, so the shading trade-off is often "accept some winter loss" rather than "add more land."
  • 5Local shading from trees, parapets or adjacent structures should be checked separately — row-to-row spacing only solves self-shading between your own rows.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Row pitch is driven by the worst-case (lowest) sun elevation angle the design targets, almost always tied to winter solstice solar noon.
  • ✓Tilt and pitch are coupled through the shadow-length geometry — a steeper tilt at the same latitude demands more row spacing.
  • ✓Ground coverage ratio is the practical dial: tighter rows raise energy density per acre but add winter self-shading loss.
  • ✓Real layouts usually accept a small, modeled amount of shading rather than spacing for zero shade year-round.
