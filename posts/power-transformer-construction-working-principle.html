Power Transformers Explained: Construction & Working Principle

A transformer has no moving parts and no direct electrical connection between its input and output — it moves power purely through a shared magnetic field. Here's what's actually inside the tank, and why each part is there.

Two windings, one magnetic field, zero electrical contact

A transformer's core job is deceptively simple: change AC voltage from one level to another without a generator, a rectifier, or any moving parts. It does this through mutual induction. The primary winding, energized by the incoming supply, sets up an alternating magnetic flux in the core. That flux links the secondary winding and induces a voltage in it — without the two windings ever touching. Primary and secondary are galvanically isolated from each other by design, which is itself a safety feature as much as an operating principle.

How much voltage comes out relative to what goes in is set entirely by the turns ratio — the number of turns on the secondary divided by the number of turns on the primary. More secondary turns than primary means a step-up transformer; fewer means step-down. Nothing else in the design changes that basic relationship.

1Core (Laminated Silicon Steel)

  • Provides a low-reluctance magnetic path linking the primary and secondary windings
  • Built from thin, insulated laminations rather than solid steel to choke off eddy currents
  • Laminating the core is what keeps core losses manageable at full-time energization

2Primary & Secondary Windings

  • Primary winding draws current from the supply and generates the core's magnetic flux
  • Secondary winding has an EMF induced in it purely by that shared flux, not by direct contact
  • Their turns ratio is what sets the output voltage relative to the input

3Insulation & Winding Former

  • Layers of insulation keep windings electrically separated from the core and from each other
  • The former (bobbin) physically supports the coil geometry as it's wound
  • Insulation class and BIL rating govern how much voltage stress the transformer can survive

4Tank, Insulating Oil & Conservator

  • Sealed tank holds the core and windings fully submerged in insulating mineral oil
  • Oil does two jobs at once — electrical insulation and heat transport away from the core
  • Conservator tank sits above the main tank to absorb oil's expansion and contraction with temperature

5Cooling System (Radiators / Fans)

  • Radiator fins circulate oil past a larger surface area to dissipate heat to ambient air
  • Fans (ONAF/OFAF-type cooling) add forced airflow across radiators for higher-load ratings
  • Cooling class in the nameplate (e.g. ONAN, ONAF) tells you what cooling stages are actually fitted

6Bushings & Tap Changer

  • Bushings carry HV and LV conductors safely through the grounded tank wall
  • Tap changer adjusts the effective turns ratio in steps to regulate output voltage
  • Off-circuit taps are changed with the transformer de-energized; on-load tap changers switch live

7Protection: Buchholz Relay & Breather

  • Buchholz relay sits in the pipe to the conservator and detects gas from internal arcing or overheating
  • Silica-gel breather keeps atmospheric moisture out of the oil as the conservator "breathes" with temperature
  • Both exist because a fault developing inside a sealed tank isn't otherwise visible from outside

How the cycle actually runs

1AC voltage applied to the primary winding sets up an alternating magnetic flux in the laminated core.
2The core channels that flux with minimal loss from the primary leg to the secondary leg.
3The changing flux induces an EMF in the secondary winding purely by mutual induction.
4The turns ratio between primary and secondary sets the output voltage relative to the input.
5Oil and the cooling system carry away the heat from core and copper losses, keeping windings within their thermal limits.

Where the losses actually come from

Transformer losses split cleanly into two categories, and they behave very differently. Core (no-load) loss comes from magnetizing the core itself — hysteresis and residual eddy currents in the laminations — and it's present at a constant level the entire time the transformer is energized, whether or not it's carrying any load. Copper (load) loss is the I²R heating in the windings, and it rises with the square of load current, so it's zero at no load and largest at full load. Understanding which loss dominates at a given operating point is what separates a transformer that's efficiently sized from one that's needlessly oversized or overloaded.

Why this architecture is common

Voltage Transformation
High Efficiency
Galvanic Isolation

Keeping the primary and secondary windings electrically isolated — linked only by a magnetic field — is what lets a transformer step voltage up or down safely, without ever putting the high-voltage side in direct electrical contact with the low-voltage side. That same isolation is also why a transformer, unlike a generator or a rectifier, can move enormous amounts of power with no moving parts and, aside from cooling equipment, almost nothing to wear out.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Voltage transformation happens through mutual induction between two electrically isolated windings, not a direct connection.
  • ✓The laminated core exists specifically to reduce eddy current losses that a solid core would allow.
  • ✓Oil in oil-filled transformers does two jobs at once: electrical insulation and heat transport.
  • ✓The tap changer is what keeps output voltage stable as supply voltage or load varies.
  • ✓Protection devices like the Buchholz relay exist because internal faults inside a sealed tank aren't otherwise visible.
