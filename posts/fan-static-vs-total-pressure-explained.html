Fan Static vs Total Pressure: What the Fan Is Really Working Against

A fan curve can be quoted in static or total pressure, and a field test can measure either. Here is how the two connect, with worked numbers, and why mixing them up leads to the wrong fan.

01
Total pressure is static pressure plus velocity pressure, and velocity pressure is always positive.
02
Fan total pressure is outlet total pressure minus inlet total pressure.
03
Fan static pressure is fan total pressure minus the velocity pressure at the fan outlet.

Always compare a fan curve and a system requirement on the same pressure basis.

A fan datasheet can quote its pressure two ways, and a field test can measure it two ways. Mixing them up is a common reason a fan appears to under-perform, or why a replacement fan ends up the wrong size. The difference comes down to one term: the velocity pressure of the air leaving the fan.

Three pressures in a moving airstream

Static pressure

The pressure that pushes sideways on the duct walls. It is what a manometer connected to a wall tap reads, and it can be positive or negative relative to the room.

Velocity pressure

The pressure the moving air itself carries, always positive and equal to ½ × density × velocity². For standard air it is about 0.6 × v² in pascals, with v in m/s.

Total pressure = static pressure + velocity pressure

Fan total pressure and fan static pressure

The fan has to raise the total pressure of the air from its inlet to its outlet, so the fan total pressure (FTP) is the outlet total pressure minus the inlet total pressure. The fan static pressure (FSP) is then the fan total pressure minus the velocity pressure at the fan outlet. The reasoning is that the velocity pressure of the air leaving the fan is not recovered by the system, so it is excluded from the useful static pressure. This is the relationship in the AMCA field-test method that the fan static & total pressure calculator follows.

Worked example: fan drawing from a plenum

A fan draws from an open plenum, so the inlet velocity is taken as zero. The inlet static pressure reads −100 Pa, the outlet duct velocity is 10 m/s and the outlet static pressure reads +300 Pa, with air at 1.2 kg/m³.

  • Outlet velocity pressure: 0.5 × 1.2 × 10² = 60 Pa.
  • Inlet total pressure is −100 Pa (no velocity pressure). Outlet total pressure is 300 + 60 = 360 Pa.
  • FTP = 360 − (−100) = 460 Pa.
  • FSP = 460 − 60 = 400 Pa.

The fan is working against 460 Pa in total, of which 400 Pa shows up as static pressure the ductwork has to overcome. Pressure gauges in the US often read inches of water gauge, where 1 in. w.g. is about 249 Pa, so 400 Pa is about 1.6 in. w.g.

With a ducted inlet

When the fan also draws through duct, the inlet carries velocity pressure and the sums change. With an inlet velocity of 8 m/s, an inlet static pressure of −120 Pa, an outlet velocity of 10 m/s and an outlet static pressure of +250 Pa: inlet velocity pressure is 38.4 Pa, so inlet total pressure is −81.6 Pa. Outlet total pressure is 250 + 60 = 310 Pa. FTP = 310 − (−81.6) = 391.6 Pa and FSP = 391.6 − 60 = 331.6 Pa. Ignoring the inlet velocity pressure would have given the wrong answer, which is why both readings are needed.

Why it matters when you select or check a fan

  • Know which basis the fan curve uses. Catalogues publish curves on a static or a total pressure basis. Compare your system's required pressure on the same basis, or the fan will be mis-sized.
  • Power follows total pressure. Air power is flow times total pressure. A fan moving 2 m³/s at 460 Pa FTP delivers 920 W of air power, and the shaft power is higher by the fan's total efficiency.
  • Outlet velocity is a loss. The faster the air leaves the fan, the more of the total pressure is velocity pressure that the system cannot use, which is why a diffuser or a well-designed outlet duct can improve performance.
  • Density changes the numbers. At altitude or high temperature the air is lighter, so the same velocity gives less velocity pressure. Use the actual air density for the reading, not just the standard 1.2 kg/m³.

For how fan speed changes pressure and power, see pump and fan affinity laws, and for the duct side, duct sizing.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Velocity pressure is 0.5 × density × velocity², about 0.6 × v² Pa for standard air.
  • ✓FTP = TP(out) − TP(in), and FSP = FTP − VP(out).
  • ✓A ducted inlet carries its own velocity pressure, so measure both inlet and outlet readings.
  • ✓Check whether the catalogue curve is static or total before comparing it with the system.
