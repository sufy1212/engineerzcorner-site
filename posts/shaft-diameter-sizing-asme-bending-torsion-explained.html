Shaft Diameter Sizing: Combining Bending and Torsion with the ASME Equation

A power shaft bends and twists at the same time. Here is how the ASME equation combines the two, what the shock factors and allowable stress mean, and a worked example with the limits of the method.

A shaft that transmits power is almost never loaded in pure torsion. Belt pull, gear forces and overhung couplings all bend it while it twists. The ASME design equation combines the two into one check and adds factors for shock and fatigue, which makes it a quick way to find a minimum solid diameter for a section of shaft.

The ASME equation

d³ = 16 ÷ (π × τ) × √[ (Kb × Mb)² + (Kt × Mt)² ]

Mb is the bending moment and Mt the torque at the section, τ is the allowable shear stress, and Kb and Kt are the combined shock-and-fatigue factors. When the torque is not given, it comes from power and speed: Mt = 9,550 × P ÷ N with P in kW, N in rpm and Mt in N·m.

The inputs

Allowable shear stress

For steel bought to a specification, the ASME approach takes 30% of the yield strength, but not more than 18% of the ultimate strength, and commonly reduces that by 25% where there is a keyway. For commercial shafting steel, values of about 55 MPa without a keyway and 40 MPa with one are often used.

Shock and fatigue factors

These raise the design moment for harsher loading. A stationary shaft with a gradual load uses 1.0 for both. A rotating shaft with a steady load is usually 1.5 for bending and 1.0 for torsion, and heavy shocks push the pair up to between 2 and 3 for bending and 1.5 to 3 for torsion.

Worked example

A shaft carries 15 kW at 1,450 rpm, with a bending moment of 150 N·m at the critical section. The shaft has a keyway, so the allowable shear stress is taken as 40 MPa, and the load is a rotating shaft with minor shocks, Kb = 2.0 and Kt = 1.5.

  • Torque: 9,550 × 15 ÷ 1,450 = 98.8 N·m.
  • Equivalent moment: √[ (2.0 × 150)² + (1.5 × 98.8)² ] = 334.6 N·m.
  • d³ = 16 × 334.6 ÷ (π × 40×10⁶) gives d = 34.9 mm.
  • The torsional shear stress at that size is about 11.8 MPa, well below the allowable value, since the shock factors and the bending moment do most of the sizing.

The shaft diameter calculator then adds a simplified 10% allowance for the keyway, giving 38.4 mm, and the next stock size up, such as 40 mm, would be chosen. Changing the load type to a rotating shaft with a steady load (Kb = 1.5, Kt = 1.0) in the same case gives 31.5 mm, or 34.7 mm with the keyway allowance, which shows how much the shock factors matter.

What this check does not cover

  • Where the bending moment comes from. It is an input. Work it out from the bearing positions and the loads, as in beam bending basics.
  • Fatigue life. The K factors are a simple allowance. A shaft that runs for a long life under reversing bending needs a proper fatigue analysis with stress concentration at shoulders and keyways.
  • Critical speed and deflection. A shaft can be strong enough and still whirl or deflect too much at its operating speed. Check both separately.
  • Other sections. The result applies to one section. Check each place where the moment or torque is high, or where a shoulder or keyway concentrates the stress.

For the fasteners that hold the assembly together, see bolt torque vs preload and the belt drive calculator, which supplies the belt forces that cause the bending.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Compute torque from power and speed as 9,550 × kW ÷ rpm.
  • ✓Select Kb and Kt for the real loading, since they change the diameter more than people expect.
  • ✓Add an allowance for keyways and round up to a stock size.
  • ✓Check fatigue, whirling speed and deflection separately, and check every section, not just one.
