Level Control of an Integrating Process

Tuning a level loop is not like tuning a temperature loop. A tank never settles by itself: it keeps filling or draining until something changes the flow. That one fact explains why P-only control leaves an offset and why adding integral action can make the loop ring.

Level is an integrating process: dL/dt = (Qin − Qout) / A. It has no natural resting level, so the controller has to hold it.

With P-only control the valve must equal the outflow, so the error must be non-zero: offset = outflow demand / Kp. A bigger Kp shrinks it but never removes it.

With PI control the damping is ζ = (Kp / 2) × √(K / Ki). Raising Ki lowers damping, raising Kp increases it, the opposite of what many people expect.

Whether to control level tightly or loosely depends on the job: a surge tank should absorb flow swings, not pass them downstream.

A tank is an integrator

Liquid level follows the mass balance: the rate of change of level is the net flow in divided by the tank cross-section. If inflow equals outflow, level holds wherever it happens to be. If they differ even slightly, level keeps drifting until the tank overflows or runs dry. A process like this is called integrating, or non-self-regulating, in contrast to a self-regulating process such as a heated vessel that finds its own equilibrium temperature.

In the level control simulator the mass balance is level change = (inflow − outflow) × dt, with inflow = valve position × 16 %level/s at full open (and the same for the outlet). So the plant gain is K = 16/100 = 0.16 %level per second for each 1% of inlet valve opening.

Why P-only leaves an offset

At steady state the level is steady, so the inlet valve must pass exactly what the outlet demands. With P-only control the valve position is u = Kp × error, so a non-zero valve position needs a non-zero error:

Offset = outlet demand (as % valve) ÷ Kp. With a 30% outlet demand and Kp = 4, the level settles 30 / 4 = 7.5 points below setpoint.

Running the simulator's model with Ki = 0 confirms it: setpoint 55%, Kp = 4 settles at 47.5%, and Kp = 8 settles at 51.25%, an offset of 3.75. Doubling Kp halves the offset. If the demand rises (a disturbance), the offset grows with it. Integral action is what removes it: the integral keeps ramping the valve until the error is exactly zero.

PI control of an integrator: damping from Kp and Ki

With the simulator's control law u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt acting on a plant K/s, the closed loop has the characteristic equation s² + K·Kp·s + K·Ki = 0, so (ignoring the valve lag):

ζ(Kp / 2) × √(K / Ki)

ζ of about 1 or more means no overshoot; below about 0.7 the loop rings. The simulator's default of Kp = 4, Ki = 0.6 gives ζ = 1.03, close to critical damping, which is why a start from an empty tank approaches 55% without overshooting. The damping limit for no overshoot is Ki ≤ K·Kp² / 4, which is 0.64 for Kp = 4.

The overshoot column comes from running the simulator's discrete model (tick 0.3 s, valve lag of 0.3 per tick) from an empty tank, so it includes the valve lag and the 100% valve limit and does not match a pure second-order formula. The pattern is what matters. Raising Ki from 0.6 to 2.0 cuts ζ to 0.57 and the level overshoots; cutting Kp to 1 drops ζ to 0.26 and the level swings far past setpoint. The practical rule for an integrating process is to set Kp first so the loop is well damped, then choose Ki small enough to keep ζ high.

Disturbance rejection

The simulator's Kick Disturbance steps the outlet valve open by 35 points for 9 simulated seconds, a sudden draw on the tank. Starting from a settled loop at setpoint, the level dips and the controller opens the inlet to recover:

A higher Kp reacts sooner to the same disturbance and gives a smaller dip, and integral action brings the level back to setpoint afterwards. The price of a high Kp is a more active valve, and noise on the level signal gets passed to the valve more strongly. Derivative action is rarely used on level for that reason. These are the model's numbers for this tank and valve; another tank area or valve size changes K and therefore every gain.

Tight control or averaging control

Tight control holds the level close to setpoint, which is right when the level itself matters, for example a boiler drum. It passes every outflow disturbance to the manipulated flow. Averaging (surge) control does the opposite: the tank is there to smooth flow for the downstream unit, so the controller is tuned low-gain and the level is allowed to drift within a wide band, keeping the outflow changes gradual. The same simulator shows the trade-off: lower Kp, a larger dip and a gentler valve.

Whichever you choose, keep the high and low alarms outside the normal control band, as covered in alarm setpoint bands, and treat the safety trips as independent of the control loop.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Level is an integrator: it only holds if inflow equals outflow, so the controller must keep it there.
  • ✓P-only offset = outlet demand ÷ Kp (30 / 4 = 7.5 points in the simulator); integral action removes it.
  • ✓PI damping ζ = (Kp / 2) × √(K / Ki): raise Kp to damp, lower Ki to avoid overshoot.
  • ✓A higher Kp shrinks the dip from a disturbance at the cost of a busier valve.
  • ✓Choose tight or averaging control by what the tank is for, and treat these as teaching figures, not a tuning study of a real loop.
