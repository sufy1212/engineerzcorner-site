Breaker Selectivity: Why Two Correctly Rated Breakers Can Still Trip Together

Correct ratings on paper don't guarantee correct behaviour in the field — selectivity is decided by where the trip curves sit relative to each other, not by the ratings alone.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Selectivity / IEC 60947-2
01
Selectivity (discrimination) means only the breaker closest to a fault trips, leaving the rest of the installation supplied — not every upstream breaker opening for the same event.
02
It's checked across the whole current range, not just at one point: thermal (long time), magnetic/short-time (Isd), and instantaneous (Ii) settings all have to be considered together.
03
The standard settings hierarchy is Ir < Isd < Ii from downstream to upstream — get that order wrong and the two breakers can race each other on the same fault.
04
Two individually well-rated breakers can still fail to discriminate if their time-current curves cross or overlap at the actual fault current level.

What selectivity actually means

Selectivity between two breakers in series means that when a fault occurs downstream of both, only the nearer (downstream) breaker opens. The upstream breaker stays closed, so everything else it feeds keeps running. Without selectivity, a fault on one final circuit can trip a breaker several levels upstream too, blacking out far more of the installation than the fault itself affected.

Selectivity isn't a property of a single breaker — it's a relationship between a pair (or chain) of breakers, decided by how their trip characteristics compare at every current level a fault could produce.

The settings hierarchy

Ir  <  Isd  <  Ii
Ir = long-time (thermal overload) pickup · Isd = short-time (magnetic) pickup · Ii = instantaneous pickup — each setting on the upstream breaker should sit above the corresponding threshold on the downstream breaker

Per IEC 60947-2, each protection stage on an electronic trip unit (ETU) must be set so the upstream breaker's thresholds sit above the downstream breaker's, for every stage. If an upstream Ir is left lower than a downstream Isd, or an upstream Isd overlaps a downstream Ii, both breakers can respond to the same fault instead of just the one nearer the fault.

Why "correctly rated" isn't the same as "selective"

A breaker can be entirely correctly rated for its own load and interrupting duty and still fail to discriminate with its neighbour, because rating and coordination are different checks. Rating asks whether the breaker can carry and interrupt current safely. Selectivity asks whether, plotted on the same log-log time-current graph, the two breakers' curves stay clear of each other across the full fault current range — including right up at the instantaneous trip level, where curves are flat vertical lines and most prone to overlapping.

Total selectivityDownstream breaker clears the fault at every current up to its full breaking capacity, without the upstream breaker ever operating.
Partial selectivitySelective up to a defined current limit (Is); above that, both breakers may trip together.

Where it typically breaks down

The most common coordination failure is at the instantaneous stage: if the downstream breaker's instantaneous pickup (Ii) is set at or above the upstream breaker's short-time pickup (Isd), a high fault current can trip both simultaneously, because the upstream breaker's short-time protection reacts before the downstream instantaneous stage has time to clear the fault alone. This is exactly the kind of hierarchy violation worth checking explicitly when commissioning ETU-based breakers, not just assuming factory defaults are already coordinated.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Selectivity is a pairwise relationship between breakers' trip curves, not a property of one breaker's rating alone.
  • ✓Keep Ir < Isd < Ii consistently higher moving upstream at every stage.
  • ✓Check coordination across the full current range on a plotted TCC, not just at one fault level.
  • ✓The instantaneous stage is the most common place selectivity quietly breaks down — verify it explicitly.
