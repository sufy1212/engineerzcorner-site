Solar Panel Cross-Section: The Layers Explained

A solar panel is a sandwich, not a single sheet of magic glass. Six layers, each doing one job, laminated together under heat and pressure into something meant to survive 25+ years on a roof.

It's a laminate, not a single sheet

A standard crystalline-silicon solar panel is built as a stack of distinct layers, fused into one rigid unit in a lamination press using heat and pressure. Take one apart (carefully — don't) and from front to back you'd find: tempered glass, an encapsulant layer, the solar cells themselves wired together, another encapsulant layer, and a backsheet — all held at the edges by an aluminum frame, with a junction box on the back where the electrical output leaves the panel.

The six layers, front to back

What each layer is actually there to do.

Tempered Glass
Front cover, ~3–4mm
Low-iron tempered glass, chosen for high light transmittance and impact resistance — IEC 61215 requires it to survive a 25mm hailstone at roughly 60mph without cracking through to the cells.
EVA Encapsulant
Front & rear, ethylene-vinyl acetate
A polymer film that melts during lamination and bonds the glass, cells, and backsheet into one sealed unit, keeping moisture and oxygen away from the cells and metal interconnects.
Solar Cells
Monocrystalline or polycrystalline silicon
The actual photovoltaic material, wired in series strings by thin metal ribbons soldered to the cells' front and rear busbars — a modern module is typically 120-plus half-cells strung together.
Backsheet
Rear polymer film, or glass on bifacial modules
A multi-layer polymer film providing a moisture barrier, chemical/corrosion resistance, and electrical insulation on the panel's rear face — replaced by a second glass layer entirely on bifacial modules.
Aluminum Frame
Edges only, not a full-back layer
An extruded aluminum frame around the laminate's edges, providing structural rigidity, edge protection, and a standard mounting point for racking — anodized silver or black depending on the manufacturer.
Junction Box & Bypass Diodes
Rear-mounted electrical exit point
Houses the panel's positive and negative output leads and — in almost every module — three bypass diodes, one per roughly 20-cell substring, wired in parallel with each substring rather than in series with the main circuit.

Why bypass diodes matter

Cells within a panel are wired in series, which means the same current has to flow through every cell in the string. If one cell is shaded, it can't produce as much current as its unshaded neighbors — but it's still forced to carry the string's full current, dissipating the excess as heat rather than passing it through as electricity. That's a hot spot, and left unchecked it can permanently damage the cell or even start a fire. A bypass diode wired in parallel across a substring of cells gives that current somewhere else to go: once a shaded cell forces enough voltage drop, its diode forward-biases and the current routes around the whole substring instead of forcing its way through the shaded cell.

Three diodes per panel is the near-universal default, each covering roughly 18–24 cells — but it's a compromise. A single shaded cell can still trigger a hot spot within its substring before the diode kicks in fully, which is why some premium modules use a bypass diode per cell instead of per substring.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A solar panel is a laminated sandwich of six layers, each with one specific protective or functional job — not a single sheet of active material.
  • ✓EVA encapsulant is what actually seals the cells from moisture — its quality is a bigger long-term reliability factor than people often assume.
  • ✓Bypass diodes are wired in parallel with cell substrings, not in series with the circuit — they exist purely to give shaded cells' current somewhere else to go.
  • ✓Bifacial modules aren't a different cell technology — they're the same cells with the backsheet swapped for glass, so the rear side can see light too.
