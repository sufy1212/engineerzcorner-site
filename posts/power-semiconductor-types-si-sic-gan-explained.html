Power Semiconductors: Si, SiC & GaN

Wide-bandgap devices aren't replacing silicon across the board — they're taking over the specific corners of the voltage/frequency map where their extra cost buys back more in efficiency, size, or heat than it adds in bill of materials.

What the material actually changes

A power semiconductor's job is to switch current on and off efficiently — every time it switches, some energy is lost as heat, and every microsecond it takes to fully turn on or off adds to that loss. Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) are wide-bandgap materials: their semiconductor bandgap is roughly three times wider than silicon's, which lets them sustain much higher electric fields and operate at higher temperatures. In practice that translates into lower switching losses, faster switching speeds, and (for SiC especially) higher breakdown voltages than an equivalent silicon device — but at a higher cost per device, which is why the choice is still a genuine trade-off rather than a settled upgrade.

The three material families

Each sits in a different part of the voltage/frequency map — the right choice depends on where the application falls on that map, not which material is newest.

Silicon IGBT / MOSFET
Established, mature material
Decades of manufacturing refinement give silicon devices the lowest cost per ampere, a high safe operating area, and strong short-circuit withstand. Slower switching (limited by minority-carrier recombination in IGBTs) makes them best suited to high-power, lower-frequency work.
Silicon Carbide (SiC)
High-voltage, high-temperature specialist
Sits strategically between silicon and GaN — devices rated up to roughly 1,200V+ with much lower switching losses than silicon IGBTs and strong high-temperature robustness. Contains a body diode that introduces some reverse-recovery loss, unlike GaN.
Gallium Nitride (GaN)
High-frequency specialist
The highest electron mobility of the three, enabling switching frequencies well into the megahertz range with no reverse-recovery losses. Currently strongest below roughly 650V — the sweet spot for compact, high-density power supplies.

Selection by application

  1. 1
    Industrial motor drives & utility-scale invertersSilicon IGBTs remain the cost-effective default for high-power, moderate-frequency loads where decades of proven reliability matter.
  2. 2
    EV traction invertersSiC's low switching losses at high voltage and strong thermal performance have made it the emerging standard, improving range and reducing cooling requirements.
  3. 3
    Utility-scale solar & large grid convertersSiC's high-voltage, high-efficiency switching reduces losses across long duty cycles, where every percentage point of efficiency compounds over the system's life.
  4. 4
    Compact switched-mode power supplies & EV chargersGaN's very high switching frequency shrinks the magnetics and capacitors around it, cutting size and weight where power density matters most.
  5. 5
    Data center power suppliesGaN's efficiency and power density at moderate voltage suit the compact, high-density power stages increasingly used in AI/GPU racks.

Key terms

TermWhat it means
BandgapThe energy gap a material's electrons must cross to conduct — a wider bandgap allows higher voltage and temperature operation before the device breaks down
Switching lossesEnergy lost as heat during the brief transition between fully on and fully off — lower switching losses allow higher frequencies without more heat
Reverse-recovery lossExtra loss from a body diode's stored charge when a device switches off — SiC MOSFETs have this, GaN devices generally don't
Power densityOutput power per unit volume or weight — higher switching frequency shrinks passive components, raising the achievable power density

Selection checklist

  • 1Required blocking voltage — GaN's advantage narrows above roughly 650V, where SiC or Si takes over
  • 2Target switching frequency and how much it shrinks passives, weighed against the wide-bandgap device's added cost
  • 3Ambient and junction temperature the device must survive over its service life
  • 4Total system cost, not just device cost — a pricier SiC/GaN device can still reduce overall cost through smaller cooling and passives
  • 5Gate driver compatibility, since wide-bandgap devices often need faster, more careful gate drive design than silicon
  • 6Supply chain maturity and second-source availability for the specific device family being designed in
None of these three materials is displacing the others outright — silicon IGBTs still lead on cost per ampere at high power, SiC leads in high-voltage/high-temperature efficiency, and GaN leads in high-frequency power density. The practical question for any design is which corner of that map the application actually sits in.

Common applications

Key takeaways

  • ✓Wide-bandgap devices (SiC, GaN) win on switching losses and frequency, but silicon IGBTs still win on cost per ampere at high power — the choice is a system-level trade-off, not a strict upgrade path.
  • ✓SiC's niche is high-voltage, high-temperature efficiency — EV traction, solar, and grid-scale converters.
  • ✓GaN's niche is very high frequency at lower voltage — compact power supplies, chargers, and data center power stages where power density matters most.
  • ✓A higher device cost for SiC or GaN can still lower total system cost once reduced cooling and smaller passive components are accounted for.
