What Is an ELV System? Types, Components & the BIM Engineer's Role

Every modern building runs two nervous systems side by side — one delivering power, the other carrying data, alarms, and control signals at a fraction of the voltage. That second one is ELV, and it's a lot bigger than "CCTV and fire alarm."

ELV covers any system operating at 50V AC or 120V DC or less — communication, security, monitoring and building automation, not power distribution.

Extra Low Voltage, defined

ELV stands for Extra Low Voltage — electrical systems that operate well below normal power-distribution voltages, generally used for communication, security, monitoring, and building automation rather than for running motors, lighting loads, or appliances.

ELV threshold
≤ 50V ACalternating current
≤ 120V DCdirect current

Because ELV equipment runs at such low voltage, it's inherently lower-risk to work around than power circuits — but that doesn't make it a lighter engineering discipline. A large commercial or hospitality project can easily carry more individual ELV devices than power points, spread across six genuinely different subsystems that each have their own standards, cabling rules, and commissioning sequence.

The six ELV system categories

What each one does, and the equipment that typically shows up on the drawings
1. ICT

Information & Communication Technology — provides reliable data, voice, and internet infrastructure.

Key functions
  • Internet connectivity
  • Data transfer
  • Voice communication
  • Network connectivity
2. Security Systems

Ensures safety and security of building occupants and assets.

  • Access control
  • Surveillance
  • Threat detection

3. Fire Alarm System

Detects fire and alerts people to take necessary action.

Smoke Detector Heat Detector Manual Call Point (MCP) Fire Alarm Control Panel (FACP) Sounder & Strobe
Key functions
  • Early fire detection
  • Alarm notification
  • Life safety

Used for public announcements and emergency voice evacuation.

Speakers Microphone Amplifier
Key functions
  • Public announcements

5. Building Management System

Monitors and controls various building services from a central system.

ELV system vs. electrical system

ELV and electrical power are two separate design disciplines that happen to share the same risers, shafts, and ceiling voids — which is exactly why coordination between them matters so much on a real project.

Where the MEP BIM engineer fits in

ELV might run at a fraction of the voltage, but on a BIM-coordinated project it goes through the same modeling and coordination workflow as any other MEP discipline — arguably more so, since ELV containment competes for the same ceiling and shaft space as power, HVAC ductwork, and plumbing.

  • 13D modeling of every ELV system — cabling, containment, and devices — at the required LOD.
  • 2Cable tray and conduit routing coordinated with structure and other services.
  • 3Equipment placement for panels, cameras, detectors, speakers, and controllers.
  • 4Multidisciplinary coordination across electrical, HVAC, fire, and plumbing models.
  • 5Clash detection, typically run in Navisworks against the federated model.
  • 6Shop drawings and installation drawings issued for site execution.
  • 7Modeling to LOD 300 / 350 / 400 as the design progresses toward construction.
  • 8Quantity take-off (BOQ) generated directly from the coordinated model.
  • 9As-built model creation once installation is verified on site.

Key takeaways

  • ✓ELV means ≤50V AC or ≤120V DC — used for communication, security, monitoring, and automation, not for running loads.
  • ✓Six categories cover almost every low-voltage system in a building: ICT, security, fire alarm, PA/voice evacuation, BMS, and other ELV.
  • ✓ELV and electrical power are separate disciplines sharing the same physical space, so coordination between them is not optional.
  • ✓ELV still goes through full BIM modeling, clash detection, and BOQ extraction like any other MEP discipline.
