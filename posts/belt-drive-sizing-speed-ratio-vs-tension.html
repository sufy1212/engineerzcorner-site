Belt Drive Sizing: Why the Speed Ratio Alone Doesn't Tell You the Tension

Two belt drives with identical pulley ratios can need very different tension, because the geometry that sets the speed ratio isn't the same geometry that sets the grip.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Belt Drive Sizing
01
Speed ratio between driver and driven shaft is pure geometry — the inverse ratio of pulley diameters.
02
Belt (pitch-line) velocity comes from pulley diameter and rotational speed, and it's what most belt life and noise guidance is actually referenced against.
03
Tension isn't decided by the speed ratio at all — it comes from how much grip the belt has on the pulley, via the wrap angle and the friction coefficient.
04
The tension ratio between the tight and slack sides follows the capstan (belt-friction) equation, and it's what actually limits how much power a given belt and pulley combination can transmit before slipping.

Speed ratio: simple geometry

N2  /  N1  =  D1  /  D2
N1, N2 = driver and driven shaft speeds · D1, D2 = driver and driven pulley diameters

The driven shaft's speed relative to the driver depends only on the inverse ratio of pulley diameters — a smaller driven pulley spins faster than the driver, a larger one spins slower. Belt (pitch-line) velocity, v = πDN, follows directly and has to be identical for both pulleys since it's the same belt connecting them; that consistency is exactly what makes the diameter ratio determine the speed ratio in the first place. None of this geometry says anything yet about how much power the drive can actually transmit.

Tension is a friction problem, not a geometry problem

T1  /  T2  =  eμθ
T1, T2 = tight-side and slack-side belt tension · μ = coefficient of friction between belt and pulley · θ = angle of wrap (radians) on the smaller/driving pulley

This is the capstan or belt-friction equation, and it's a completely different relationship from the one that sets the speed ratio. It says the maximum ratio between tight-side and slack-side tension a belt drive can sustain before slipping depends on the friction coefficient between belt and pulley material, and how much of the pulley's circumference the belt actually wraps around. A smaller pulley (common on the higher-speed side of a reduction drive) wraps a smaller angle — less grip — which is one reason small-diameter drive pulleys can become the limiting factor in a design even when the speed ratio itself looks unremarkable.

What this means for two drives with the same ratio

Power transmitted is P = (T1 − T2) × v — so for a given power and belt speed, the achievable tension difference is fixed, but how that translates into required tight-side tension (and therefore shaft and bearing load) depends on the T1/T2 ratio the wrap angle and friction allow. Two drives with an identical pulley diameter ratio, but different centre distances or pulley sizes, can end up wrapping different angles — and therefore need meaningfully different tensioning to transmit the same power without slipping. Speed ratio and tension have to be checked as two separate steps, not assumed to follow from the same geometry.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Speed ratio between shafts is set purely by the inverse ratio of pulley diameters.
  • ✓Tension capability is a friction problem — governed by wrap angle and the belt-pulley friction coefficient, not by the diameter ratio.
  • ✓A smaller drive pulley wraps a smaller angle, reducing grip even when its diameter ratio to the driven pulley looks unremarkable.
  • ✓Two drives with the same speed ratio can need different tensioning if their wrap angles differ, so check both relationships separately.
