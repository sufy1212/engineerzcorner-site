Busbar Sizing: Why the Short-Circuit Check Often Governs, Not the Continuous Rating

A busbar sized only for continuous current can still fail the moment a fault current has to pass through it.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Busbar & Enclosure Sizing
01
The continuous current rating sets a minimum cross-sectional area (CSA) — it says nothing about whether the busbar survives a fault.
02
Short-circuit thermal withstand (I²t heating) and electrodynamic force between conductors are separate checks that can demand a larger CSA or tighter support spacing.
03
Electrodynamic force between parallel busbars is proportional to the peak fault current squared and inversely proportional to conductor spacing.
04
Enclosure sizing is a heat-dissipation problem: internal losses have to escape through the enclosure surface by natural convection, or internal temperature climbs past what the gear inside is rated for.

Two different jobs, one piece of copper

A busbar has to do two unrelated things well: carry its continuous rated current all day without overheating, and survive the brief, enormous current of a short circuit without melting or being torn off its supports. Sizing for the first doesn't automatically satisfy the second — at higher fault levels, the short-circuit check is often what actually decides the final cross-section, not the load current.

Continuous rating is a straightforward current-density exercise: CSA is chosen so the busbar's steady-state temperature rise stays within its insulation/paint class, using tabulated current-density figures for copper or aluminium in free air. That's Step 1 on the calculator. Step 2 is where the fault-current checks come in.

Thermal withstand: I²t heating

S  ≥  (Ik × √t)  /  k
S = required CSA · Ik = symmetrical short-circuit current · t = fault clearing time · k = material constant (accounts for initial/final temperature limits and conductor material)

This is an adiabatic assumption: over the short duration of a fault (typically well under a second), essentially none of the heat generated has time to leave the conductor, so all of it goes into raising its temperature. The busbar has to survive that temperature rise without annealing or melting before the protective device clears the fault. The fault current Ik here comes straight from a short-circuit study at that point in the system — the same Ik″ used for breaker interrupting ratings.

Electrodynamic force: why spacing matters as much as size

Parallel conductors carrying current in the same direction attract each other; opposite directions repel. During a fault, the peak (asymmetrical) current can be more than double the symmetrical RMS value, and the force between busbars scales with the square of that peak current:

F/L  =  (μ0 × Ipk²)  /  (2π × d)
F/L = force per unit length between conductors · Ipk = peak fault current · d = spacing between conductor centrelines

This is why busbar supports have a rated bracing distance: closer supports (shorter unsupported span) let the busbar resist a higher electrodynamic force before it deflects or breaks free. A busbar that comfortably passes the thermal check can still fail mechanically if the supports are spaced too far apart for the fault level present.

Enclosure sizing is a different problem: heat has to get out

Once the busbar and connected gear are sized, the enclosure has to be large enough to shed the heat they generate. For a sealed, naturally-ventilated enclosure, that's a surface-area problem, not a volume problem:

A  =  Ploss  /  (k × ΔT)
A = effective dissipating surface area · Ploss = total internal power loss (W) · k = heat transfer coefficient for natural convection off sheet steel · ΔT = allowable temperature rise above ambient

Since dissipating area grows slower than volume as an enclosure gets bigger, doubling the internal losses doesn't just mean a slightly bigger box — it can mean a disproportionately larger one, or forced ventilation instead of relying on natural convection alone.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Continuous current rating and short-circuit withstand are independent checks — passing one says nothing about the other.
  • ✓Thermal (I²t) withstand assumes the fault is too fast for any heat to escape, so all of it raises the conductor's temperature.
  • ✓Electrodynamic force scales with the square of peak fault current, which is why support spacing tightens as fault levels rise.
  • ✓Enclosure sizing is a surface-area heat-dissipation check against total internal losses, separate from the electrical sizing inside it.
