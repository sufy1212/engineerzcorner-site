Motor Overload Protection: How Overload Relays and Trip Classes Work

A motor must be able to start without tripping and still be protected from overheating. Here is how an overload relay works, what the trip classes mean in seconds and how to match a class to the load.

A motor that is overloaded does not fail at once. It heats, the insulation ages faster, and the winding eventually breaks down. Protection has to act before that happens, yet it also has to let the motor start, since a starting motor draws several times its running current for a few seconds. The overload relay and its trip class are how that balance is set.

What an overload relay does

A thermal overload relay passes the motor current through a bimetal in each phase. The bimetals heat in proportion to the current and bend, and when they have bent far enough the relay trips the contactor. Electronic overload relays copy the same behaviour with current sensing, and add extras. Neither one protects against a short circuit. That is the job of the fuse or breaker upstream, which is why overload and short-circuit protection are chosen as a pair.

Trip classes

IEC 60947-4-1 defines trip classes by how long the relay takes to trip from cold at 7.2 times its current setting, which is roughly what a motor draws at standstill.

ClassTrip time at 7.2 × setting (cold)Typical use
10A2 to 10 sQuick-starting loads, most small motors
104 to 10 sNormal starting, such as pumps
206 to 20 sLonger starts, such as some fans and compressors
309 to 30 sHeavy, high-inertia loads

The standard also sets the slow end of the curve. A relay must not trip at 1.05 times its setting, must trip within 2 hours at 1.2 times, and at 1.5 times the maximum trip time is 2, 4, 8 and 12 minutes for classes 10A, 10, 20 and 30. The class describes a curve, so a higher class gives a motor longer to start before it trips, at the price of slower protection.

Worked example

An 11 kW motor on 400 V draws about 20.8 A at full load. The overload is set to the nameplate current of 20.8 A. At 7.2 times that, 150 A, a class 10 relay trips in 4 to 10 s from cold, and a class 20 relay in 6 to 20 s. If this motor drives a fan with a long acceleration time of, say, 8 s, a class 10 relay could trip during a normal start, so class 20 is the better choice. A pump that reaches speed in 2 s fits class 10 comfortably. The class has to be matched to the start time, and the start time has to come from the load, not from a guess. Beyond simple overload

  • Phase loss and unbalance. Most relays trip faster when one phase is lost or the currents are badly unbalanced, since the motor heats much faster in that state.
  • Ambient temperature. Bimetal relays are affected by the temperature where they sit, so use one with ambient compensation, or an electronic one, when the panel is hot.
  • Thermistors. Sensors built into the winding act on actual winding temperature, which also catches cooling failure and frequent starts that current alone would miss.
  • Motor protection breakers. An MPCB combines an adjustable thermal trip with a magnetic trip for short circuits in one device. See circuit breaker types for how magnetic and thermal tripping differ.
  • Drives. A VFD has its own electronic motor protection, so check the drive manual before adding a separate overload relay.

Setting it up

Set the relay to the motor's rated current from the nameplate, after allowing for the service factor if the motor has one, and confirm the rating of the fuse or breaker that backs it up. Check that the settings suit the actual start. Work out the running current with the motor full-load current calculator, and for how protection devices coordinate, read breaker selectivity and relay basics.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Set the overload to the motor nameplate current, adjusting for service factor where it applies.
  • ✓Pick the trip class from the real start time of the load, using a higher class for long, high-inertia starts.
  • ✓Overload relays do not clear short circuits, so check the fuse or breaker that backs them up.
  • ✓Look at phase-loss sensitivity, ambient compensation and thermistors for added protection.
