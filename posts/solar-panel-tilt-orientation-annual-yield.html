Solar Panel Tilt & Orientation: Why Latitude Isn't the Whole Answer

"Set the tilt to your latitude" is a fine starting point and a poor final answer — the best fixed tilt depends on whether you're optimizing for annual yield, winter performance, or summer peak shaving.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Fixed-Tilt PV Arrays
01
A fixed panel captures the most annual energy when it faces true equator-facing azimuth and tilts at roughly the site's latitude — but "roughly" hides a real design choice.
02
Tilt flatter than latitude favours summer output; tilt steeper than latitude favours winter output — the sun's elevation angle swings with the seasons, not just the day.
03
Azimuth error costs less energy than most people expect near due-equator, but the penalty accelerates fast past about 30° off-axis.
04
Roof-constrained arrays often trade a small annual yield loss for a tilt/azimuth that matches the existing roof pitch and orientation — and that's usually the right call.

The starting rule of thumb

For a fixed (non-tracking) array optimized for maximum annual energy yield, the classic starting point is: face the array toward the equator (due south in the northern hemisphere, due north in the southern hemisphere), and tilt it at an angle roughly equal to the site's latitude.

βannual ≈ φ
β = panel tilt angle from horizontal · φ = site latitude — this approximation gets you close to the annual-optimum tilt for most latitudes, not the exact one

This rule works because it roughly balances the sun's path across the whole year: in summer the sun tracks high and the panel is "under-tilted" for those months, while in winter the sun tracks low and the same panel is closer to perpendicular to the winter sun — the two seasons partially cancel out over a full year.

Optimizing for a season instead of the whole year

Annual yieldTilt ≈ latitude. Balances summer and winter sun angles for the highest total kWh across 12 months.
Winter-weightedTilt ≈ latitude + 10–15°. Steeper angle faces a lower winter sun more directly, useful where winter demand or grid value is higher.
Summer-weightedTilt ≈ latitude − 10–15°. Flatter angle suits a higher summer sun, common for cooling-dominated loads.

Which of these matters depends on what the system is actually offsetting. A site with a strong summer air-conditioning load and net metering that pays less for winter export often does better tilted flatter than latitude, even though that lowers the theoretical annual total by a small margin.

Azimuth: how much does facing off-equator cost?

Azimuth offset from equator-facingTypical annual yield impact
0° (due equator)Reference (100%)
±15°~1–2% loss
±30°~3–5% loss
±45°~8–12% loss
±90° (due east/west)~20–25% loss

These figures vary with latitude and local weather, but the shape holds everywhere: azimuth error is forgiving near zero and increasingly expensive as it grows, which is why a modest roof orientation mismatch is rarely worth re-engineering the mounting structure over.

Real installations rarely hit the theoretical optimum

  • 1Roof pitch is fixed — a rooftop array typically follows the existing roof angle rather than an independently engineered tilt.
  • 2Ground-mount racking has a limited set of manufactured tilt angles, not a continuous range.
  • 3Wind loading increases with tilt angle, so very steep fixed tilts may need heavier structural bracing.
  • 4Row-to-row shading on ground-mount arrays limits how steep the tilt can go before rows start shading each other in low winter sun.
  • 5Snow shedding favours steeper tilts in snow-prone climates, sometimes overriding the pure-yield optimum.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Tilt ≈ latitude is a solid starting point for annual yield, not a fixed law — adjust ±10–15° to weight toward summer or winter production.
  • ✓Equator-facing azimuth is the annual-yield optimum; small deviations cost little, but the penalty accelerates past ~30° off-axis.
  • ✓What the system needs to offset — summer cooling load, winter heating, or flat year-round demand — should drive the tilt choice, not yield alone.
  • ✓Roof pitch, racking limits, wind load, row shading, and snow all constrain the "ideal" tilt in practice.
