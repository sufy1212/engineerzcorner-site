Fire Alarm Systems Explained: Detection, Circuits and Standby Power

Detectors, a control panel, sounders, survivable wiring and a backup power supply: what each part does, the NFPA 72 numbers that shape a design, and a worked battery sizing example.

A fire alarm system has one job: detect a fire early and make sure everyone who needs to know finds out quickly. Under that single goal sit detectors, a control panel, sounders, wiring that has to keep working when part of it is damaged, and a power supply that works when the mains does not. The details differ between countries, so this guide uses US NFPA 72 for its numbers and notes where other codes, such as EN 54, may differ.

The chain from detector to evacuation

Inputs: initiating devices

Smoke detectors, heat detectors, manual call points and sprinkler flow switches. They report a condition to the panel.

Outputs: notification and control

Horns, strobes and voice speakers tell occupants to leave. Other outputs can shut down air handlers, release magnetic door holders, recall lifts and signal a monitoring centre.

The fire alarm control panel sits in the middle. It watches every input, decides what the signal means, drives the outputs and supervises its own wiring and power so that a fault is reported instead of staying hidden.

Conventional or addressable

A conventional system wires detectors in zones. The panel knows which zone is in alarm but not which detector. An addressable system gives every device its own address on a signalling line circuit, so the panel shows the exact device, and one pair of wires can serve many devices. Addressable panels cost more but make faults and false alarms far easier to trace, which is why they dominate larger buildings.

Choosing and spacing detectors

Smoke detectors suit most occupied spaces. Photoelectric types respond well to the slow, smouldering fires common with soft furnishings, and ionization types to fast flaming fires. Heat detectors, which respond to a fixed temperature or a rapid rise, suit places where smoke detectors would false-alarm, such as kitchens, boiler rooms and dusty areas.

NFPA 72 uses a nominal spacing of 30 ft (9.1 m) for spot-type smoke detectors on a smooth, flat ceiling. That grid works out to roughly 900 ft² (about 84 m²) per detector, a useful sanity check on a quote but not a code minimum. No point on the ceiling should be further than 0.7 times the spacing from a detector, which is about 21 ft (6.4 m) for a 30 ft grid. Heat detector spacing comes from the listing of the specific detector and is reduced as the ceiling gets higher. Beams, sloped ceilings, airflow from diffusers and tall spaces all change the layout, so the final placement follows the code and the manufacturer's listing, not the nominal grid alone.

Circuits that survive damage

NFPA 72 defines circuit styles by how the wiring behaves when it is cut or shorted. A Class B circuit is a single path: a break stops every device beyond it. A Class A circuit returns as a loop, so a single break still leaves every device reachable from one end or the other, and a fault is reported. Class X adds short-circuit isolation to that. Larger and higher-risk buildings typically use the more survivable styles, while small systems often use Class B.

Standby power: 24 hours, then alarm

The system has to keep working through a mains failure and still sound the alarm at the end of it. NFPA 72 requires secondary power for 24 hours of standby followed by 5 minutes of alarm. Emergency voice and mass notification systems need 15 minutes of alarm instead, because speakers draw more and messages must be heard.

Battery Ah = standby A × 24 h + alarm A × alarm time (h)

Worked example with invented values: a panel and devices drawing 0.5 A in standby, and 3.0 A in full alarm for 5 minutes. Standby needs 0.5 × 24 = 12.0 Ah and the alarm needs 3.0 × 5 ÷ 60 = 0.25 Ah, so 12.25 Ah in total. Designers commonly add a margin for battery ageing and temperature, for example 20%, giving about 14.7 Ah, and then pick the next standard battery size. The margin is design practice, not a figure from the code.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Think of the system as inputs, a supervising panel and outputs, with the wiring and power monitored for faults.
  • ✓Use addressable systems where fast fault-finding matters, and conventional zones for small, simple buildings.
  • ✓Treat the 30 ft grid as a starting point only. Ceilings, beams and airflow change the layout.
  • ✓Size batteries from standby and alarm current, then add a margin and round up to the next standard size.
