4-20mA Current Loops: Why Industry Still Trusts an Analog Signal

Decades into the digital age, most field transmitters still send their reading down two wires as a current between 4 and 20 milliamps. That's not inertia — the format itself is doing real work.

Why current instead of voltage

A voltage signal sent down a long cable run loses accuracy: resistance in the wire itself causes voltage drop, so what the receiving end reads isn't quite what the transmitter sent. Current doesn't have that problem. In a series loop, the same current flows through every point in the circuit no matter how long the wire is or how much resistance it adds along the way — that's exactly why instrumentation moved to reading current rather than voltage for anything running any real distance.

Why the range starts at 4, not 0

Live-zero: the gap that catches a broken wire

If 0% of the measured range were represented by 0mA, there would be no way to tell "the process variable is genuinely at zero" apart from "the wire broke, the transmitter died, or the power failed." Both situations would read identically: nothing. Starting the scale at 4mA closes that blind spot — a live, healthy loop reading 0% output should always show 4mA. Anything reading below that, especially a hard 0mA, is a fault signal in itself, not a valid process reading.

This is why 4mA is called live-zero — the loop staying "alive" at the bottom of its range is itself proof the circuit is intact.

Example: a level transmitter ranged 0–5 m reading 12mA → (12−4)/16 = 0.5, so 0.5 × 5 m = 2.5 m. A mid-scale current always means a mid-scale process value, by design.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Current signals stay accurate over long wire runs; voltage signals don't.
  • ✓Live-zero (4mA floor) lets a control system tell "zero reading" apart from "broken loop."
  • ✓A reading at or near 0mA should always be treated as a fault indication, not a valid process value.
  • ✓Digital fieldbuses add more data per device, but 4-20mA remains common for its simplicity, robustness, and universal compatibility across legacy and new systems alike.
