Percentage Points vs Percent Change: Why They're Not the Same Number

"Up 5 percentage points" and "up 5 percent" describe two different jumps — and the gap between them grows the moment the base value isn't 100.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Percentages & Ratios
01
A percentage point is a plain subtraction between two percentages — 25% minus 20% is 5 percentage points, full stop.
02
Percent change is that same difference measured relative to the starting value — here, 5 points on a base of 20 is a 25% change.
03
The two numbers only match when the base is 100 — everywhere else, percent change is larger (rising from a small base) or smaller (rising from a large base) than the point difference.
04
Mixing the two is a common source of misleading headlines and mis-stated KPIs, not just a pedantic distinction.

Two different questions

"Percentage points" and "percent" answer different questions about the same pair of numbers. A percentage point is a direct subtraction: if a rate moves from 20% to 25%, it has moved 5 percentage points, because 25 minus 20 is 5. Percent change asks a different question: relative to where it started, how big was that move? Going from 20 to 25 is a jump of 5 units on a starting value of 20, which works out to a 25% increase — not 5%.

Both numbers are correct descriptions of the same event. They just answer "how far did it move on the percentage scale itself" versus "how much bigger is the new value compared to the old one."

The formulas

Δpp  =  Pafter − Pbefore
Δpp = change in percentage points · a plain subtraction of two already-percentage values, no division involved
%Δ  =  (Pafter − Pbefore)  /  Pbefore  ×  100
%Δ = percent change · the same numerator as above, but divided by the starting percentage — this is what turns a fixed point-gap into a base-dependent ratio

Why the gap grows at small bases

The smaller the starting value, the more dramatic percent change looks for the same point move. A move from 2% to 4% is only 2 percentage points — but it's a 100% increase, because the new value is double the old one. The same 2-point move from 50% to 52% is only a 4% increase. The point difference is identical in both cases; the percent change is not, because it's being divided by a much smaller starting base in the first example.

Small baseA given point move produces a large, sometimes eye-catching percent change — e.g. 1% to 2% is "+1pp" but also "+100%".
Large baseThe same point move produces a small percent change — e.g. 60% to 61% is "+1pp" but only "+1.7%".

Where the mix-up causes real problems

This distinction shows up constantly in interest rates, survey results, conversion rates, and error rates. An interest rate moving from 3% to 4% is a 1-percentage-point rise, but a 33% increase in the rate itself — both descriptions are used in reporting, and swapping one for the other overstates or understates the change depending on direction. A defect rate falling from 4% to 2% is a 2-point drop, but a 50% reduction in defects, which is the number that actually matters for a quality report. Reading "percent" when the source meant "percentage points" (or the reverse) can make a modest shift look dramatic, or a dramatic shift look modest.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Percentage points is a subtraction between two percentages — it doesn't reference the starting value at all.
  • ✓Percent change divides that same difference by the starting value, so it depends heavily on how large or small the base is.
  • ✓The two figures are only equal when the starting value is exactly 100.
  • ✓When reading or writing about a rate change, state explicitly which one you mean — "points" or "percent" are not interchangeable words.
