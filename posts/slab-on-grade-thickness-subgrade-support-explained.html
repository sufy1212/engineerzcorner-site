Slab-on-Grade Thickness: What Actually Drives the Number

A slab-on-grade isn't a beam spanning between supports — the ground underneath is part of the structural system, and that changes how thickness gets decided.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Civil / Structural
01
A slab-on-grade is supported continuously by the ground, not spanning between discrete points.
02
Subgrade modulus (k) describes how stiffly the ground pushes back — and it moves thickness as much as load does.
03
The same load needs a thicker slab on soft, poorly compacted ground than on well-prepared, stiff subgrade.
04
Point/wheel loads and uniform loads are checked differently, and often both apply to the same slab.

Why slab-on-grade design isn't beam design

A structural slab spanning between beams or columns is checked against bending between those support points. A slab-on-grade instead rests on soil across its entire underside, which acts more like a bed of springs than a set of point supports — the ground itself deflects and redistributes load. That's why slab-on-grade thickness design (commonly via the PCA method or similar) treats the subgrade as an active part of the system, not just "whatever's underneath."

Subgrade modulus: the ground's own stiffness number

Bearing Pressure ÷ Deflectionfrom a plate load test
k is measured (or estimated from soil type/CBR correlations) in units of pressure per unit deflection. A higher k means the ground resists settling more per unit of load — which lets a thinner slab carry the same load without excessive deflection or cracking.

What moves the required thickness

FactorEffect on required thickness
Higher subgrade modulus (k)Reduces required thickness
Heavier point/wheel loadsIncreases required thickness
Higher concrete flexural strengthReduces required thickness
Poor compaction / soft spotsIncreases required thickness — or requires subgrade improvement instead
Well-graded compacted base courseEffectively raises the working k value the slab sees

Two different load checks, often both needed

Uniform loads (storage racking footprints, general floor loading) are checked differently from concentrated point or wheel loads (forklifts, truck axles) — a concentrated load creates a much higher local stress for the same total tonnage, because it isn't spread over the whole slab area. A warehouse floor often has to satisfy both checks: uniform storage load across the field, and concentrated wheel loads along travel aisles.

A compacted granular base course under the slab isn't just drainage — it measurably increases the effective subgrade modulus the slab bears on, which is often the more cost-effective way to reduce required slab thickness compared with adding concrete.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Slab-on-grade thickness depends on subgrade stiffness as much as it depends on load.
  • ✓Subgrade modulus (k) is a measured or estimated property — it isn't a fixed constant across sites.
  • ✓Point/wheel loads and uniform loads are separate checks — many slabs need both satisfied.
  • ✓Improving the base course is often cheaper than adding slab thickness for the same capacity gain.
