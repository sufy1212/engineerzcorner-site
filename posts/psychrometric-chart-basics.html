Reading a Psychrometric Chart: The Five Lines That Matter

A psychrometric chart looks like a wall of overlapping curves, but it's really just five properties of moist air plotted against each other. Once you know which line is which, the chart tells you almost everything a cooling process is doing.

One point, five properties

Moist air has more going on than a single temperature reading can capture. A psychrometric chart plots dry-bulb temperature on the horizontal axis and humidity ratio on the vertical axis, then overlays curved lines for relative humidity, wet-bulb temperature, and enthalpy across that same grid. Because air at a given pressure only needs two independent properties to be fully defined, plotting any two of them — say dry-bulb and relative humidity — automatically fixes every other property at that point.

That's the whole value of the chart: instead of solving separate equations for humidity ratio, enthalpy, and dew point, you read them straight off the same point.

The five lines that matter

PropertyAxis / line shapeWhat it tells you
Dry-bulb temperatureHorizontal axis, vertical linesWhat a normal thermometer reads
Humidity ratioVertical axis, horizontal linesMass of water vapour per mass of dry air
Relative humidityCurved family, 10–100%How close the air is to saturation
Wet-bulb temperatureDiagonal lines, upper-left to lower-rightEvaporative cooling limit; sets cooling-tower performance
EnthalpyDiagonal lines, close to wet-bulbTotal heat content — sensible plus latent — per kg of dry air

Tracing a cooling process across the chart

Where the process line points tells you what the coil is actually doing

Straight down (sensible-only)

Dry-bulb temperature drops, humidity ratio stays flat. This happens when the coil surface stays above the air's dew point — no condensation, no dehumidification, just cooling.

Down and to the left (cooling + dehumidification)

Both dry-bulb temperature and humidity ratio fall. The coil surface has dropped below the entering air's dew point, so water vapour is condensing out as the air cools — the normal state for most comfort-cooling coils.

Extend that diagonal process line backward until it hits the 100% RH curve, and the dry-bulb temperature at that intersection is the entering air's dew point — the surface temperature at which condensation would just start to form.

A worked example

Take air at 24°C dry-bulb and 50% relative humidity — a common indoor design condition. Locating that point on the chart reads off a humidity ratio around 9.3 g/kg, an enthalpy near 47.5 kJ/kg, and a wet-bulb temperature around 17°C. Cool that same air sensibly to 16°C without removing any moisture, and the point moves straight down to a relative humidity near 90% — close enough to saturation that condensation risk on any cold surface becomes a real concern.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Any two independent properties on the chart fix the full state — that's why plotting one point unlocks all five values.
  • ✓A vertical process line means sensible-only cooling; a diagonal one means moisture is also being removed.
  • ✓Dew point is read by extending the process line back to the 100% RH curve, not by reading straight down from the point itself.
  • ✓Wet-bulb and enthalpy lines run close together but aren't identical — for quick estimates they're often used interchangeably, but don't treat them as the same value in precise work.
