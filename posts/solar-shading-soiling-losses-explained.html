Solar Shading and Soiling Losses: Why Small Obstructions Cost More Than Their Size

A chimney shadow or a layer of dust takes only a small bite of the array, yet can take a bigger bite of the output. Here is why it happens and how to judge whether it matters on your roof.

Solar yield estimates usually assume a clean array in full sun. A real roof has a vent pipe, a chimney or a tree, and the glass collects dust between rains. Both effects are small in area but can cost more energy than their size suggests, which is why they deserve their own line in any loss budget.

Why a little shade costs a lot

The cells in a panel are wired in series, and a series chain can only pass as much current as its weakest element. Shade one cell and it limits the current of every cell it is connected to, so the loss is far larger than the shaded area. To stop that, each module has bypass diodes. A typical module is split into three groups of cells, each with its own diode. NREL's partial-shading work describes modules of three groups of 18 cells, where a conducting bypass diode removes that group's share of the module voltage and lets current pass around it.

What the bypass diode does

It gives current a path around a shaded group of cells. The module gives up roughly that group's share of its voltage and power, about a third for a three-group module, instead of being dragged down in full.

What it cannot do

It cannot restore the lost group. If several modules in one string are partly shaded, the string still loses output, and the inverter's tracking has to work around the changed voltage.

Timing matters as much as size. Shade at midday removes light when the array would otherwise produce the most, while shade only at the start or end of the day costs much less. Equipment that works per module, such as microinverters or power optimizers, limits how far one shaded panel affects the others. See string vs microinverter vs power optimizer for that trade-off.

Soiling: the slow loss

Dust, pollen, bird droppings and film reduce the light reaching the cells. The loss builds up between rains or cleanings, and it varies widely with place.

Figures reported from NREL work and industry summaries. Your site can differ a lot, so measure where you can.

Rain does much of the cleaning, so the same array loses far more in a dry season than in a wet one. Tilt helps too, since dirt sheds more easily from a steep panel than from a flat one. In one NREL modelling example, reported by pv magazine, two cleanings a year reduced an average 5% soiling loss to about 1.3%, so the first cleaning recovers the most.

Is cleaning worth paying for?

It comes down to one comparison: the value of the energy recovered against what the cleaning costs. Take a hypothetical system producing 5,000 kWh a year. If soiling takes 5% and cleaning brings the average loss down to 2%, the recovered energy is 5,000 × 0.03 = 150 kWh. At a tariff of 0.15 per kWh that is worth 22.50 a year, so a cleaning that costs more than that does not pay for itself on energy alone. A large dusty array with a high tariff can tip the other way, which is why the answer is site-specific.

Putting numbers on your own site

Check shade at the times that matter, midday above all, and note the seasons, since the sun is lower in winter and casts longer shadows. Then use the shading and soiling loss estimator for a rough annual figure to sanity-check a quote. Remember that the general loss allowance in a yield estimate already includes an average for these effects, as covered in solar panel derating factors, so avoid counting the same loss twice. Key takeaways

  • ✓Shade loss is out of proportion to the shaded area because of the series cell connection.
  • ✓Midday shade costs more than morning or late-afternoon shade.
  • ✓Soiling varies from a few percent to much higher in dusty, dry places, and rain does a lot of the cleaning.
  • ✓Compare the value of the recovered energy with the cleaning cost before scheduling regular cleanings.
