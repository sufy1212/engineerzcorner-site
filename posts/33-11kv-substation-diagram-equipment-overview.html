33/11 kV Substation: Single Line Diagram & Equipment Overview

A 33/11 kV substation is the step between bulk transmission and the network that actually reaches consumers. Here's every piece of equipment along that path, in the order power actually flows through them.

What a 33/11 kV substation does

A 33/11 kV substation is the backbone link between the bulk transmission network and the local distribution network that actually reaches buildings and consumers. It receives power at 33 kV, steps it down to 11 kV through a power transformer, and redistributes it across multiple outgoing feeders — each of which then feeds further distribution transformers that step down again to utilization voltage. Everything else in the substation exists to make that one transformation happen safely: isolating equipment for maintenance, protecting against faults, and metering what's flowing through.

The single line diagram, stage by stage

Reading the SLD top to bottom follows the actual path power takes through the station:

1
33 kV Incoming LinePower arrives from the upstream transmission network.
2
Lightning Arrester (LA)Diverts lightning and switching surges to earth before they reach the equipment.
3
Isolator with Earth SwitchProvides a visible, off-load break for safe isolation, plus an earth switch for maintenance safety.
4
Current Transformer (CT)Steps down line current to a safe, measurable level for metering and protection relays.
5
Circuit Breaker (VCB / SF6 CB)Interrupts fault current on command from the protection relays — the primary fault-clearing device on the incomer.
6
CT + PT/CVTA second CT plus a potential transformer or capacitor voltage transformer, giving both current and voltage signals for metering and protection.
7
33 kV BusbarThe common connection point the incomer feeds into, and from which the power transformer draws.
8
33/11 kV Power Transformer (10/16/20 MVA)The core of the station — steps 33 kV down to 11 kV at the substation's rated capacity.
9
11 kV Circuit BreakerProtects the transformer's secondary side and the downstream 11 kV busbar.
10
11 kV BusbarDistributes the stepped-down supply to each outgoing feeder.
11
Feeder-1, Feeder-2, Feeder-3 …Each feeder, with its own breaker, carries power out to a section of the distribution network.

Power flow, end to end

Beyond the substation's own busbars, the same sequence continues out to the consumer: 33 kV incoming line → lightning arrester → isolator → CT → circuit breaker → 33 kV busbar → power transformer (33/11 kV) → 11 kV circuit breaker → 11 kV busbar → outgoing feeders → distribution transformers → consumers. Every arrow in that chain is a point where the supply is switched, protected, or transformed — nothing in a substation is decorative.

The DC battery bank deserves a second look: it's what lets protection relays and breakers operate correctly even during an AC supply disturbance on the very station they're protecting. A substation that loses its own AC feed still needs to be able to trip a breaker — that's the entire reason the battery-backed DC supply exists as a separate system from the station transformer.

Protection provided

Overcurrent & earth fault Over Current Protection (50/51), Earth Fault Protection (50N/51N), Restricted Earth Fault (REF) — the first line of defence against phase and ground faults.
Transformer & equipment protection Differential Protection (87T), Buchholz Relay, Surge Protection (lightning arresters) — specifically guarding the power transformer and its insulation.
  • Over Flux Protection (24)
  • Over Voltage Protection (59)
  • Under Voltage Protection (27)
  • Thermal & Other Auxiliary Alarms

These schemes layer deliberately: overcurrent and earth fault relays are the general-purpose backstop, differential protection (87T) is specific to the power transformer and reacts far faster because it compares current in versus current out, and the Buchholz relay catches developing faults inside the transformer tank itself — gas accumulation from arcing or insulation breakdown — before they escalate into something the electrical protection alone would only catch after the fact.

Key features of a well-run substation

  • ✓Reliable and continuous power supply to the downstream network.
  • ✓Safe operation and maintenance, backed by visible isolation and earthing.
  • ✓Advanced protection system with layered relay schemes.
  • ✓Efficient power management across all outgoing feeders.
  • ✓Built for sustainability and future load growth.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The SLD is the physical power path: LA → isolator → CT → breaker → busbar → transformer, repeated at 33 kV and again at 11 kV.
  • ✓CTs and PTs exist purely to feed metering and protection relays — they carry no load current or voltage themselves.
  • ✓Differential (87T) and Buchholz protection are transformer-specific and layered on top of general overcurrent/earth fault protection.
  • ✓The station's own DC battery bank and station transformer keep protection and auxiliary loads running independent of the substation's outgoing supply.
