RCD, RCCB and RCBO Explained: How Residual Current Protection Works and Which Type to Use

A breaker cannot see a 30 mA leak through a person. An RCD can. Here is how it senses current imbalance, what its trip limits are, what types AC, A, F and B detect, and how to choose and test one.

Most electric shocks do not come from touching live parts on purpose. They come from current that finds a way to earth through a damaged cable, a wet appliance or a person. A circuit breaker will not notice a leak of 30 milliamps, because it is far below any overload setting. A residual current device, or RCD, is built for exactly that gap.

How an RCD senses a leak

Inside the RCD, the live and neutral conductors pass through a ring-shaped current transformer. In a healthy circuit, the current going out on the live equals the current returning on the neutral, so the magnetic effects cancel and the transformer sees nothing. If some current leaks to earth, perhaps through a person, the two no longer match. The difference, called the residual current, induces a signal that trips the device, normally within a few tens of milliseconds.

What it protects against

Earth leakage: current flowing from a live conductor to earth through a fault, a damaged insulation or a person. It also gives some protection against fire caused by earth faults.

What it does not protect against

Overload and short circuit, which need a breaker or fuse. It also cannot detect a person touching live and neutral together, because the current returns through the neutral, so there is no imbalance.

RCCB or RCBO?

An RCCB is an RCD without overcurrent protection, so it needs a breaker or fuse upstream to guard against overload and short circuits. An RCBO combines the RCD and a circuit breaker in one device, so each circuit gets both protections and a leak on one circuit trips only that circuit.

Ratings and trip limits

The rated residual operating current is written IΔn. Common values are 10, 30, 100, 300 and 500 mA. For additional protection against electric shock in final circuits, IEC 60364-4-41 calls for RCDs rated at 30 mA or less. A device does not trip at exactly its rating. The standard gives a window, and a time limit that gets shorter as the leak grows.

ConditionGeneral RCD (IEC 61008/61009)Time-delayed S-type
Below 0.5 × IΔnMust not tripMust not trip
At 1 × IΔnTrips within 300 msTrips between 130 and 500 ms
At 2 × IΔnTrips within 150 msTrips between 60 and 200 ms
At 5 × IΔnTrips within 40 msTrips between 50 and 150 ms

Worked example for a 30 mA RCD on AC leakage: it must stay in at 15 mA or less and will trip somewhere between 15 and 30 mA, so a steady leak of 20 mA may or may not trip it. At 150 mA, which is 5 times its rating, it must open within 40 ms. That speed is the reason it protects people: the risk to the body depends on the current and how long it flows, and 30 mA with a fast trip is meant to be below the dangerous zone in the IEC 60479 body-current curves. North American GFCIs for personnel protection work on the same idea, with a lower trip level of about 5 mA.

Types AC, A, F and B

Modern loads such as drives, chargers and electronic power supplies can produce leakage that is not a plain sine wave. The RCD type says which kinds of leakage it will detect.

TypeDetectsTypical use
ACSinusoidal AC residual current onlySimple resistive loads
AAC and pulsating DC, and tolerates a small smooth DC offsetMost general circuits with electronics
FAs type A, plus mixed frequencies up to 1 kHz and a larger DC offsetCircuits with single-phase drives and inverters
BAll of the above plus smooth DC and higher-frequency ACThree-phase drives, EV charging, PV inverters

A type AC device can be blinded by a DC component, so it may fail to trip on a fault that a type A device would catch. Which types the code accepts for which circuits depends on the country and the equipment, so check the local rules and the equipment manual, especially for EV chargers and drives.

Choosing and applying them

  • Personal protection: 30 mA, general type, on socket circuits and wet areas.
  • Discrimination: an S-type with a built-in delay upstream lets the 30 mA device nearer the fault trip first. The downstream device should be rated at one third or less of the upstream one. See breaker selectivity.
  • Fire protection: 300 mA devices are commonly used on circuits where a small earth fault could cause a fire, since 300 mA is not a shock-protection rating.
  • Nuisance tripping: many electronic loads leak a little to earth through their filters, and the leakage adds up on a shared circuit. Splitting loads across RCDs or using a short-time-delay type helps.

Testing

Press the built-in test button regularly, for example every three months, since it checks the mechanism. A test instrument then confirms the actual trip points and times. Instrument tests commonly apply half the rated current, where the device should not trip, and the full rated current, where it should trip within its limit. Note that some regulations have changed which tests are required, so follow the current edition. An RCD that is bypassed because it trips too often is a hazard, so fix the leakage instead.

For how earthing arrangements change what an RCD can do, read earthing and grounding systems and earthing vs grounding. For the overcurrent side, see circuit breaker types and switchgear protection devices, and for the insulation faults that cause leakage, insulation resistance testing.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Use 30 mA RCDs for personal protection and 300 mA devices for fire protection on suitable circuits.
  • ✓Choose RCBOs to give each circuit overcurrent and leakage protection together.
  • ✓Pick the RCD type for the load, since type AC can miss DC components from electronics.
  • ✓Test the built-in button regularly, check trip times with an instrument and fix nuisance trips instead of bypassing the device.
