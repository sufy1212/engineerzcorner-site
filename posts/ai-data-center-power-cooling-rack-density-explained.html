AI Data Centers Explained: Why Racks Jumped to 120 kW and Went Liquid-Cooled

AI racks went from 10 kW to 120 kW and beyond. Here is what that does to cooling, voltage and the power chain, with a density ladder, the heat-balance maths and a calculator to try.

Ask anyone in the electrical or HVAC business what has changed most in the last two years and many will say the same thing: AI. The computers that train and run today’s large models are packed into racks that draw as much power as a small factory. That has forced a rethink of everything around them, from the cooling loops to the voltage on the busbars. This post puts numbers and pictures on it, with a calculator you can play with.

A rack that draws 120 kW

A typical enterprise rack draws 7 to 10 kW, and Uptime Institute surveys, as reported, put the average of operators’ most common rack densities at about 11 kW in 2026. NVIDIA lists about 120 kW for a GB200 NVL72 rack, which holds 72 GPUs and 36 CPUs in one liquid-cooled frame, and 135 kW for the GB300 version. Facility designers such as Vertiv plan around 130 and 142 kW. Next-generation racks are listed even higher.

Why air gave up

Air cooling reaches a practical limit near 30 kW per rack. Beyond that, the fans that move the air draw serious power, make serious noise and still struggle. The physics is the heat balance Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT. Water has a far larger specific heat and density, so a small flow does the work of a very large airflow.

Why liquid wins: the same heat, 3,500 times less volume

Heat removed = mass flow × specific heat × temperature rise

Cooling 120 kW with air

14,000 cfm

6.6 m³/s of air through one rack, with a 15 K temperature rise. Fans, ducts and floor space cannot keep up.

Cooling 120 kW with water

115 L/min

1.9 L/s with the same 15 K rise. Pipes the size of a garden hose carry the load.

Air at 1.2 kg/m³ and 1.005 kJ/kg·K. Water at 4.186 kJ/kg·K. Water carries about 3,500 times as much heat per unit volume, which is why a thin pipe replaces a duct.

For this reason, GB200-class racks are sold with direct-to-chip liquid cooling and no air-only option. Cold plates sit on the processors, and the liquid goes to a coolant distribution unit that exchanges heat with the facility water loop. Some heat still goes to the air, so rooms still need airflow. For the airflow side of the same maths, see the sensible heat airflow calculator and sensible vs latent heat.

PUE: how much overhead?

Power usage effectiveness is total facility power divided by IT power. A PUE of 1.5 means half a watt of overhead for every watt that reaches the servers. The Uptime Institute’s 2025 survey put the global average at 1.54, flat for years. Purpose-built liquid-cooled halls target about 1.1 to 1.25. On a 10 MW IT load, going from 1.54 to 1.15 cuts the facility from 15.4 MW to 11.5 MW, saving 3.9 MW. Over 8,760 hours that is about 34,000 MWh a year, or roughly 2.7 million a year at 0.08 per kWh. Higher voltage, thinner copper

Delivering 120 kW at the 54 V common in older rack designs needs more than 2,000 A, which means heavy bus bars. NVIDIA has announced a move to 800 V DC distribution for coming rack generations, and the maths shows why: the same power needs about 150 A. Lower current means smaller conductors and much lower resistive loss. For how voltage and current trade off in ordinary cables, see voltage drop in LV feeders.

Why the voltage is going up

Current needed to deliver 120 kW to one rack: I = P ÷ V (AC at a power factor of 0.95)

54 V DC2,222 A
415 V AC, 3-phase176 A
800 V DC150 A

For the same conductor, resistive loss follows I²R. Moving from 54 V to 800 V cuts current by about 15 times, so the loss in the same conductor falls by about 220 times.

What it means on site

  • Cooling plant and loops. NVIDIA’s GB200 documentation calls for facility water at roughly 25 to 45 °C through the cold plates, so chillers can often run warmer, or dry coolers can do the job in cool climates. See chiller capacity and COP.
  • Weight. A 132 kW rack can weigh about 1.4 tonnes, so floors need checking.
  • Electrical design. At 120 kW a rack, a hall of 100 racks is a 12 MW load, and the design must handle harmonics and size supply equipment with realistic demand factors.
  • Leaks and monitoring. Water moving through electronics needs leak detection, good isolation and well-planned maintenance access.
  • Heat reuse. Warm return water is a useful heat source, as discussed for ventilation in heat recovery ventilation.

Figures in this post come from NVIDIA specifications as reported, Vertiv reference designs, Uptime Institute surveys and published engineering guides, and they change quickly. Use manufacturer documents for real designs.
