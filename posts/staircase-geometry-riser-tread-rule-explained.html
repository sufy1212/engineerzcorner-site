Staircase Geometry: The Riser-Tread Rule That Keeps Stairs Walkable

Two stairs can each pass a building code check on paper and still feel completely different underfoot — the riser-tread relationship is why.

Why riser and tread can't be picked separately

A tall riser paired with a shallow tread forces a short, steep step; a low riser paired with a deep tread forces an unnaturally long stride at floor level. Neither feels right underfoot, even though both might individually sit inside a code's allowed range. Stair comfort is a relationship between the two numbers, not a property of either one alone.

The 2R + T rule

600–650mmtarget range
This targets an average adult stride length on level ground, applied to the sloped act of climbing. It's a design guideline used across most residential and commercial stair codes worldwide — the exact target band varies slightly by code, but the relationship is the same everywhere.

The logic: on a stair, a stride involves two "flat" foot movements (the tread) and lifting the body's weight twice (the riser, counted twice because climbing costs roughly double the horizontal effort). Balancing the two against a constant keeps the stride length — and the physical effort per step — roughly consistent whether the stair is shallow or steep.

Independent limits on each dimension

DimensionTypical usable rangeCommon use case
Riser height150–190mmLower end: public/accessible stairs. Upper end: max allowed for most residential.
Tread depth (going)250–300mmDeeper treads pair with lower risers on gentle, public-facing stairs.
Nosing projection0–25mmCan add to the effective going without adding to the physical tread.

These ranges exist independently of the 2R+T check — a riser and tread pair can satisfy the combined rule and still fail if either dimension alone falls outside its own allowed band.

Headroom: checked along the slope, not at one spot

Minimum headroom (commonly around 2.0–2.1m) has to clear the full diagonal line of travel up the stair — not just be measured at the bottom or top landing. A soffit, beam, or floor opening that looks fine at the first step can still clip headroom partway up a run.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Riser and tread work as a pair — the 2R+T relationship is what makes a stair feel natural to climb.
  • ✓Each dimension also has its own independent min/max — passing the combined rule isn't enough on its own.
  • ✓A consistent riser height within one flight matters as much as hitting the target range — uneven risers are a trip hazard.
  • ✓Headroom has to be checked along the full sloped line of travel, not just at the landings.
