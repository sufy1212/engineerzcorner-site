Data Center Cooling: Air to Immersion

Rack power density has climbed faster than air can carry heat away, which is why liquid cooling has gone from a niche option to the default conversation for any new high-density rack — and why picking the right type now starts with a single number: kW per rack.

01
Air can only carry so much heat away before the fan power and floor space needed become impractical — which is exactly the wall that AI-era rack densities have run into, pushing liquid cooling from a specialty option to a mainstream design decision.
02
Air, liquid, and immersion cooling aren't strictly a ladder of "better" options — each is matched to a rack density range, and over-specifying liquid cooling for a low-density rack adds cost and complexity for no real benefit.
03
Liquid cooling doesn't replace the facility's chilled water or condenser water plant — it replaces air as the medium that carries heat from the chip to that plant, via a coolant distribution unit that keeps facility water and coolant on separate, isolated loops.
04
PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) is the standard yardstick for cooling and infrastructure efficiency — a PUE of 1.0 would mean every watt fed to the facility goes to IT equipment, with none spent on cooling, lighting, or power conversion losses.

What data center cooling actually has to solve

A rack of servers converts nearly all the electrical power it draws into heat, and that heat has to be removed continuously or the equipment throttles or fails. For decades, moving air across the equipment and back to a cooling coil was enough. As rack power density has climbed — driven largely by dense GPU and AI accelerator hardware — the airflow required to remove that heat at a reasonable temperature rise has grown to the point where air alone becomes impractical: too many fans, too much noise, too much floor space, and diminishing cooling capacity per unit of power spent moving the air. Liquid conducts heat far more effectively than air for the same volume, which is why liquid-based cooling methods have moved from a specialized option to the default conversation for new high-density deployments.

Types of data center cooling

Each type is really matched to a rack density range — the right choice depends on kW per rack, not on which technology sounds most advanced.

CRAC Unit
Computer room air conditioner
A self-contained DX (direct expansion) refrigeration unit that cools and dehumidifies room air directly, rejecting heat to an outdoor condenser. Simple and self-contained, but less efficient at scale than a chilled-water approach.
CRAH Unit
Computer room air handler
Cools room air using chilled water supplied from a central chiller plant rather than its own refrigeration circuit. Scales more efficiently than CRAC across a large facility since the chiller plant serves many units at once.
In-Row Cooling
Cooling unit placed between racks
A dedicated cooling unit placed directly in the row between server racks, shortening the air path dramatically compared to perimeter units. Handles higher densities than room-level CRAC/CRAH by cooling closer to the actual heat source.
Rear-Door Heat Exchanger
Coil mounted on the rack's rear door
A liquid-cooled coil built into the rack's rear door captures hot exhaust air right as it leaves the servers, before it ever mixes into the room. A practical middle step between room-level air cooling and cooling directly at the chip.
Direct-to-Chip Liquid Cooling
Cold plates on the highest-heat components
A cold plate mounted directly on the CPU/GPU circulates coolant that carries heat away right at the source, typically still paired with some air cooling for lower-heat components. The current standard approach for high-density AI racks.
Single-Phase Immersion
Servers submerged in dielectric fluid
Entire server boards are submerged in a non-conductive dielectric fluid that stays liquid throughout, circulated to a heat exchanger. Removes fans entirely and cools every component, not just the hottest ones.
Two-Phase Immersion
Fluid boils and recondenses at the lid
A dielectric fluid with a low boiling point vaporizes on contact with hot components, then condenses back to liquid at a coil in the tank's lid, cycling continuously without a pump. Handles very high heat flux but needs careful fluid and vapor containment.
Free Cooling / Economizer
Uses outside air or water directly
Uses cool outside air or water directly (or through a heat exchanger) to reject heat whenever ambient conditions allow, cutting or eliminating mechanical refrigeration during those hours. A major driver of overall facility efficiency in favorable climates.

Main parts of a direct-to-chip liquid cooling loop

  1. 1
    Cold plateMounted directly on the CPU/GPU, transfers heat from the chip into the circulating coolant.
  2. 2
    Coolant Distribution Unit (CDU)Isolates the sensitive technology-side coolant loop from the facility water loop, via a heat exchanger, pumps, and controls.
  3. 3
    ManifoldDistributes coolant from the CDU to each server's cold plate and collects the return flow.
  4. 4
    Quick-disconnect fittingsAllow individual servers to be serviced or swapped without draining or leaking the whole loop.
  5. 5
    Pump(s)Circulate coolant through the technology-side loop, often in a redundant pair for reliability.
  6. 6
    Leak detection sensorsMonitor for coolant leaks at connections and drip trays, since a leak near live electronics is a serious risk.
  7. 7
    Facility water loop connectionWhere the CDU ultimately rejects the collected heat to the building's chilled or condenser water system.

Selection by rack density

Rack densityWell-suited cooling type
Up to ~10 kW/rackRoom-level CRAC/CRAH with hot/cold aisle containment
~10–30 kW/rackIn-row cooling or rear-door heat exchangers
~30–100 kW/rackDirect-to-chip liquid cooling (typically with supplemental air for remaining components)
100+ kW/rackImmersion cooling (single- or two-phase)

Key performance term: PUE

TermWhat it means
PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness)Total facility power ÷ IT equipment power. A PUE of 1.0 is the theoretical ideal — no power spent beyond the IT load itself.
Why it mattersThe gap above 1.0 represents cooling, power conversion losses, and other overhead — the primary metric used to compare facility efficiency
What lowers itFree cooling hours, efficient chiller plants, reduced fan power, and liquid cooling's lower parasitic cooling load all push PUE closer to 1.0

Selection checklist

  • 1Actual (not nameplate) rack power density, current and planned for future hardware generations
  • 2Whether the facility can supply and reject heat to a liquid loop, or only has an air-based plant
  • 3Retrofit complexity into existing floor layout, structural floor loading, and containment
  • 4Local climate and free-cooling hours available to reduce mechanical cooling load
  • 5Leak detection, containment, and maintenance access for any liquid-cooled equipment
  • 6Target PUE and whether the cooling approach can realistically achieve it
  • 7Serviceability — how easily individual servers can be swapped without disrupting the whole loop

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓Coolant leaks at quick-disconnect fittings or manifold connections, monitored continuously with leak detection sensors
  • ✓CDU pump failure or degraded flow, tracked through differential pressure and flow monitoring
  • ✓Condensation risk where a liquid loop runs below the room's dew point without adequate insulation
  • ✓Air filter clogging on CRAC/CRAH units, quietly reducing airflow and raising rack inlet temperatures
  • ✓Hot/cold aisle containment breaches (missing blanking panels, open gaps) undermining an otherwise well-designed air cooling layout
  • ✓Dielectric fluid degradation or contamination in immersion tanks, checked through periodic fluid quality testing
Liquid cooling doesn't eliminate the facility's chiller or cooling tower plant — it changes what carries heat from the rack to that plant. A coolant distribution unit keeps the sensitive technology-side loop isolated from the facility water loop specifically so a leak or fluid-quality issue on one side never contaminates the other.

Common applications

Hyperscale & cloud data centers AI/ML training & inference clusters Colocation facilities Enterprise on-premise server rooms Edge computing sites High-performance computing (HPC) clusters

Key takeaways

  • ✓Cooling type should follow rack power density, not the other way around — over-specifying liquid cooling for a low-density rack adds cost with little benefit.
  • ✓Direct-to-chip liquid cooling has become the practical standard for high-density AI/GPU racks, with immersion reserved for the very highest densities.
  • ✓A CDU isolates the technology-side coolant loop from the facility water loop — liquid cooling changes the heat-transport medium, not the underlying chiller/tower plant.
  • ✓PUE remains the standard yardstick for comparing how much power a facility spends on cooling and overhead versus actual IT load.
