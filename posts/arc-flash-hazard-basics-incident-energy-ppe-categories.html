Arc Flash Hazard Basics

An arc flash releases as much thermal energy in a fraction of a second as some industrial furnaces do continuously — and the PPE category on the label is really just a shorthand for one number: how much of that energy would reach a worker at a defined distance.

What an arc flash study actually solves

An arc flash is an electrical fault that jumps through the air between conductors or from a conductor to ground, releasing intense heat, light, pressure, and molten metal in a fraction of a second. Unlike a bolted short circuit, the fault current in an arc flash flows through ionized air rather than a solid connection, so its magnitude and duration depend on the specific equipment, voltage, and available fault current at that point. An arc flash study exists to answer one practical question before anyone works on or near energized equipment: how much thermal energy would reach a worker standing at a normal working distance, and what protection does that require?

Main variables that drive incident energy

The IEEE 1584 model (and the older Doughty-Neal-Floyd and Ralph Lee equations it built on) calculates incident energy from a handful of inputs — changing any one of them changes the result.

Arcing fault current
Not the same as bolted fault current
The current that actually flows through the arc, typically lower than the bolted three-phase fault current at the same point. Both matter — the arcing current drives incident energy, and the bolted current is used to select protective device settings.
Clearing time
How fast the protective device trips
Incident energy scales directly with how long the arc is allowed to burn — a protective device that trips faster cuts energy roughly in proportion, which is why upstream coordination and maintenance mode switches matter as much as fault current.
Working distance
Distance from the arc to the worker
Incident energy falls off sharply with distance — standard working distances are assumed for typical tasks, but any work that requires getting closer than normal (troubleshooting inside an open panel) increases exposure.
Electrode configuration & enclosure
Open air vs. enclosed, box size
IEEE 1584-2018 models five electrode configurations and accounts for enclosure dimensions — an arc inside a smaller enclosure directs more energy toward the opening a worker would be standing in front of.
System voltage
Sets which model applies
The IEEE 1584/Doughty-Neal-Floyd equations cover systems up to 15kV; above that, methods such as the Ralph Lee equation are used instead. Voltage alone doesn't determine severity — it's one input among several.
Grounding & bus gap
System configuration details
Grounding configuration and the physical spacing between conductors both influence how readily a fault sustains an arc versus clearing quickly — details that come from the equipment's actual design, not just its nameplate rating.

Steps in an arc flash study

  1. 1
    Data collectionSingle-line diagram, equipment ratings, conductor lengths, protective device settings, and utility fault contribution.
  2. 2
    Short-circuit studyCalculates available bolted fault current at every point in the system, the foundation the arc flash calculation builds on.
  3. 3
    Protective device coordination reviewDetermines actual clearing time at each location, since this is often the single biggest lever on incident energy.
  4. 4
    Incident energy calculationApplies IEEE 1584 (or an equivalent recognized method) using fault current, clearing time, working distance, and equipment configuration.
  5. 5
    Arc flash boundary determinationCalculates the distance at which incident energy drops to the threshold requiring arc-rated PPE.
  6. 6
    PPE selectionChooses arc-rated PPE with a rating that meets or exceeds the calculated incident energy, using the Incident Energy Analysis or PPE Category method — not both, for the same equipment.
  7. 7
    LabelingEquipment is field-labeled with incident energy (or PPE category), arc flash boundary, and required PPE, per the applicable standard.

Key term: arc flash boundary

TermWhat it means
Arc flash boundaryThe distance from a potential arc source at which incident energy drops to the level requiring arc-rated PPE — inside this boundary, PPE is required; outside it, it generally isn't
Working distanceThe assumed distance between the worker's face/chest and the arc source, used as the reference point for the incident energy calculation
Not the same as shock boundariesLimited and restricted approach boundaries (for shock protection) are separate distances governed by voltage, not incident energy — both can apply to the same task

Practical ways to reduce incident energy

  • 1Faster protective device clearing time — often the single most effective lever, since incident energy scales directly with arc duration
  • 2Maintenance mode switches that temporarily lower instantaneous trip thresholds while work is being performed
  • 3Remote racking and remote switching, which increases the effective working distance to zero for the operator
  • 4Arc-resistant switchgear, designed to direct arc energy away from the operator side of the equipment
  • 5Current-limiting fuses or breakers where applicable, reducing let-through energy
  • 6De-energizing equipment before work whenever it's feasible, which remains the most effective control of all

Common mistakes & oversights

  • ✓Assuming a closed panel door removes the need for PPE during switching — NFPA 70E's 2024 edition specifically clarifies it generally doesn't for abnormal operating conditions
  • ✓Mixing the Incident Energy Analysis and PPE Category methods on the same piece of equipment, which isn't permitted
  • ✓Treating arc flash labels as permanent — a study needs updating whenever protective device settings, fault current availability, or equipment changes
  • ✓Confusing the arc flash boundary with shock protection boundaries, which are calculated independently and governed by different rules
  • ✓Overlooking that arcing current, not bolted fault current, drives the incident energy result — a higher bolted fault current doesn't always mean higher incident energy
Clearing time is usually the most practical lever available after a system is already built — protective device settings can often be adjusted with no equipment changes, while fault current, working distance, and enclosure geometry are largely fixed by the installation itself.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Incident energy is the number everything else derives from — the PPE category is a shorthand for it, not an independent judgment.
  • ✓Clearing time usually matters as much as, or more than, fault current — a faster-tripping device can meaningfully cut incident energy without any equipment changes.
  • ✓NFPA 70E's Incident Energy Analysis and PPE Category methods are both valid but can't be mixed on the same equipment — pick one per piece of equipment and stay consistent.
  • ✓A closed door reduces exposure but isn't a substitute for PPE during switching or abnormal conditions — a point the 2024 NFPA 70E edition specifically addressed.
