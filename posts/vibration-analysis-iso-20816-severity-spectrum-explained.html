Vibration Analysis Basics: ISO 20816-3 Severity Zones and What the Spectrum Tells You

Vibration changes before most machines fail. Here is how velocity relates to displacement, how ISO 20816-3 zones classify severity by machine group and how to read the spectrum to find the cause.

A rotating machine that is running well produces a small, steady vibration. When something changes, such as a worn bearing, a loose foot or a shaft that has drifted out of line, the vibration changes with it, often weeks before anything else shows. Measuring it is the oldest and most reliable form of condition monitoring for pumps, fans, motors and gearboxes.

Velocity, displacement and acceleration

Vibration can be expressed as how far the surface moves, how fast it moves or how hard it accelerates, and for a steady sine wave they are linked: v = 2π × f × x, where f is the frequency in hertz and x is the displacement. Overall machine severity is judged on velocity, because across the usual 10 to 1,000 Hz range it tracks the energy that fatigues a machine best. Displacement suits slow machines, and acceleration suits high-frequency bearing and gear faults.

A worked case: a motor at 1,480 rpm runs at 24.7 Hz. If the bearing housing moves 20 micrometres peak, the peak velocity is 2π × 24.7 × 0.020 = 3.1 mm/s and the RMS velocity is 3.1 ÷ √2 = 2.2 mm/s. Severity limits use the RMS figure.

Severity zones under ISO 20816-3

ISO 20816-3, which carries forward ISO 10816-3, rates machines by broadband RMS velocity measured on the bearing housings. Readings land in four zones: A for newly commissioned machines, B acceptable for long-term operation, C unsatisfactory for long-term running but possible for a limited period, and D severe enough to cause damage. The limits depend on the machine size and on whether it sits on a rigid or flexible support.

Older documents based on ISO 10816-1 use different class limits, with lower boundaries for small machines. Check which standard and group an alarm table follows.

A 150 kW pump motor on a rigid base reading 3.0 mm/s RMS is in Zone C. Keep it running if you must, and plan the repair. The same reading on a large machine on a flexible support would still be in Zone B, which shows why the group has to be right before the limit means anything.

Alarms and change

The standard suggests setting an alarm above the machine’s own steady baseline by 25% of the upper limit of Zone B, and not normally above 1.25 times that limit. For a Group 2 machine on a rigid base, the Zone B upper limit is 2.8 mm/s, so a motor that normally reads 1.2 mm/s gets an alarm at 1.9 mm/s, and no alarm on that class would normally sit above 3.5 mm/s. The standard also treats a change of more than 25% of the B/C boundary, 0.7 mm/s here, as significant even inside Zone B. A pump that moves from 1.0 to 1.8 mm/s has not left Zone B, but it has changed enough to need a spectrum.

Reading the spectrum

A single severity number tells you that a machine is in trouble but not why. A frequency spectrum splits the vibration by frequency, and the faults leave recognisable signatures, in multiples of the running speed (1×).

FaultTypical signature
UnbalanceA strong peak at 1× running speed, mostly radial
MisalignmentPeaks at 1× and 2×, often with high axial vibration
LoosenessMany harmonics of 1×, and sometimes half-order components
Rolling bearing defectNon-synchronous peaks at the bearing defect frequencies, with high-frequency noise early on
Electrical (motor)Peaks at twice the line frequency, 100 or 120 Hz, that vanish when the power is cut

Taking a useful measurement

  • Sensor position. Measure on the bearing housings, as close to the bearing as practical, in the horizontal and vertical directions, and axially where it helps diagnosis. Use the same points and the same method every time.
  • Operating condition. Read at normal speed, load and temperature. A cold or unloaded machine gives a different answer.
  • Trend, not just limits. A rising trend at a point is the earliest warning, so keep the history.
  • Machine range. The standard applies to machines running between 120 and 15,000 rpm, and slower machines need measurement from lower frequencies.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Classify the machine by group and support before applying any limit.
  • ✓Judge change as well as level, since a 25% step is significant even inside Zone B.
  • ✓Use the spectrum to find the cause once the overall level or trend says there is a problem.
  • ✓Measure at the same points and operating condition each time so the trend means something.
