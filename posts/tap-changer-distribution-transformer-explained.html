Tap Changer of a Distribution Transformer Explained

Supply voltage drifts, but the output still has to land in range — a tap changer is how a transformer adjusts its turns ratio to compensate, without doing anything fancier than picking a different connection point on the winding.

What a tap changer is actually adjusting

A transformer's output voltage is set by its turns ratio — the number of HV winding turns divided by the number of LV winding turns. A tap changer gives access to several different points along the HV winding, each with a slightly different number of turns between the tap and the winding's start. Selecting a different tap changes that turns count, which changes the turns ratio, which changes the LV output voltage for the same HV supply — all without touching the LV winding at all.

This matters because incoming supply voltage isn't perfectly constant. It drifts with loading on the wider network, distance from the source substation, and time of day. Without some way to compensate, that drift would show up directly as out-of-range voltage at the transformer's LV output. The tap changer is the mechanism that absorbs that drift by nudging the turns ratio instead.

Where it sits, and what kind it usually is

HV side, off-load type — the two defaults for distribution transformers

In distribution transformers, the tap changer is almost always provided on the HV winding rather than the LV winding — HV current is lower for the same power transfer, so the tap connections and switching contacts can be lighter and simpler there. It's also almost always an off-load tap changer (OLTC — off-load, not on-load): the transformer has to be de-energized and taken off supply before the tap position can be changed. That's a deliberate simplification for distribution-class equipment, where taps are set occasionally (commissioning, or a seasonal adjustment) rather than continuously, unlike the on-load tap changers used on larger power transformers that need to regulate voltage while staying in service.

Off-load tap changerSimple contact-and-resistor selector switch; must be switched with the transformer isolated. Standard on distribution transformers.
On-load tap changerMore complex mechanism (diverter switch, transition resistors/reactors) that changes taps while the transformer stays energized and loaded — used on larger power/grid transformers where taking the unit offline isn't practical.

Typical tap positions and their effect

A common arrangement is a ±5% tap range in 2.5% steps, giving five selectable positions. More turns on the HV side raises the effective turns ratio and lowers LV output voltage for a given HV supply; fewer turns does the reverse.

Tap PositionChange in Turns Ratio (HV Side)Effect on LV Voltage (Approx.)
+5%More turnsIncreases voltage
+2.5%Slightly more turnsSlightly increases voltage
0% (Neutral)Normal turnsNormal voltage
−2.5%Slightly less turnsSlightly decreases voltage
−5%Less turnsDecreases voltage
Note the direction: a "+5%" tap adds turns to the HV winding, which raises the turns ratio and actually pushes LV output voltage up for a given HV supply — the tap label describes the winding change, not the voltage direction directly, so it's worth double-checking against the nameplate rather than assuming.

Worked example: an 11kV/433V distribution transformer

Picture an 11kV HV supply feeding a transformer normally set to give 433V on the LV side at the neutral (0%) tap. Two situations call for a different tap:

If HV supply dropsSelect the +2.5% or +5% tap (more turns on HV side). LV voltage increases back toward the desired 433V, compensating for the lower-than-normal supply.
If HV supply risesSelect the −2.5% or −5% tap (fewer turns on HV side). LV voltage decreases back toward the desired 433V, compensating for the higher-than-normal supply.

Either way, the goal is the same: land back near the desired 433V output despite the HV side not being exactly 11kV. The transformer itself doesn't change — only which tap point the HV winding connects through.

Inside an off-load tap changer

Physically, an off-load tap changer is a rotary selector switch mounted on (or connected to) the transformer tank, usually with an external operating handle and a position indicator so the set tap is visible without opening the unit. Internally, a selector switch and contact fingers connect to different terminal studs — each stud wired to a different tap point on the HV winding — while the whole assembly sits in an oil chamber shared with the transformer's main tank, keeping the switching contacts insulated and cooled the same way the windings are.

Key takeaways

  • ✓A tap changer adjusts turns ratio by selecting a different HV winding connection point — it's mechanical, not electronic.
  • ✓Distribution transformers almost always carry it on the HV side, as an off-load type switched only while de-energized.
  • ✓More HV turns → higher turns ratio → higher LV voltage; fewer HV turns → lower turns ratio → lower LV voltage.
  • ✓A typical ±5% range in 2.5% steps gives enough adjustment to correct for normal supply and loading variation without changing the transformer.
