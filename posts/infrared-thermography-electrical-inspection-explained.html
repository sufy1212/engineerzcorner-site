Infrared Thermography for Electrical Inspection: Finding Hot Spots Before They Fail

A failing connection heats up long before it trips. Here is how an infrared camera finds it, what conditions a valid scan needs, how the NETA table ranks findings and why comparing similar components beats comparing to ambient.

A loose lug, a corroded joint or an overloaded fuse clip all behave the same way before they fail: they add resistance, and resistance turns current into heat. Infrared thermography sees that heat without touching anything, so a technician can survey a switchboard in service and find problems months before they become outages.

Why hot spots appear

Heat in a connection is I²R. A joint that has gone from a few micro-ohms to a few hundred has the same current passing through, so it dissipates far more power. The heat degrades the joint further, which raises the resistance again, and the process feeds on itself. A camera shows this as a bright spot on an otherwise even pattern.

Survey conditions that decide the result

Load

Heating follows the square of the current, so a lightly loaded panel can hide a real fault. NETA guidance is to scan at a minimum of 40% load or the highest normal load available, and to treat findings on lightly loaded equipment with care.

Surface and emissivity

Shiny bare copper reflects its surroundings and reads cooler than it is. Painted, taped or oxidised surfaces have an emissivity near 0.9 and read accurately. Set emissivity and reflected temperature correctly, or measure a taped reference spot.

Other points matter too: the camera needs a clear line of sight, the covers have to be opened or an infrared window used, and wind or sun on outdoor equipment can distort what the camera sees. Opening live equipment is an electrical hazard, so follow arc flash rules and the site safe-work procedure, and do not rely on the camera as a substitute for them.

How findings are ranked

The ANSI/NETA maintenance testing table ranks anomalies by temperature difference. The preferred method compares a component with a similar one under a similar load, because that cancels out ambient temperature, load and time-of-day effects. The second method compares to ambient air.

Worked example

A three-phase breaker is scanned under steady load with the ambient air at 30 °C. The three lug temperatures are L1 = 48 °C, L2 = 50 °C and L3 = 67 °C.

  • Compared with the similar phases, the average of L1 and L2 is 49 °C, so L3 is 18 °C hotter. That is above 15 °C, a major discrepancy.
  • Compared with ambient, L3 is 37 °C over, which falls in the 21 to 40 °C band, only “monitor”.

The two columns disagree. The comparison with similar components is the better indicator here, since the other two phases carry the same current and show the problem is local to L3. This is why the ambient column should be a fallback. Finding the cause matters more than the label: shut down and inspect the L3 connection, and re-torque or replace it.

Load also scales the picture. If the same scan had been taken at half load, the same fault would show roughly a quarter of the temperature rise, since the rise goes with current squared. An 8 °C rise at 50% load suggests something near 32 °C at full load. That scaling is an approximation, so record the load at the time of each scan.

What temperature cannot tell you

The temperature difference cannot predict how long a connection has left. Two joints with the same rise can fail weeks apart. Other organisations publish less strict limits than NETA, but a hot spot of any size deserves a look. Treat the table as a way to set urgency, then investigate every anomaly.

Making a survey useful

  • Record the conditions: load, ambient temperature, emissivity setting, equipment ID and a matching visible-light photo.
  • Repeat on a schedule, for example annually or quarterly for critical equipment, so you can trend each connection.
  • Find the cause, not just the symptom. Loose terminations, overloaded circuits, unbalanced loads and failing fuses or contactors all show up as heat, and a warm motor may point to the bearings or the winding.
  • Verify after repair with a second scan under load.

Thermography complements other tests, not replaces them. Key takeaways

  • ✓Scan under real load and record the load, ambient temperature and emissivity setting.
  • ✓Compare similar components first and use the ambient column only as a fallback.
  • ✓Temperature rise sets urgency but does not predict time to failure.
