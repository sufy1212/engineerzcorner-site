Duct Sizing: Why Velocity Limits Matter as Much as Airflow

A duct sized purely to carry the required airflow, with no velocity check, is a common source of the noise complaints that show up after the system is commissioned.

Getting to a required airflow

The starting point is a required airflow, either entered directly or derived from room volume and a target air-change rate (how many times the room's air volume is replaced per hour). That figure alone doesn't size a duct, though — the same airflow can pass through a small duct at high velocity or a larger duct at low velocity, and the choice between those has real consequences.

Velocity: the number that actually decides the size

V  =  Q  /  A
V = air velocity · Q = airflow rate · A = duct cross-sectional area

Choosing a target velocity, then solving for the cross-sectional area that delivers the required airflow at that velocity, is the velocity method of duct sizing. Typical velocity targets differ by application — main trunk ducts in an office building run faster than branch ducts feeding diffusers close to occupants, because noise tolerance and available pressure both change along the distribution path.

Why velocity limits exist

NoiseAir velocity through ductwork, fittings and diffusers generates noise that rises sharply as velocity increases — not linearly. A duct sized for a low-noise space (a meeting room, a bedroom) needs a meaningfully lower velocity target than a duct serving a plant room or warehouse.
Pressure lossFriction pressure loss along a duct run scales with velocity squared. Higher velocity means a smaller duct for the same airflow, but it also means a bigger fan and more energy spent pushing air through the system — a trade-off between first cost (smaller ductwork, less building space used) and ongoing fan energy cost.

Circular-equivalent diameter for rectangular ducts

Most velocity and friction-loss guidance is published for round duct. Where space constraints call for rectangular ductwork instead, circular-equivalent diameter converts a rectangular cross-section to the round-duct diameter that would give equivalent airflow capacity for the same friction rate — letting a rectangular duct be checked against the same velocity and pressure-loss references without a separate rectangular-duct dataset.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Required airflow comes from room volume and air-change rate, or can be entered directly.
  • ✓The same airflow can pass through many duct sizes — velocity is what actually determines the appropriate one.
  • ✓Velocity limits balance noise (which rises sharply with velocity) against pressure loss and fan energy (which rises with velocity squared).
  • ✓Circular-equivalent diameter lets rectangular ducts be checked against standard round-duct velocity and friction guidance.
