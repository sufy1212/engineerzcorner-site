Deadband & Hysteresis: Why a Single Setpoint Isn't Enough

A switch that trips on and off at exactly the same value will chatter the moment the signal sits right on that line. Two trip points fix it.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Automation / Instrumentation
01
A single trip point makes a switch chatter every time a noisy signal crosses it.
02
Deadband separates the "switch on" and "switch off" points so the signal has to move past both.
03
Hysteresis is the same idea applied to a control action, not just an alarm or a relay.

Wider deadband cuts chatter but adds lag before the switch reacts to a real change.

The problem with one trip point

Set a single setpoint — say a pump starts when tank level drops below 40% — and the moment the level sits right at 40%, any small ripple in the signal flips the pump on, then off, then on again. That's chatter: rapid on/off cycling driven by noise sitting on top of a signal that's genuinely hovering near the trip point.

Chatter isn't just annoying. It wears out relay contacts, hammers motor starters with repeated inrush current, and can make an alarm screen unreadable during exactly the moment an operator most needs a clean signal.

Deadband width is the difference between the two trip points. The signal has to cross the entire gap — not just tick over a single line — before the output changes state again.

Deadband vs. hysteresis

TermWhere it's usually usedWhat it does
DeadbandAlarms, on/off switches, relaysSeparates the trip and reset points around a single setpoint
HysteresisControl loops, sensors, actuator logicSame separation, applied to a control action rather than a binary alarm

In practice the two terms describe the same mechanism from different angles: a gap between the point that triggers an action and the point that releases it, so the signal has to move a meaningful amount — not just cross a hairline — to flip state again.

An alarm setpoint band (High, High-High, with a reset deadband on each) is the same principle applied to a multi-level alarm rather than a single on/off switch — wider bands trade responsiveness for fewer nuisance alarms.

Choosing a deadband width

Deadband set too narrowDeadband set too wide
Chatter returns as soon as noise exceeds the gapReal changes take longer to register and act on
Contact/relay wear from rapid cyclingProcess can drift further from setpoint before correction starts
Alarm floods during noisy or transitional conditionsSlower alarm response, masking a genuine excursion

The right width comes from the signal's actual noise level, not a round number picked by habit — a deadband narrower than the signal's normal noise band will always chatter, no matter how it's tuned elsewhere.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Chatter happens when a noisy signal repeatedly crosses a single trip point.
  • ✓Deadband and hysteresis both fix it by separating the "on" and "off" trip points into a gap.
  • ✓Widening the gap trades responsiveness for stability — there's no free lunch either direction.
  • ✓Size the deadband against the signal's real noise level, not an arbitrary round number.
