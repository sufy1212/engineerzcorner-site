kW, kVA, and kVAR: What Actually Separates Them

A generator is rated in kVA, a heater in kW, and a capacitor bank in kVAR — and none of those are interchangeable. Here's what each one is actually measuring, how the power triangle ties them together, and why power factor decides how far apart kW and kVA end up.

Three numbers, one circuit

Ask an electrical engineer for "the power" of a system and you'll usually get a follow-up question back: real, apparent, or reactive? In a DC circuit there's only one answer — voltage times current is the power, full stop. AC circuits with inductive loads (motors, transformers, chokes) split that single number into three, because current and voltage stop rising and falling together. Some of the current is doing work; some of it is just sloshing back and forth building and collapsing magnetic fields, contributing nothing to output but still flowing through every cable, breaker, and winding in the circuit.

kW, kVA, and kVAR are how that split gets named. They're not three ways of measuring the same thing — they're three genuinely different quantities that happen to be related by one equation, and mixing them up is how transformers get undersized and capacitor banks get misjudged.

kW — the power that does work

Real power, measured in kilowatts, is the portion of the supplied power that's actually converted into heat, light, mechanical rotation, or whatever the load is built to produce. It's what a heater, a light fixture, or a pump's shaft output is rated in, and it's what an electricity bill is ultimately charging for. Real power depends on the power factor of the load — a motor drawing the same current at a worse power factor delivers less real power for that same current, not more.

kVA — the power the source has to deliver

Apparent power, in kilovolt-amperes, is simply voltage times current, with no regard for how much of that current is actually productive. It's the total electrical "traffic" a source has to be built to handle — every amp of it heats up windings and cable insulation whether or not it's doing useful work downstream. That's exactly why transformers, generators, and UPS units are rated in kVA rather than kW: the equipment has to be sized for the total current it will carry, and it has no way of knowing in advance what power factor the connected loads will present.

kVAR — the power that builds the field, then gives it back

Reactive power, in kilovolt-amperes-reactive, is the energy that inductive equipment — motors, transformers, reactors — pulls from the supply to establish a magnetic field, then hands back to the source as that field collapses, twice every cycle. It performs no net work of its own, but it isn't optional either: a motor with zero reactive power has no magnetic field, and a motor with no magnetic field doesn't turn. The catch is that this back-and-forth current still has to travel down the same cables and through the same transformer as the real power, taking up capacity without ever showing up as useful output.

The power triangle

Because real and reactive power act at 90° to each other rather than simply adding, the three quantities form a right triangle: kW along the base, kVAR up the side, and kVA as the hypotenuse connecting them. The angle between kW and kVA — usually labelled θ — is the same angle whose cosine defines power factor.

Pythagoras applied to the power triangle — rearrange to solve for whichever side is unknown.

Where power factor fits in

Power factor is the ratio of kW to kVA — the fraction of the apparent power that's actually landing as useful output. A power factor of 1.0 means every bit of supplied current is productive and the kVA and kW triangle collapses flat, with zero kVAR. A power factor of 0.7 means the source is delivering nearly 43% more current than the load's real power would suggest, all of it needed just to carry the reactive component. That's the entire reason poor power factor matters commercially: the utility, the cable, and the transformer all have to be sized for the kVA, but the bill and the useful output only reflect the kW.

Worked example — 3-phase supply

GivenLine voltage 415 V, line current 100 A, power factor 0.85 lagging.
Step 1 — kVA√3 × 415 × 100 ÷ 1000 ≈ 71.9 kVA
Step 2 — kWkVA × PF = 71.9 × 0.85 ≈ 61.1 kW

With kVA and kW both known, kVAR follows straight from the triangle: √(71.9² − 61.1²) ≈ 37.9 kVAR. Notice that the source still has to supply the full 71.9 kVA — the 37.9 kVAR of reactive current is real current flowing through the cable and the transformer, even though only 61.1 kW of it ever reaches the load as useful output.

Why a low power factor is worth fixing

  • 1Lower supply current for the same real power delivered, since less current is wasted on the reactive component.
  • 2Smaller cable sizes become viable, because cable rating is driven by current, not by kW alone.
  • 3Less loading on transformers and switchgear, which are limited by kVA, not by useful output.
  • 4Reduced voltage drop across the distribution system as reactive current on the feeders falls.
  • 5Lower electricity costs where utilities apply a power-factor penalty for poor kW-to-kVA ratios.

Key takeaways

  • ✓kW is useful output, kVA is total supplied power, kVAR is the non-working reactive component that magnetises motors and transformers.
  • ✓The three form a right triangle — kVA is always the largest of the three whenever kVAR is non-zero.
  • ✓Transformers and generators are rated in kVA because they must carry the full current regardless of the connected load's power factor.
  • ✓Improving power factor shrinks kVAR and pulls kVA closer to kW, freeing up capacity across cables, transformers, and switchgear.
