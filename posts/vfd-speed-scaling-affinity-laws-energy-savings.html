VFD Speed Scaling: Why Affinity Laws Make Small Speed Cuts Deliver Outsized Savings

Slow a centrifugal pump or fan down by 20% and it doesn't use 20% less power — it uses roughly half.

Speed scaling with frequency

Ns  =  (120 × f)  /  p
Ns = synchronous speed (RPM) · f = VFD output frequency (Hz) · p = number of motor poles

A VFD changes motor speed by changing the frequency of the AC waveform it outputs, since synchronous speed is directly proportional to frequency for a given pole count. Below the motor's base (nameplate) frequency, standard control keeps the voltage-to-frequency ratio (V/Hz) constant, which keeps magnetic flux in the motor roughly constant too — and with it, the motor's available torque across that speed range. Assuming constant slip in RPM terms across the operating range is a reasonable approximation here, and is what makes a quick speed-at-frequency estimate useful for commissioning checks or sanity-checking a speed reference.

Why this matters far more for pumps and fans than it sounds

For centrifugal loads — pumps and fans, as opposed to constant-torque loads like conveyors — the affinity laws relate speed to flow, head/pressure, and power in a way that isn't linear across the board:

QuantityRelationshipAt 80% speed
Flow (Q)Q ∝ N80% of original flow
Head / pressure (H)H ∝ N²64% of original head
Power (P)P ∝ N³~51% of original power

That last row is the one that matters for energy consumption: a 20% speed reduction on a centrifugal pump or fan doesn't cut power by 20% — it cuts power roughly in half, because power scales with the cube of speed. This is the entire economic case for VFD-driven pumps and fans over throttling a valve or damper to reduce flow: throttling wastes energy across a fixed-speed pump or fan that's still spinning at full speed and full power, while slowing the motor down via VFD actually avoids generating that energy in the first place.

Where the simple model breaks down

Above base frequency, the VFD can't keep raising voltage proportionally (it's already at the supply's maximum), so the motor enters field weakening: flux falls, and available torque falls with it. Constant-V/Hz assumptions, and the affinity laws as stated above, apply cleanly below base speed — running a centrifugal load above nameplate frequency for more flow than the motor was designed for needs its own separate torque and mechanical (bearing, vibration) checks, not just an extrapolation of the same formulas.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Below base frequency, motor speed scales directly with VFD output frequency at roughly constant slip.
  • ✓For centrifugal pumps and fans, power scales with the cube of speed — not linearly like flow does.
  • ✓A 20% speed cut on a centrifugal load saves roughly 50% of the power, which is the core case for VFDs over throttling.
  • ✓Above base frequency the motor enters field weakening and loses torque capability — the simple V/Hz and affinity-law relationships no longer apply directly.
