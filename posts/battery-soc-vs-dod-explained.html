Battery SoC vs DoD: Why They're Not the Same Number

State of charge and depth of discharge describe the same battery from opposite ends — but SoC is a real-time estimate built on assumptions, and DoD is a design limit chosen to protect cycle life, not a fixed physical ceiling.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Off-Grid & Battery Storage
01
State of charge (SoC) is how full the battery is right now; depth of discharge (DoD) is how much of its capacity has been used — they always sum to 100%, but they answer different design questions.
02
SoC can't be measured directly in most systems — it's estimated from voltage, coulomb counting, or a combination of both, and each method has known failure modes.
03
The maximum allowed DoD is a chemistry- and design-driven limit chosen to hit a target cycle life, not the point at which the battery is "empty."
04
Usable capacity, not nameplate capacity, is what a system should be sized against — and usable capacity is nameplate capacity times the allowed DoD.

Two views of the same number

SoC + DoD = 100%
SoC = state of charge, the remaining usable capacity as a percentage · DoD = depth of discharge, the capacity already withdrawn as a percentage — they're complementary, not independent

A battery at 70% SoC has been discharged to 30% DoD from full. Systems that specify a "maximum DoD of 80%" are really saying the same thing as "never let SoC fall below 20%" — the two specs describe the identical operating floor from opposite directions, and mixing them up is a common source of undersized battery banks.

How SoC is actually estimated

Voltage-basedReads open-circuit voltage against a known discharge curve. Simple and cheap, but voltage sags under load and varies with temperature, so it's inaccurate mid-cycle.
Coulomb countingIntegrates current in and out over time from a known starting point. Accurate short-term, but drifts over weeks without periodic recalibration to a known full or empty state.
Combined / model-basedBlends voltage, current, temperature and an internal battery model (common in modern BMS units). Most accurate in practice, but only as good as the model's calibration.

This is why two identical battery banks can show different SoC readings for the same real charge level — the number on the display is always an estimate, not a direct measurement, and its accuracy depends entirely on which method (and how well-calibrated it is) sits behind it.

Why DoD limits exist

ChemistryTypical recommended max DoDWhy
Flooded lead-acid~50%Deep, frequent discharges accelerate plate sulfation and shorten cycle life sharply
AGM / sealed lead-acid~50–60%Similar sulfation mechanism, slightly better tolerance than flooded
LiFePO₄ (lithium iron phosphate)~80–90%Much flatter capacity-fade curve vs DoD, tolerates deeper regular cycling

The pattern across chemistries is the same: cycle life falls faster than linearly as DoD increases, so a battery cycled to 50% DoD daily typically lasts far more than twice as many cycles as one cycled to 100% DoD daily — which is why manufacturers publish a recommended DoD instead of just a raw capacity number.

Sizing against usable capacity, not nameplate capacity

  • 1Usable capacity = nameplate capacity × allowed DoD — this is the number that should be sized against daily load, not the nameplate rating on the label.
  • 2Temperature further de-rates usable capacity — most chemistries deliver less capacity in cold conditions than their rated figure assumes.
  • 3Inverter/charge controller low-voltage disconnect settings should align with the chemistry's DoD limit, not be left at a generic default.
  • 4SoC drift from coulomb-counting error compounds over time without a periodic full-charge recalibration, so a battery bank left partially cycled for weeks can report inaccurate SoC.
  • 5Round-trip efficiency losses mean the energy put back in to reach 100% SoC is always somewhat more than the energy withdrawn to reach the DoD floor.

Key takeaways

  • ✓SoC and DoD are complementary views of the same battery state, not two separate specifications.
  • ✓SoC is always an estimate — voltage-based, coulomb-counted, or model-based — never a direct measurement.
  • ✓Maximum DoD is a cycle-life design choice driven by chemistry, not the physical point of "empty."
  • ✓System sizing should use usable capacity (nameplate × allowed DoD), not the nameplate rating alone.
