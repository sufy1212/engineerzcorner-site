Corrosion and Cathodic Protection Basics: Why Steel Rusts and How Coatings and Anodes Stop It

Rust is a small battery. Here is how it works, how to put a number on it, why zinc and magnesium protect steel, and how coatings and cathodic protection work together.

Steel is refined from ore, and it spends its life trying to go back. In air, water or soil that return trip is a slow electrochemical reaction, and understanding it is what makes every protection method make sense. This post draws the reaction, puts a number on it and shows how coatings and cathodic protection break it.

Rust is a small battery

On a wet steel surface, some areas become anodes and others cathodes. At the anode, iron gives up electrons and dissolves. The electrons travel through the steel to the cathode, where they are used up, usually by dissolved oxygen. Ions carry the charge through the water. All four ingredients have to be present.

The corrosion cell: a tiny battery on every wet steel surface

Steel dissolves where electrons leave. Rust forms where the products meet.

1. AnodeWhere metal dissolves into ions
2. CathodeWhere electrons are used up, often by oxygen
3. ElectrolyteWater carrying salts, so ions can move
4. Metallic pathThe steel itself, so electrons can flow
Take away any one and corrosion stops. Coatings cut off the electrolyte. Cathodic protection turns the whole steel surface into a cathode. Reactions: at the anode Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. At the cathode O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The products meet and form rust.

The attack is rarely even. It can spread over the whole surface, or it can concentrate in a pit, a crevice or a joint between two metals. Pits and crevices can penetrate far faster than the average rate suggests, which is why a calculated uniform rate is only part of the story.

Putting a number on it

The simplest measurement is a weight-loss coupon: a small steel sample exposed in the environment, cleaned and weighed before and after.

CR (mm/y) = 87.6 × W ÷ (D × A × t)

W is the mass loss in milligrams, D the density in g/cm³, A the exposed area in cm² and t the exposure time in hours. For mils per year, the constant is 534 and the area is in square inches.

Worked example. A steel coupon (D = 7.86 g/cm³) with 20 cm² exposed loses 150 mg in 30 days, which is 720 hours. CR = 87.6 × 150 ÷ (7.86 × 20 × 720) = 0.116 mm/y, or 4.6 mpy. That is at the top of the “moderate” band. Over a 20-year life, the average loss would be about 2.3 mm, which is the order of corrosion allowance a designer would need, before allowing for pitting. Corrosion tends to speed up in warmer conditions, and a common rule of thumb is that the rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise.

Why zinc protects steel

Different metals have different tendencies to give up electrons, which shows up as a potential measured against a reference electrode. When two metals are connected in the same electrolyte, the more negative one becomes the anode and corrodes, while the other is protected. Zinc, aluminium alloys and magnesium are all more negative than steel, so they sacrifice themselves.

Two ways to apply cathodic protection

Cathodic protection makes the entire steel surface a cathode by supplying electrons from outside. The common target for buried or submerged steel is a polarised potential of at least −850 mV against a copper/copper sulfate electrode, with a polarisation shift of 100 mV as an alternative criterion in some standards. Measurements have to allow for the voltage drop in the soil, which is why surveys often use instant-off readings.

Sacrificial anodes

A more active metal is wired to the steel and corrodes in its place. No power supply is needed.

  • Zinc and aluminium suit low-resistivity soils, seawater and tanks. Magnesium has the highest driving voltage and suits higher-resistivity soils.
  • Simple and low maintenance, but the current is limited and the anodes are used up.
  • Best for smaller or well-coated structures.

Impressed current (ICCP)

A rectifier pushes direct current from inert anodes to the steel. The output can be adjusted.

  • Suits large structures, bare or poorly coated steel and high-resistivity soils.
  • Needs a power source, monitoring and care over polarity.
  • Can interfere with neighbouring buried metalwork, so it needs design and testing.

More is not better. Over-protection can disbond coatings, and on high-strength steels it can cause hydrogen embrittlement, so standards also set a limit on how negative the potential may go. Coatings and cathodic protection are designed together: the coating keeps the bare area small, so the current needed stays practical. The coating specification can reflect this directly. In the Aramco APCS immersion systems, for example, the coating within 2 m of anode connections must be 30% thicker.

The corrosion control toolbox

Most real systems use several defences at once.

🧱
Choose and design
Pick the right alloy, avoid crevices and water traps, drain low points and keep dissimilar metals from touching.
🎨
Coat it
A coating system blocks the electrolyte. See protective coating systems for layers, thickness and blast grades.
⚡
Add cathodic protection
Sacrificial anodes or impressed current for buried pipe, tank bottoms, jetties and offshore steel. Used together with coatings.
🧪
Treat the environment
Corrosion inhibitors, oxygen removal, pH control and drying reduce how aggressive the fluid or air is.
📏
Allow and monitor
Add a corrosion allowance to wall thickness, then track it with coupons, probes and thickness surveys. See pipe wall thickness to ASME B31.3.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Think of every wet steel surface as a corrosion cell, and defend by removing one of its four ingredients.
  • ✓Measure corrosion rate by weight loss, grade it low to very severe, and remember that pitting runs faster than the average.
  • ✓Zinc, aluminium and magnesium protect steel because they are more electrochemically active.
  • ✓Use coatings and cathodic protection together, and watch for over-protection.
