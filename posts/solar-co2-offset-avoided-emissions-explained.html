Solar CO2 Offset: How Avoided Emissions Are Estimated, and Why the Factor Matters

Avoided CO2 is energy generated multiplied by a grid factor. The answer depends heavily on which factor you pick. Here is how average and marginal factors differ, what lifecycle emissions add and how to report a figure honestly.

Every kilowatt-hour a solar array produces is a kilowatt-hour the grid did not have to supply, and grid electricity almost everywhere still carries some carbon. Turning that into a number of tonnes of CO2 avoided is simple arithmetic, but the answer depends heavily on which emission factor you choose, so it is worth understanding before quoting a figure.

The basic calculation

CO2 avoided = energy generated × grid emission factor

For a 5,000 kWh a year system and a factor of 0.42 kg CO2 per kWh, that is 2,100 kg a year, or 52.5 tonnes over 25 years if the output and the factor are assumed constant. The CO2 offset calculator does exactly this, and adds tree and driving equivalents. The hard part is the factor.

Average or marginal factor?

Average factor

Total grid emissions divided by total generation. It describes the whole system. US Energy Information Administration data, as used in one carbon footprint methodology, puts the typical US grid near 0.42 kg CO2e/kWh for 2022.

Marginal factor

The emissions of the plants that actually ease off when demand falls. The US EPA uses a non-baseload (marginal) rate for avoided electricity, updated in 2024 to 0.672 kg CO2 per kWh.

The two can differ by more than half. On the same 5,000 kWh a year, the average factor gives 2,100 kg avoided and the EPA marginal factor gives 3,360 kg. Neither is wrong, since they answer different questions, but a claim should say which one it uses. Regional grids vary too, from hydro- and nuclear-heavy grids to coal-heavy ones, so a national figure may not match your site. Use a recent figure for your own grid, from your grid operator or national energy agency, and quote its year.

Solar is not zero-carbon

Building panels, inverters and mounting takes energy. NREL's harmonized lifecycle assessment, as used in one carbon footprint methodology, gives about 0.043 kg CO2e per kWh for solar electricity over its life. A fair net figure subtracts it: with the 0.42 average factor, 5,000 kWh a year avoids 5,000 × (0.42 − 0.043) = 1,885 kg a year. The calculator on this site counts only the electricity displaced, so it slightly overstates the net benefit.

Lifetime totals: two corrections

  • Degradation. Panel output falls slowly each year. At a commonly cited rate near 0.5% a year, the average output over 25 years is about 94% of year-one output, so a lifetime figure that assumes constant output is roughly 6% too high.
  • A cleaner grid. As the grid adds more low-carbon generation, the factor falls. Using today's factor for 25 years overstates the later years. Many reports use a declining projection instead.

The everyday equivalents

Trees and cars make a figure easier to picture but they are rough. The calculator uses 21 kg of CO2 per tree per year, and figures of 21 to 22 kg per mature tree are commonly cited, though the real number depends on species and age. Its 0.12 kg per km for a car suits a typical European new car. The EPA's average US passenger vehicle figure is higher, about 0.393 kg per mile, or 0.244 kg per km. Choose the figure that matches the audience and say what it is.

Reporting it well

State the energy production, the factor, its source and year, and whether it is an average or marginal factor. Show the lifetime total as an estimate. For the energy side of the calculation, see solar system sizing and derating factors, and for the financial side, payback and ROI.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Quote the factor, its source, its year and whether it is average or marginal.
  • ✓Subtract lifecycle emissions for a fairer net figure.
  • ✓Treat lifetime totals as estimates, since degradation and grid changes alter them.
  • ✓Use tree and car equivalents as a scale aid, with the assumption stated.
