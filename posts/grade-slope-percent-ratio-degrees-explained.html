Grade & Slope: Why Percent, Ratio and Degrees Aren't the Same Number

A '10% grade' and a '1 in 10 slope' sound interchangeable and aren't quite — knowing which convention a drawing uses matters before anything gets built.

One fraction, three ways to write it

Every slope starts as the same simple fraction: vertical rise divided by horizontal run. Percent grade, a ratio like "1 in 20", and an angle in degrees are just three different ways of presenting that one number — and each convention is standard in a different corner of civil practice.

Slopebase fraction
=
Rise ÷ Runvertical ÷ horizontal
Percent grade is this fraction ×100. A ratio "1 : n" describes the same slope as 1 unit of rise per n units of run. The angle θ comes from θ = arctan(rise/run).

Where the conventions come from

ConventionTypical use
Percent gradeRoads, drainage pipe fall, site grading plans
Ratio (1:n)Ramps, embankments, retaining wall batter, roof pitch in some regions
DegreesStructural and geotechnical calculations, trigonometric formulas

Why they don't track linearly against each other

At shallow slopes, percent and ratio track each other closely and degrees stay roughly proportional too — a 5% grade is very nearly a 1:20 ratio and very nearly 2.9°. But the arctangent relationship isn't linear, so the gap widens as slope steepens: a 100% grade isn't 20× steeper than 5% in angle terms — it's 45°, only about 15× the angle of the 5% case. Treating percent and degrees as if they scale together gets increasingly wrong the steeper the slope gets.

A 1:1 ratio is a 100% grade and exactly 45° — the one point where all three conventions line up neatly. Everywhere else, converting between them needs the actual trig relationship, not a straight-line assumption.

Where mixing conventions causes real problems

ContextTypical maximum quoted asRisk of mixing it up
Accessible ramp1:12 to 1:20 ratioReading "12%" as "1:12" understates the actual slope
Pipe invert fallPercent or mm/mConfusing % with ratio changes the fall by an order of magnitude
Slope stabilityDegrees or H:V ratioGeotechnical formulas need degrees — percent has to be converted first

Key takeaways

  • ✓Percent, ratio and degrees all derive from the same rise/run fraction — none is more "correct" than another.
  • ✓They only line up exactly at 45° / 100% / 1:1 — elsewhere the relationship is nonlinear.
  • ✓Always confirm which convention a drawing or spec is using before applying a "maximum slope" figure.
  • ✓Geotechnical formulas typically need degrees — convert from percent or ratio before plugging in.
