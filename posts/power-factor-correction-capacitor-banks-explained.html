Power Factor Correction & Capacitor Banks Explained

A low power factor means your cables, transformers and generators carry current that does no useful work — and your utility may bill you for it. Here's how a capacitor bank fixes that, how to size one, and the harmonic trap that catches people out.

Engineerz CorneRIntermediate · Power Quality
01
Power factor is real power (kW) divided by apparent power (kVA). A low value means more current for the same useful work — bigger cables and transformers, more I²R loss, more voltage drop, and often a utility penalty.
02
A capacitor supplies the reactive power that motors and transformers need right where it's used, so it no longer has to travel from the source. The size required is Qc = P × (tan φ1 − tan φ2).
03
Correcting from 0.80 to 0.95 cuts line current by about 16% and upstream I²R losses by about 29% — but the load's own kW and kWh don't change.
04
Capacitors and system inductance can resonate. Near VFDs and other harmonic-producing loads, an untuned bank can amplify harmonics — which is why detuned (reactor-fitted) banks exist.

Why low power factor costs money

If you're not sure how kW, kVA and kVAR relate, start with kW vs kVA vs kVAR. The short version: motors, transformers and other inductive equipment draw reactive current to build their magnetic fields. That current does no work, but it still flows through every cable, breaker and winding upstream. For a three-phase load the current is I = P / (√3 × V × PF), so a load at 0.80 PF draws 25% more current than the same kW at unity.

  • ✓Capacity is used up. Transformers, generators, cables and switchgear are limited by kVA, not kW. Reactive current takes room that real load could have used.
  • ✓Losses rise with the square of current. Extra reactive current means extra I²R heating in every cable and winding upstream of the load.
  • ✓Voltage drop increases. More current through the same impedance drops more voltage — see voltage drop in LV feeders.
  • ✓Utility charges. Many utilities bill a penalty below a set power factor (thresholds around 0.90–0.95 are common) or charge on kVA demand instead of kW. The rules differ by utility, so check your tariff.

How a capacitor bank corrects it

An inductive load's reactive power is lagging; a capacitor's is leading. Place a capacitor next to the load and the two exchange reactive power with each other instead of drawing it all from the source. The supply now only has to provide the remaining kVAR, so kVA and current fall while kW stays the same.

Required capacitor size: Qc = P × (tan φ1 − tan φ2), where φ = cos−1(PF). P is the load in kW, φ1 comes from the existing power factor and φ2 from the target. The result is in kVAR. The Power Factor Correction calculator does this for you.

Worked example: 400 kW at 0.80, corrected to 0.95

A 415 V three-phase plant drawing 400 kW at a power factor of 0.80.

1Before correction: S1 = 400 / 0.80 = 500 kVA, and Q1 = 400 × tan(cos−10.80) = 400 × 0.750 = 300 kVAR.
2Target PF 0.95: tan(cos−10.95) = 0.3287, so the supply only has to deliver Q2 = 400 × 0.3287 = 131.5 kVAR (S2 = 400 / 0.95 = 421 kVA).
3Capacitor needed: Qc = 300 − 131.5 = 168.5 kVAR. Banks come in steps, so round up to a real combination such as 7 × 25 kVAR = 175 kVAR, which lands at PF 0.954.
4Effect: line current at 415 V falls from about 696 A to about 586 A (−15.8%). Upstream I²R loss falls by 1 − (0.80/0.95)² ≈ 29%.
Correction doesn't reduce what the load itself consumes — the kWh at the meter stay the same. The savings come from avoided penalties, lower upstream losses, and capacity released on transformers, generators and cables.

Where to install the capacitors

The closer to the load, the more of the network you unload — but the more banks you have to buy and maintain.

Individual (at the load)One capacitor per motor, switched with it. Relieves the whole feeder. Keep the capacitor kVAR below the motor's no-load reactive power (commonly about 90%) so it can't over-excite the motor when it coasts down. Never connect one on the output of a VFD.
GroupOne bank serving several loads that start and stop together, such as a motor control centre section. Fewer banks than individual, with most of the feeder benefit.
Central (main LV bus)An automatic bank at the incomer or main distribution board. The most common choice: cheapest per kVAR and easy to maintain, but it doesn't unload feeders downstream of the bus.
Medium-voltage bankUsed on large plants and utility substations where the load is fed at MV. Sized and protected as part of the substation design.

The harmonic resonance trap

The system's inductance (mainly the transformer) and the bank's capacitance form a parallel resonant circuit. Its resonant harmonic order is roughly hr ≈ √(Ssc / Qc), where Ssc is the short-circuit level at the bank's bus and Qc is the bank size in the same units. If hr lands near a harmonic that the plant produces, the current at that frequency is magnified.

Example: a 1000 kVA transformer with 6% impedance gives about 16.7 MVA of short-circuit level on a stiff supply. Add a 675 kVAR bank and hr = √(16,667 / 675) ≈ 4.97 — almost exactly the 5th harmonic, which is what a six-pulse VFD injects. The bank fixes the power factor and overloads itself.

A detuned bank puts a reactor in series with each capacitor so the resonance is pushed below the lowest harmonic the loads produce. The reactor percentage sets where:

The last column is the voltage each capacitor sees, V/(1 − p), so pick the next standard capacitor rating above it. See harmonic sources, effects and mitigation for where these harmonics come from.

A widely quoted manufacturer rule of thumb compares the harmonic-generating load to the transformer rating: up to about 15% standard capacitors are usually fine, 15–25% use over-rated capacitors, 25–60% use detuned reactors, and above 60% a harmonic filter is needed. Use it for screening only — confirm with a harmonic study or your capacitor supplier.

Mistakes to avoid

  • ✓A fixed bank sized for peak load. If the load in the example falls to 100 kW at 0.80 PF it needs only 75 kVAR, but a fixed 175 kVAR bank leaves 100 kVAR of leading reactive power — a power factor of 0.71 leading, and a voltage rise. Use an automatic bank for varying loads.
  • ✓Correcting all the way to 1.00. The extra saving is small and the risk of over-correction is real. 0.95–0.98 is the usual target unless your utility asks for more.
  • ✓Forgetting voltage. A capacitor's kVAR is quoted at its rated voltage and scales with V². A 415 V bank on a 400 V system delivers about 93% of its label.
  • ✓Undersized cable and protection. Size them for roughly 1.3–1.5× the bank's rated current to allow for tolerance and harmonics. 175 kVAR at 415 V is about 244 A rated, so plan for around 350 A.
  • ✓Ordinary contactors and no inrush control. Switching a capacitor draws a very large inrush current. Use capacitor-duty contactors, and inrush-limiting reactors where banks are switched in steps.
  • ✓Touching a "dead" bank. Capacitors hold charge after they're switched off. Discharge devices bring it down over time, but wait, isolate, and verify the residual voltage before working on one.
  • ✓Running a bank on a generator. Generators tolerate little leading reactive power. Check with the manufacturer before connecting a capacitor bank to generator supply.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Low power factor raises current for the same kW, which costs capacity, losses and voltage drop — and sometimes a utility penalty.
  • ✓Qc = P × (tan φ1 − tan φ2). Round up to real bank steps, and check the result against the capacitor's voltage rating.
  • ✓Correction saves penalties, losses and capacity, not the load's own kWh. Aim for about 0.95–0.98 rather than unity.
  • ✓Use an automatic bank when load varies, so it never over-corrects at light load.
  • ✓Where VFDs, UPS or rectifiers are present, check resonance (hr ≈ √(Ssc/Qc)) and use a detuned bank instead of a plain one.
