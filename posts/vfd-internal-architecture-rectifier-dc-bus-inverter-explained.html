VFD Internal Architecture: Rectifier, DC Bus & Inverter Explained

Open up a variable frequency drive and there are really only three power stages inside — a rectifier, a DC bus, and an inverter. Everything a VFD does, good and bad, traces back to what happens across those three sections.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Electrical / Power Electronics
01
Every VFD's power circuit boils down to three stages in series: a rectifier (AC→DC), a DC bus (stores & smooths it), and an inverter (DC→variable-frequency AC) — everything else is support circuitry around those three.
02
A 480V drive runs its DC bus at roughly 678VDC — the rectified peak of the incoming line voltage, not some arbitrary control-circuit number.
03
The inverter doesn't actually produce a sine wave — it produces a rapid series of full-amplitude DC bus pulses of varying width (PWM), and it's the motor's own inductance that filters that into a roughly sinusoidal current.
04
Those same fast PWM edges are the root cause of two well-known drive side effects — input-side harmonics from the diode bridge, and reflected-wave voltage doubling on long output cables.

Three stages, one job each

A variable frequency drive's entire purpose is to take a fixed-frequency, fixed-voltage AC supply and deliver a variable-frequency, variable-voltage AC output to a motor, so the motor can run at any speed rather than just the one fixed line frequency gives it. The most common way to do that — the voltage-source inverter (VSI) topology — does it in three stages, and nearly every general-purpose drive on the market today follows this same basic layout.

The three main sections

Rectifier, DC bus, inverter — first in terms of power flow, in that order.

Rectifier / Converter
AC → DC
Converts the incoming three-phase AC to DC — most commonly a six-pulse diode bridge, giving a fixed DC voltage regardless of drive speed setting. Some drives use SCRs or an "active front end" of transistors instead, mainly to enable regeneration back to the line.
DC Bus
Store & smooth
Mainly capacitors, storing the rectified power and buffering it against ripple — a 480VAC drive's DC bus sits at roughly 678VDC. Often also includes a link choke/inductor for additional smoothing and a pre-charge circuit to limit inrush current when the drive is first energized.
Inverter
DC → variable AC
A bridge of IGBTs (Insulated Gate Bipolar Transistors) that switches the DC bus voltage on and off thousands of times per second across the motor windings, synthesizing a variable-frequency, variable-voltage three-phase output using pulse width modulation.
Control Board
Microprocessor + PWM generator
Runs the control algorithm, reads current/voltage feedback, and generates the precise PWM gate-switching pattern that determines the inverter's output frequency and voltage — the "brains" directing the two power stages downstream of it.
Braking Chopper & Resistor
Deceleration energy dump
When a load decelerates faster than friction alone can slow it, the motor briefly generates power back into the DC bus; on a standard (non-regenerative) drive, a chopper transistor dumps that energy into a resistor as heat to keep the bus voltage from tripping out.
Output Filter
Optional dV/dt or sine-wave filter
Slows the inverter's fast voltage edges before they reach the motor cable — relevant when the cable run is long enough to trigger reflected-wave voltage doubling at the motor terminals (see the related calculator below).

How power actually flows through it

1Fixed-frequency three-phase AC enters the rectifier bridge and is converted to a fixed DC voltage — roughly 1.41× the incoming line voltage (480VAC in gives about 678VDC).
2DC bus capacitors (and often a link choke) store and smooth that DC power, buffering it against line disturbances and the pulsed demand of the inverter stage.
3The IGBT inverter switches the full DC bus voltage across the motor windings in a pattern of varying-width pulses (PWM) — set by the control board to produce the commanded output frequency and voltage.
4The motor's own winding inductance filters those voltage pulses into a roughly sinusoidal current, which is what actually drives the motor at the commanded speed.
Step 3 is also where two of the drive's best-known side effects come from: the diode-bridge rectifier draws non-sinusoidal current from the line (harmonics), and the inverter's fast switching edges are exactly what can trigger reflected-wave voltage doubling on a long motor cable — worth checking with the VFD Cable Length / Reflected Wave calculator before a long run.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Rectifier, DC bus, inverter — nearly every general-purpose VFD's power circuit is those three stages, in that order, regardless of vendor.
  • ✓The DC bus voltage is set by the incoming line voltage through simple rectification, not chosen independently by the drive.
  • ✓PWM doesn't create a sine wave directly — it creates a pulse pattern the motor's own inductance smooths into one.
  • ✓The same fast switching that makes PWM efficient is the direct cause of the drive's input harmonics and output reflected-wave issues — they're two sides of the same mechanism.
