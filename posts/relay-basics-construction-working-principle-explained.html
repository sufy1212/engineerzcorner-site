Relay Basics: Construction, Working Principle & Contact Types

A relay is a small device with a big job: a low-power signal switching a completely separate, higher-power circuit. Here's what's inside one, how the coil and armature make that switch happen, and where relays still earn their place.

What a relay actually is

A relay is a device that opens or closes a circuit when its coil is energized or de-energized. It acts as an automatic switch, but the key detail is what triggers it: a small control signal, not the load current itself. That's what lets a relay provide electrical isolation between a low-power control circuit and a high-power circuit — a PLC output, a sensor, or a push-button can safely switch a motor, a heater, or a bank of lights without ever touching that higher-power wiring directly.

Construction

Strip a relay down and there are five parts doing the work. The coil is an electromagnet wound on a frame (the yoke), which is a fixed iron core that channels the magnetic field. A spring holds a movable armature away from the core when the coil is off. The armature carries the moving contact, and a set of fixed contacts — NO and NC — complete the switched circuit. External terminals bring the coil leads and the contact leads out to the base, keeping the two circuits physically separate everywhere except at that one mechanical linkage.

Working principle

Two states, one moving part

Energize the coil and it becomes an electromagnet, pulling the armature in against the spring. That motion is the entire mechanism: it closes the NO contact and opens the NC contact at the same time. De-energize the coil and the field collapses — the spring pulls the armature back to its rest position, the NO contact opens again, and the NC contact closes. Nothing about the power-side contacts depends on how much current the load draws; they just follow wherever the armature is sitting.

The coil circuit and the contact circuit share no electrical path — only a magnetic field and a mechanical link. That's the isolation a relay is built to provide.

Types of contacts

A changeover (CO) contact has one common terminal that alternates between the NO and NC positions — useful whenever a circuit needs to break one path and make another in the same instant.

Circuit diagram: control side vs. power side

Every relay application is really two separate circuits drawn side by side. The control circuit is low power: a supply, a switch or PLC output, and the coil (terminals A1/A2). The power circuit is what the relay actually switches: line and neutral feeding a load through the COM/NO/NC contact set. The only thing connecting the two drawings is the dashed line representing the armature's mechanical action — no shared conductor, no shared reference.

Specifications that matter when selecting one

  • ✓Coil voltage — must match the control supply (e.g. 24VDC, 230VAC).
  • ✓Contact rating — the voltage and current the load side is rated to switch, not the coil side.
  • ✓Number of poles — SPDT, DPDT and similar describe how many independent contact sets one coil drives.
  • ✓Operating time — how fast the armature moves after the coil is energized.
  • ✓Pick-up / drop-out voltage or current — the thresholds at which the relay reliably operates and releases.
  • ✓Life (mechanical & electrical) — expected operations before the moving parts or the contacts wear out.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Relay = small signal in → electromagnetic action → switching of high power out.
  • ✓NO closes and NC opens on energizing; a CO contact does both, switching one common between them.
  • ✓The control circuit and power circuit are electrically isolated — only the armature's motion links them.
  • ✓Selection comes down to coil voltage, contact rating, pole count and expected operating life for the application.
