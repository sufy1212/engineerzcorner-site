Order-of-Magnitude Checks

A misplaced decimal point or a mixed-up unit almost never moves an answer by 10% — it moves it by a factor of 10, 1000, or more. That's exactly the kind of error a rough estimate catches instantly.

What the check is actually for

An order-of-magnitude check isn't about getting a precise number — it's about confirming the detailed calculation landed in the right neighborhood. The idea is to round every input to one significant figure, do the arithmetic in your head or on the back of an envelope, and see whether the result comes out within roughly the same power of 10 as the full calculation. It won't catch a 15% error. It will catch the errors that actually cause real damage: a misplaced decimal point, a unit left in the wrong scale, or an exponent typed in wrong — the kind of mistake that moves an answer by a factor of ten or more without the arithmetic itself looking obviously broken.

Why this catches what unit-tracking doesn't

Dimensional analysis (tracking units through a calculation) catches a different class of error — using the wrong kind of unit, like feeding a pressure in psi into a formula expecting Pa. An order-of-magnitude check catches something dimensional analysis lets straight through: using the right kind of unit at the wrong scale. Entering "1500" when a field expects kilowatts but the source data was in watts is dimensionally consistent — the units cancel just fine — and yet the answer is 1,000× too large. Only a rough magnitude estimate flags that kind of mistake, because dimensional analysis has no opinion on scale, only on kind.

Doing the estimate

Rough Estimate  =  (1 sig-fig value) × 10exponent  ×  ... for every input, then combine
Round each input hard, keep only the leading digit and its power of 10, and let the powers of 10 add or subtract as the calculation multiplies or divides — the leading digits barely matter for this purpose.
Rough estimate: ~4,000Rounding each input to one significant figure and multiplying gives a same-order-of-magnitude sanity check.
Detailed answer: 3.8 millionA 1,000× gap between the rough estimate and the full calculation is the signature of a missed unit-scale conversion somewhere in the input chain.

When to run it

The check works best run before you see the detailed result, using only the raw inputs — the moment you've already seen the calculated answer, your rough estimate quietly starts drifting toward confirming it rather than checking it independently. Running the estimate first, then comparing, keeps the two genuinely independent. It's also worth re-running whenever an input changes source or unit convention — a spreadsheet fed from a new data source is exactly where a scale mismatch tends to sneak in unnoticed.

Common terms, decoded

TermMeaning
Order of magnitudeA power-of-10 scale for a quantity — two numbers are "the same order of magnitude" if they're within roughly a factor of 10 of each other
Significant figureA meaningful digit in a number; rounding "to one sig-fig" keeps only the leading digit and its power of 10
Fermi estimateA rough, rapid estimate built from simplified assumptions, used to bound a problem before doing the detailed work
Unit-scale slipUsing the correct type of unit at the wrong prefix or scale (e.g., mm entered where m was expected)

Key takeaways

  • ✓An order-of-magnitude check rounds inputs to one significant figure — it's meant to catch scale errors, not to replace the detailed calculation.
  • ✓Decimal-point and unit-scale slips typically show up as a mismatch of 10× or more — exactly what a rough estimate is built to flag.
  • ✓Dimensional analysis catches the wrong kind of unit; an order-of-magnitude check catches the right kind of unit at the wrong scale — the two checks are complementary, not redundant.
  • ✓Run the rough estimate before looking at the detailed answer, or it stops being an independent check.
  • ✓A 20–30% gap between the rough estimate and the detailed answer is normal; a 10× or greater gap is the signal worth investigating.
