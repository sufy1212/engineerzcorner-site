BMS Point Types Explained: AI, AO, BI, BO, MI, MO, BV, AV, MV

Nine two-letter abbreviations show up on nearly every points list a BMS engineer touches — once you can read the pattern behind them, the whole list stops looking like alphabet soup.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Automation / BMS
01
The first letter says what kind of signal it is (Analog, Binary, Multi-state); the second says which direction it moves — Input from the field, or Output to it.
02
AI and BI are things the BMS reads from field devices; AO and BO are commands the BMS sends back out to them.
03
MI and MO carry more than two states — think Off/Low/Medium/High on a VFD — where BI/BO would only ever give you 0 or 1.
04
BV, AV and MV are virtual points: values that exist purely inside BMS logic, with no field wiring behind them at all.

Reading the abbreviation

Every point type on this list follows the same two-part pattern: the first letter tells you what kind of signal it carries, and the second tells you which direction it moves, or whether it's a virtual value that never touches field wiring at all.

A Analog — continuous value
B Binary — on/off, 0 or 1
M Multi-state — more than two discrete states
I Input — field device → BMS
O Output — BMS → field device
V Value — internal/virtual, no field wiring

Physical field points

These six point types correspond to real wiring between a controller and a field device — a sensor, actuator, relay, or drive sitting somewhere in the building.

BOBinary OutputSends a digital ON/OFF command from the BMS to a field deviceDry contact / relay outputExhaust fan start/stop

Virtual points: BV, AV, MV

These three don't correspond to any physical terminal at all — they're values that live entirely inside the BMS's own logic, used for scheduling, calculations, and internal status flags that other points or graphics can reference.

Abbr.Full formDescriptionTypical valueExample
BVBinary ValueInternal virtual point with a binary value, used in logic or scheduling0 or 1Night mode active flag
AVAnalog ValueInternal virtual point with an analog value, used in calculations or logicFloating value (any range)Calculated average zone temperature
MVMulti-State ValueInternal virtual point with a multi-state value, used in logic or statusMulti-state (2+ states)System mode: Off/Cool/Heat/Auto

Common signal types behind the abbreviations

Analog signals0–10V DC and 4–20mA are the two standard ranges behind almost every AI/AO point — sensors and actuators are wired and scaled to whichever one the device supports.
Binary & multi-state signalsBinary points ride on a simple dry contact (open/closed = 0/1); multi-state points instead carry a discrete step value, commonly read over a serial or network connection to the device rather than a single wire pair.

Why the distinction actually matters

Knowing which letter combination you're looking at tells you, before you ever open a wiring drawing, whether a point is something you'd trace back to a physical terminal on a controller or something that only exists in software. AI and BI mean you should be able to find a sensor or status contact wired in; AO and BO mean there's an actuator or relay on the receiving end. BV, AV and MV, by contrast, are a dead end if you go looking for wiring — they only ever resolve to a line of logic, a schedule, or another point's calculated result.

Key takeaways

  • ✓First letter = signal type (Analog/Binary/Multi-state); second letter = direction (Input/Output) or Value for virtual points.
  • ✓AI & BI read from the field; AO & BO write to it.
  • ✓MI & MO carry more than two states — use them wherever a point is genuinely stepped, not just on/off.
  • ✓BV, AV & MV are internal-only values used in logic, scheduling and calculations — there's no field wiring to trace for any of them.
