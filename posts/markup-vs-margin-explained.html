Markup vs Margin Explained

Ask for "30% on top" and you'll get one price. Ask for "a 30% margin" and you'll get a different, higher one — same two numbers, cost and price, just divided by a different one.

Same profit, different denominator

Markup and margin both describe the gap between what something costs and what it sells for — the disagreement is what that gap gets compared against. Markup measures profit as a percentage of cost: how much you added on top of what you paid. Margin measures the same profit as a percentage of price: what share of the final sale is actual profit. They use the same numerator and a different denominator, and that's the entire source of the confusion.

Say something costs $70 and sells for $100. The profit is $30 either way. As markup, that's $30 on a $70 cost — about 43%. As margin, it's $30 on a $100 price — exactly 30%. Same transaction, two correct percentages, because "43%" and "30%" are answering "percentage of what?" differently.

The formulas

Markup  =  (Price − Cost)  /  Cost  ×  100
Profit measured against what you paid — "how much did I add on top of cost."
Margin  =  (Price − Cost)  /  Price  ×  100
The same profit measured against what the customer paid — "what share of the sale is profit."
Price  =  Cost  ×  (1 + Markup)    |    Price  =  Cost  /  (1 − Margin)
Two different ways to get from cost to price, depending on which one was actually specified — they do not give the same price for the same target percentage.

Where it goes wrong

The costly mix-up happens when someone means margin but calculates markup, or the reverse. Told to "price this at a 30% margin," multiplying cost by 1.30 gives a 30% markup instead — which is only a 23% margin, not 30%. On a single job that's a modest shortfall; across a whole quoting process applied the same way every time, it's a consistent, silent underpricing of every job that goes out the door.

Wanted: 30% marginCorrect price = Cost / (1 − 0.30) = Cost × 1.4286 — a $70 cost prices at $100.
Mistakenly priced as 30% markupPrice = Cost × 1.30 = $91 — which is only a 23% margin, not the 30% that was asked for.

Why the gap widens at higher percentages

At small percentages, markup and margin sit close together — a 5% markup and roughly a 4.8% margin are hardly worth distinguishing. The gap opens up as the percentage grows, because margin's denominator (price) is always larger than markup's denominator (cost) once there's any profit at all, and that difference compounds. By 50% markup, the margin is only 33%; by 100% markup ("doubling" the cost), the margin is exactly 50%, not 100%. High-margin, high-markup businesses feel this gap the most — which is exactly where the mix-up does the most financial damage.

Common terms, decoded

TermMeaning
CostWhat it actually costs to produce or acquire the item or service being sold
PriceWhat the customer actually pays
MarkupProfit expressed as a percentage of cost — how much is added on top
MarginProfit expressed as a percentage of price — what share of the sale is profit
Gross marginThe same margin concept applied at the whole-business level, across total revenue and cost of goods sold

Key takeaways

  • ✓Markup divides profit by cost; margin divides the same profit by price — different denominators, different percentages for the same dollars.
  • ✓A given markup is always a smaller number than the equivalent margin, because price is always larger than cost once there's any profit.
  • ✓Calculating markup when margin was asked for silently underprices the job — the higher the target percentage, the bigger the shortfall.
  • ✓Price = Cost × (1 + Markup) and Price = Cost / (1 − Margin) are the two conversion routes — use the one that matches what was actually specified.
  • ✓Before pricing anything, confirm explicitly which one is meant — "percent profit" alone doesn't say.
