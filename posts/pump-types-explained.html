Pump Types Explained: Centrifugal to PD

Every pump moves fluid from one point to another, but it does that in one of two fundamentally different ways — spinning it fast enough to fling it forward, or physically trapping and displacing a fixed volume — and that split is what actually defines every type below.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Mechanical / Pumps
01
Every pump falls into one of two families — centrifugal (kinetic, spins the fluid) or positive displacement (traps and moves a fixed volume each cycle) — and that split, not the pump's size, decides how it actually behaves.
02
A centrifugal pump's flow drops as system pressure rises; a positive displacement pump's flow stays nearly constant regardless of pressure — which is exactly why the two need completely different overpressure protection.
03
Dead-heading (blocking the outlet) overheats a centrifugal pump but can burst a positive displacement pump or its piping — PD pumps need a relief valve on the discharge line for this reason alone.
04
Viscosity is the fastest way to rule pumps in or out — centrifugal efficiency collapses on thick fluids, while most positive displacement types handle high viscosity comfortably.

What a pump actually does

A pump adds mechanical energy to a fluid so it can move against friction, elevation, and pressure in a system. How it adds that energy splits every pump into one of two families. A centrifugal pump uses a spinning impeller to accelerate fluid outward, converting that velocity into pressure as the fluid slows down in the casing. A positive displacement (PD) pump instead traps a fixed volume of fluid in a chamber and physically pushes it out the discharge side, cycle after cycle. Almost every practical difference between pump types — how flow responds to pressure, how they handle viscosity, and what protects them from damage — traces back to that one split.

Types of pumps

Different mechanisms trade off flow-vs-pressure behavior, viscosity tolerance, self-priming ability, and how precisely they can meter flow — no single type wins across every duty.

Centrifugal Pump
Kinetic, rotating impeller
An impeller spins fluid outward, and the casing converts that velocity into pressure. High flow at moderate pressure, simple and cheap, but flow falls off sharply as system head rises — the default for water and low-viscosity duties.
Gear Pump
Rotary positive displacement
Two meshing gears trap fluid in the spaces between their teeth and carry it around the casing to the outlet. Smooth, near-constant flow, good for viscous fluids like oils — common in lubrication and hydraulic systems.
Screw Pump
Rotary positive displacement
One or more helical screws rotate inside a close-fitting casing, moving fluid axially in a smooth, continuous, low-pulsation flow. Handles high viscosity and some entrained gas well — used in fuel oil and viscous chemical transfer.
Vane Pump
Rotary positive displacement
Sliding vanes in a rotor extend against an eccentric casing wall, trapping and carrying fluid in the pockets between them. Good self-priming ability and steady flow — common in fuel transfer and some hydraulic applications.
Diaphragm Pump
Reciprocating positive displacement
A flexing diaphragm changes chamber volume, drawing fluid in through one check valve and pushing it out through another. No dynamic seal in contact with the process fluid — ideal for chemical dosing and leak-sensitive service.
Piston / Plunger Pump
Reciprocating positive displacement
A piston or plunger moves back and forth in a cylinder, displacing a precise volume each stroke through check valves. Capable of very high pressures — used in high-pressure cleaning, hydraulic power units, and dosing.
Peristaltic Pump
Reciprocating-type positive displacement
Rollers compress a flexible tube against a track, pushing a trapped slug of fluid ahead of the compression point. Fluid never touches the pump itself — ideal for hygienic, abrasive, or highly corrosive fluids in precise metering duty.
Progressive Cavity Pump
Rotary positive displacement
A helical rotor turns inside a matching elastomeric stator, forming sealed cavities that progress fluid steadily from inlet to outlet. Handles viscous, abrasive, and solids-laden fluids — a common choice for sludge and slurry transfer.

Main parts of a centrifugal pump

  1. 1
    Casing / voluteHouses the impeller and converts the fluid's velocity into pressure as it slows down.
  2. 2
    ImpellerThe rotating component whose vanes accelerate fluid outward from the center of rotation.
  3. 3
    ShaftTransmits torque from the driver (motor) to the impeller.
  4. 4
    Mechanical seal / gland packingSeals the point where the rotating shaft passes through the stationary casing, preventing leakage.
  5. 5
    BearingsSupport the shaft and absorb radial and axial loads from the impeller.
  6. 6
    Wear ringsClose-clearance rings that limit internal recirculation between the discharge and suction sides.
  7. 7
    Suction & discharge nozzlesThe inlet and outlet connection points sized for the pump's rated flow.
  8. 8
    CouplingConnects the pump shaft to the driver shaft, accommodating minor misalignment.

Flow control methods

1Throttling valve — restricts a centrifugal pump's discharge, moving its operating point back along the curve; never used to throttle a PD pump.
2VFD speed control — varies motor speed to change flow, following the pump's affinity laws; works on both pump families.
3Bypass / recirculation line — routes excess flow back to suction, protecting a centrifugal pump from operating too close to shutoff.
4Relief valve — a mandatory safeguard on PD pump discharge lines, opening to protect the pump and piping if the outlet is blocked.
5On/off duty cycling — simple start-stop control, common on smaller pumps sized with enough margin for the duty.

Selection checklist

  • 1Required flow rate and total head / discharge pressure
  • 2Fluid viscosity, abrasiveness, and any entrained solids or gas
  • 3Net positive suction head (NPSH) available vs required
  • 4Need for precise, repeatable metering vs simple bulk transfer
  • 5Materials of construction compatible with the process fluid
  • 6Drive type and speed control needs (fixed speed vs VFD)
  • 7Overpressure protection appropriate to the pump family

Common failure & inspection points

  • ✓Cavitation from insufficient NPSH available, pitting the impeller and causing noise and vibration
  • ✓Mechanical seal leakage — often the first visible sign of pump trouble on a centrifugal pump
  • ✓Bearing wear from misalignment, vibration, or inadequate lubrication
  • ✓Wear ring clearance opening up over time, dropping efficiency through internal recirculation
  • ✓A centrifugal pump run too close to shutoff head, overheating from lack of flow through the casing
  • ✓A blocked or isolated PD pump discharge with no working relief valve — a real overpressure hazard
  • ✓Check valve wear on reciprocating PD pumps, showing up as reduced volumetric efficiency
The single most common pump-selection mistake is picking a centrifugal pump for a metering or high-viscosity duty it was never built for — and the single most common PD pump mistake is skipping the discharge relief valve because "it'll never actually get blocked."

Common applications

HVAC chilled & hot water circulation Water & wastewater treatment Chemical injection & dosing Oil, fuel & lubricant transfer Fire protection systems Slurry & sludge transport Food & pharmaceutical hygienic transfer Hydraulic power units

Key takeaways

  • ✓Every pump is either centrifugal (kinetic) or positive displacement — that split, not size or brand, drives most of its behavior.
  • ✓Centrifugal flow falls as pressure rises; PD flow stays nearly constant — which is why only PD pumps need a mandatory relief valve.
  • ✓High viscosity and precise metering both point toward positive displacement, not centrifugal.
  • ✓Mechanical seal leakage and cavitation are usually the first signs of centrifugal pump trouble — check those before assuming a bigger failure.
