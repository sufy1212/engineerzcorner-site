Air Changes per Hour: What ACH Actually Tells You (and Doesn't)

ACH is one of the most quoted numbers in ventilation design — and one of the most misread. It tells you how much air moved through a room. It says nothing about whether that air actually reached the people in it.

What ACH actually measures

Air changes per hour is the number of times the ventilation system would replace the entire volume of air in a room, in one hour, if that air mixed perfectly and left in a single, orderly batch. It's a bulk-average number — a ratio of how much air moved to how much space it had to move through, nothing more.

That's a useful shorthand for comparing ventilation intensity across very different rooms, which is why it shows up constantly in codes and design guides. But "perfectly mixed" is doing a lot of work in that definition, and real rooms rarely cooperate.

The formula

ACH = supply (or extract) airflow ÷ room volume

A 72 m³ room fed by 360 m³/h of supply air is getting 5 ACH. Double the airflow, or halve the room, and it doubles to 10 ACH — the two variables trade off directly.

Where the number breaks down

Short-circuiting

If supply and return are close together, fresh air can travel straight from diffuser to grille without ever sweeping the occupied zone — full nominal ACH, minimal real benefit.

Dead zones

Corners, behind furniture, or poorly diffused areas can sit far below the room average even when the bulk number looks healthy. ACH hides this because it's a single averaged figure.

This is why ACH is treated as a sizing and code-compliance metric, not a guarantee of air quality at any specific point in the room. Diffuser selection, throw pattern, and return placement decide whether the nominal ACH actually reaches people — the volume/airflow ratio alone can't capture that.

Key takeaways

  • ✓ACH is airflow divided by volume — check units match before comparing numbers across projects.
  • ✓Higher-risk or higher-occupancy spaces get higher ACH targets by code — treat these as minimums, not design goals.
  • ✓A room can meet its ACH target on paper and still ventilate poorly if diffusers and returns are laid out badly.
  • ✓ACH doesn't account for outdoor-air fraction — a system can recirculate most of that airflow and still post a high ACH number.
