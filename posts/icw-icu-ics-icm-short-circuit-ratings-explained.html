Icw, Icu, Ics & Icm Explained: Four kA Ratings, Four Different Jobs

A breaker nameplate can carry four separate short-circuit numbers, all in kA, all describing the same fault current — but each one certifies a different thing the equipment has to survive. Mixing them up is an easy way to under- or over-spec a breaker.

Same fault, four different questions

A short circuit puts several different kinds of stress on switchgear at once: sustained thermal and mechanical stress while it lasts, an interruption event when a breaker opens to clear it, and a violent mechanical/electromagnetic shock at the instant contacts close onto a live fault. Icw, Icu, Ics and Icm are four separate certified numbers — all expressed in kA — because each one answers a different one of those questions. Reading a datasheet correctly means knowing which question each figure is actually answering, not just picking the biggest number and assuming it covers everything.

Icw — rated short-time withstand current

Can the busbar system survive the fault current for a set duration, without a breaker interrupting it at all?

Icw applies to busbars and switchgear assemblies rather than the breaker's interrupting mechanism — it's the current the conductor and support structure can carry for a specified time (commonly 1 second) purely from thermal and mechanical withstand, before anything trips. This matters because in a selective/discriminative protection scheme, an upstream breaker is often deliberately delayed so a downstream breaker clears a fault first — during that delay, the busbar itself has to survive the full fault current on its own. A typical rating is written as a current and a duration together, e.g. 50 kA for 1 second.

Icu — ultimate short-circuit breaking capacity

The maximum fault current the breaker can interrupt under specified test conditions

Icu is the highest short-circuit current the breaker is certified to interrupt at all, under the standard test sequence defined for its type. It's the number most people reach for first when checking "is this breaker big enough" against a fault level. The test standard doesn't require the breaker to remain fully serviceable afterward, though — after clearing a fault at or near Icu, the breaker may need inspection, and possibly replacement, before being put back into continuous service.

Ics — service short-circuit breaking capacity

The fault current the breaker can interrupt and still remain suitable for continuous service afterward

Ics is a stricter, more practically useful number than Icu for most applications: it's the fault current the breaker can clear while still being fit to carry on in normal service without inspection or replacement. Manufacturers typically publish it as a percentage of Icu — commonly 50%, 75% or 100% depending on the breaker's design and category — so a breaker rated Icu = 50 kA might carry an Ics of 37.5 kA at the 75% level. For a breaker that's expected to keep working after a real fault rather than get swapped out, Ics is the figure that actually reflects that expectation.

Icm — rated short-circuit making capacity

The ability to close onto an existing fault and survive the peak current that follows

Icm is different in kind from the other three: it's not about interrupting a fault, it's about closing onto one. If a breaker closes onto a circuit where a fault is already present, the very first current peak — the asymmetrical making current — can be significantly higher than the steady-state RMS fault current, and it hits the contacts and mechanism as a sudden mechanical and electromagnetic shock. Icm certifies the breaker can survive that peak while closing, which is why it's tied to dynamic mechanical stress rather than thermal withstand, and is usually expressed as the peak value rather than an RMS current.

How the four relate — worked example

All four numbers here trace back to the same 50 kA fault level, but they aren't the same number wearing different labels — Ics is deliberately lower than Icu (a service-friendly subset of it), and Icm is a peak value roughly 2× higher, reflecting the asymmetrical making current rather than the steady RMS fault current.

Selecting the right value for the right component

  • 1Start from a short-circuit study — none of these four numbers means anything without a calculated fault level to compare it against.
  • 2Check Icw against the busbar/switchgear assembly rating, factoring in the actual protection clearing time upstream — not just the breaker's own interrupting rating.
  • 3Use Icu as the absolute ceiling check, but design around Ics if the breaker needs to stay in service without inspection after a real fault.
  • 4Don't overlook Icm on incomers or anywhere a breaker might realistically close onto an existing fault — it's a mechanical/making check, not an interrupting one, and a breaker can fail here even with an adequate Icu.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Icw, Icu, Ics and Icm all describe the same fault current in kA, but each certifies a different survival requirement.
  • ✓Icw is a busbar/assembly withstand rating over time; Icu, Ics and Icm are all breaker-specific.
  • ✓Ics (often 50–100% of Icu) is the more relevant number when a breaker must remain serviceable after a fault, not just capable of clearing one.
  • ✓Icm is a peak making-current rating tied to mechanical closing stress, separate from any of the interrupting ratings.
