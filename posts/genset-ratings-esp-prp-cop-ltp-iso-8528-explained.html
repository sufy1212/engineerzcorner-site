Genset Ratings: ESP, PRP, COP, LTP

The same engine and alternator carry four different kW ratings on one rating card. Which one you may use depends on how many hours a year it runs and how hard it is loaded, so the same 320 kW load can need a 320 kW set or a 460 kW one.

Engineerz CorneRReference · ISO 8528-1
01
ISO 8528-1 defines four ratings: ESP (emergency standby), PRP (prime), LTP (limited-time) and COP (continuous). A kW figure means nothing until you know which one it is.
02
Ratings are tied to annual running hours and to the average load factor over 24 hours, typically no more than 70% for ESP and PRP.
03
A site that runs the generator every day needs a prime-rated set. Putting a standby-rated set on a prime duty is the classic cause of premature failure.
04
Rating cards are stated at 0.8 power factor and standard reference conditions. Altitude, temperature, motor starting and non-linear loads all change the set you need.

The four ISO 8528-1 ratings

ISO 8528-1 sets the definitions that generator set manufacturers use on their rating cards. The numbers differ slightly between vendors in the details (overload allowance, annual hour caps), so treat the table as the core definition and read the manufacturer's own notes for the product you are buying.

RatingNameAnnual running timeLoad patternAverage load factor
ESPEmergency standby powerUp to about 200 hVariable, for the duration of a mains outage≤ 70% of ESP over 24 h; no overload
PRPPrime running powerUnlimitedVariable≤ 70% of PRP over 24 h; 10% overload for limited periods
LTPLimited-time running powerUp to about 500 hUp to full load, for example peak shaving or parallel with the gridUp to 100%; no overload
COPContinuous operating powerUnlimitedConstantUp to 100%; no overload
Where vendors differ: the PRP overload is commonly stated as 10% for 1 hour in every 12 hours and capped at 25 hours a year, but some cards state it differently. LTP annual hours are quoted as 500 h by some manufacturers and 1000 h by others, and ESP caps (typical against maximum hours) vary. Always use the figures on the card for the exact model.

Average load factor

The 70% figure is not a limit on how much load you can connect. It is a limit on the average over a 24-hour period. Average load factor is the energy delivered over the period divided by the rated power times the period, so a prime set that sees 400 kW for 12 hours and 100 kW for 12 hours averages 250 kW. Against a 500 kW prime rating that is a 50% load factor, which is inside the 70% limit even though the peak is 80%.

That is also why a standby set cannot simply be run as a prime set: ESP assumes few hours and no overload, so its engine and alternator are not specified for daily running.

Same load, different rating

A site has a steady running demand of 320 kW. What kW rating does each duty require?

DutyRuleMinimum rating
Emergency standby (ESP)Peak demand ≤ ESP rating; average over 24 h within 70% of ESP≥ 320 kW. A short outage on a 24 h basis gives an average far below 70%
Prime (PRP), 24 h/day at 320 kWAverage 320 kW ≤ 0.70 × PRPPRP ≥ 320 / 0.70 = 457 kW
Continuous (COP), constant320 kW ≤ COPCOP ≥ 320 kW

At 0.8 power factor, 457 kW is about 571 kVA, so a 500 kW prime set (625 kVA) is the next common size. These are rating-type checks only. The result then has to pass the motor starting, voltage dip and non-linear load checks below.

Selection workflow

  • ✓Duty and hours. Choose the rating type first: standby, prime, peak-shaving or continuous. It sets the average-load limit.
  • ✓Load schedule. Running kW with a demand factor, in kW and kVA at the real power factor. Rating cards assume 0.8, so low-power-factor loads can limit the alternator before the engine.
  • ✓Largest step and motor starting. The set must start the biggest motor with the other load running and stay inside the allowed voltage and frequency dip. See sizing for motor starting and the sizing calculator.
  • ✓Non-linear loads. UPS rectifiers, VFDs and LED drivers distort the current. Alternators for these loads are often oversized or specified with a lower subtransient reactance to hold voltage distortion down; ask the manufacturer for the allowed percentage.
  • ✓Site derating. Altitude and high ambient temperature reduce engine and alternator output. Apply the manufacturer's derate curve for the model; ratings are stated at reference conditions.
  • ✓Fuel and autonomy. Required run time times fuel consumption at the expected load, plus a reserve.
  • ✓Transfer and control. Transfer scheme, start time and paralleling, covered in ATS, MTS and changeover switches.

Fuel autonomy example

A diesel set commonly uses roughly 0.25 L per kWh of output near three-quarters load; this is an approximation and the manufacturer's fuel curve for the model and load point should replace it. At 320 kW that is about 80 L/h. An 8-hour autonomy needs 640 L, and a 20% reserve takes the day tank or belly tank to about 770 L. Check the figure against the real fuel curve and any regulatory storage limit before ordering.

Mistakes that catch people out

  • ✓Reading a standby kW as a prime kW. The same machine's prime rating is lower.
  • ✓Sizing by kW only. A low power factor or non-linear load can trip the alternator limit while the engine has headroom.
  • ✓Ignoring the average load factor. A set carrying a light load most of the day can also suffer, from wet stacking and carbon build-up; very light loads need load-bank runs.
  • ✓Using nameplate numbers across vendors. Annual hour caps and overload terms differ; compare them on the cards.
  • ✓No derating for site conditions. A set that is right at sea level and 25 °C may be short on a high, hot site.

Key takeaways

  • ✓ESP, PRP, LTP and COP are different ratings for different annual hours and load patterns; quote and compare the same one.
  • ✓ESP and PRP assume an average load of no more than 70% over 24 hours; for a steady 24-hour load, PRP must be at least load / 0.70.
  • ✓Check kW and kVA, motor starting, non-linear load and site derating after choosing the rating type.
  • ✓Fuel autonomy comes from the manufacturer's consumption curve at the real load, not a rule of thumb.
  • ✓Confirm all limits against the rating card and ISO 8528-1 edition for the exact set.
