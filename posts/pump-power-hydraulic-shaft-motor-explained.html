Pump Power Explained: From Hydraulic Power to Shaft Power to Motor Size

A pump is specified by flow and head, but the motor is sized in kilowatts. Here are the three steps from the liquid to the drive, with worked numbers, and the factors that move the answer.

Pumps are specified by flow and head, but the motor that drives them is specified in kilowatts. Getting from one to the other takes three steps, one for each place where energy is lost: the fluid, the pump and the drive. Skip a step and the motor is either too small to hold the duty point or far too big to run efficiently.

With density ρ in kg/m³, flow Q in m³/s and head H in metres, the hydraulic power in kW is ρ × 9.81 × Q × H ÷ 1000. It is the useful work done on the liquid. The shaft (brake) power is what the pump takes from its motor, and the motor input is what is drawn from the supply.

Worked example

A pump delivers 50 m³/h of water, 13.9 L/s, against a total dynamic head of 30 m. The pump efficiency at this duty point is 70% and the motor and drive efficiency is 92%.

  • Hydraulic power: 1000 × 9.81 × (50 ÷ 3600) × 30 ÷ 1000 = 4.09 kW.
  • Shaft power: 4.09 ÷ 0.70 = 5.84 kW.
  • Motor input power: 5.84 ÷ 0.92 = 6.35 kW.

The pump power and NPSH calculator gives the same figures and then rounds the motor input up to the next standard size, here 7.5 kW. Many specifications size the motor on the shaft power plus a margin, so the rounding in the tool is deliberately on the cautious side. Use the margin and the standard that apply to your project. A US-unit cross-check gives the same shaft power: 220 gpm at 98.4 ft with 70% efficiency is 220 × 98.4 ÷ (3,960 × 0.70) = 7.8 hp, which is 5.8 kW.

What changes the answer

  • Efficiency at the duty point. Read it from the pump curve at the real flow and head, not from the best-efficiency figure in a brochure. A pump running far from its best point can lose many percentage points. See the centrifugal pump curve.
  • Fluid density. Power scales directly with density. Seawater at about 1,030 kg/m³ raises the 4.09 kW hydraulic figure to about 4.21 kW, and a slurry or a heavy brine more. Viscous fluids also lower pump efficiency.
  • Speed. Shaft power falls with the cube of speed, so cutting the speed by 10% cuts the shaft power to 0.9³ = 73% of its value, 4.26 kW in this example. That is the basis of the savings in VFD speed scaling and the affinity laws, valid for a system with little static head.
  • Operating hours. The 6.35 kW input over 6,000 hours a year uses about 38,100 kWh. Efficiency gains are worth chasing for pumps that run long hours.

Checks that sit beside the power figure

The power calculation says nothing about whether the pump will cavitate. Check the suction side with NPSH available and keep a real margin, as in why passing on paper can still cavitate. Also check that the motor can start the pump, and that the head used in the calculation includes the friction losses from pipe head loss.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Use the pump efficiency read from the curve at the real duty point, not the best-efficiency figure.
  • ✓Power scales with fluid density, so heavy fluids need more power for the same flow and head.
  • ✓Size the motor on shaft power plus the margin your standard requires, then round to a standard size.
  • ✓Check NPSH margin and motor starting separately from the power figure.
