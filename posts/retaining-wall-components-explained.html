Retaining Wall Components Explained

A retaining wall isn't just a slab of concrete standing on end — it's a footing shaped to use the retained soil's own weight against it, with a drainage system quietly doing as much work as the concrete. Here's what every part is actually for.

A footing shaped to fight itself

Look at a cantilever retaining wall in cross-section and the footing is never symmetric under the stem — it's deliberately offset, with more footing on the retained side than the free side. That longer side is the heel, and it sits directly under the backfill. The soil resting on top of the heel adds weight exactly where it's needed to resist the wall tipping forward, which is why cantilever walls can be built noticeably thinner than gravity walls for the same retained height.

The shorter side, the toe, does a different job: it widens the footprint on the free side, which helps spread bearing pressure and gives the wall a bit more resistance to sliding. Between the two, the footing is really doing two separate jobs from two different pieces of geometry — not one slab doing one thing.

1Stem

  • The vertical wall element that directly holds back the retained soil and resists lateral earth pressure by bending
  • Acts as a cantilevered beam fixed at the footing — bending moment and reinforcement demand are both highest at its base
  • Often tapered thicker at the base than the top, since that's where bending stress is greatest

2Footing: Toe & Heel

  • Toe (short side, free-standing side) widens the base to help resist sliding and spread bearing pressure
  • Heel (long side, under the backfill) carries the weight of soil sitting on top of it — that weight is what resists overturning
  • Both must be checked separately as cantilevered slabs off the stem, each with its own bending and shear demand

3Shear Key

  • A short vertical projection cast into the underside of the footing, extending down into the founding soil
  • Adds passive soil resistance in front of it, directly increasing the wall's resistance to sliding
  • Usually only added once the sliding check fails on friction alone — it's a targeted fix, not a default detail

4Drainage: Gravel Zone & Weep Holes

  • Free-draining gravel (or a prefabricated drainage composite) placed directly behind the stem gives water somewhere to go besides staying trapped against the wall
  • Weep holes through the stem let that water escape instead of building up hydrostatic pressure behind it
  • Filter fabric between the gravel and native backfill keeps fines from washing in and clogging the drainage path over time

5Reinforcement

  • Vertical steel in the stem resists bending from lateral earth pressure, placed on the tension (retained) face
  • Horizontal steel in the heel resists the cantilever bending from backfill weight bearing down on it
  • Horizontal steel in the toe resists upward bearing pressure pushing the toe slab upward like a cantilever in reverse

6Expansion & Contraction Joints

  • Vertical joints spaced along a long wall's length to control where shrinkage and thermal cracking occurs
  • Without them, a continuous wall tends to crack randomly instead of at a detail designed to accommodate movement
  • Waterstops are often built into these joints on walls where the retained side needs to stay watertight

How the load path actually runs

1Retained soil pushes laterally on the stem as earth pressure, increasing with depth.
2The stem, acting as a cantilever fixed at its base, bends and transfers that lateral load down into the footing.
3The heel's share of backfill weight, plus the wall's self-weight, resists the footing wanting to overturn about the toe.
4Friction under the footing — plus passive resistance from the shear key, if present — resists the whole wall wanting to slide horizontally.
5Everything ultimately bears down on the founding soil, which is why bearing capacity is checked as its own independent limit.

Why drainage gets its own detail, not an afterthought

Lateral earth pressure calculations almost always assume a drained backfill. If water is allowed to build up behind the stem instead, hydrostatic pressure adds directly on top of the soil pressure the wall was designed for — and that combined load can be dramatically higher than what the stem and footing were sized to resist. This is why the drainage zone, weep holes, and filter fabric aren't finishing touches; they're what keeps the wall's actual loading close to its design assumption.

Why this geometry is standard

Material Efficiency
Self-Weight Working For It
Independently Checkable Parts

The toe-and-heel footing shape exists because it lets a cantilever wall recruit the retained soil's own weight to help hold itself up, instead of relying purely on the wall's own mass the way a gravity wall does — which is why cantilever walls use noticeably less concrete for the same retained height. It also means each component — stem, toe, heel, key — can be checked and detailed somewhat independently, which is exactly what the overturning, sliding, and bearing checks each end up examining from a different angle.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The footing is asymmetric on purpose — the heel uses backfill weight to resist overturning; the toe widens the base for bearing and sliding.
  • ✓The stem is a cantilever fixed at the footing, with peak bending — and peak reinforcement demand — at its base.
  • ✓Drainage isn't optional detailing: it's what keeps actual loading close to the drained-backfill assumption the stem was designed for.
  • ✓A shear key is a targeted fix for sliding, added when friction alone under the footing isn't enough.
  • ✓Reinforcement runs in more than one direction — vertical in the stem, horizontal in both the toe and the heel.
