Heat Recovery Ventilation: HRV, ERV and What Effectiveness Really Means

Exhausting conditioned air while heating fresh outdoor air wastes energy. Here is how HRVs and ERVs recover it, how effectiveness is defined and calculated, and the limits that keep real savings below the headline figure.

Ventilation brings in fresh air, and in winter or summer that air arrives at the wrong temperature. A building that exhausts 21 °C air through a vent while heating 0 °C air to replace it throws away a lot of energy. A heat recovery ventilator lets the outgoing air pre-condition the incoming air, so the heating or cooling coil has much less to do.

HRV or ERV?

HRV: heat recovery ventilator

Transfers sensible heat only, which is the temperature. Moisture stays in its own airstream, so the latent effectiveness is zero.

ERV: energy recovery ventilator

Transfers both heat and moisture. In summer it dries the incoming humid air, and in winter it returns moisture to dry incoming air.

Every ERV is a heat recovery device, but not every heat recovery device is an ERV. The choice often comes down to climate: where cooling and dehumidification dominate, moisture transfer saves a large part of the load.

What effectiveness means

Effectiveness is the actual energy transferred divided by the most that could be transferred, which is the smaller airflow's capacity rate times the largest available temperature, humidity or enthalpy difference. For equal supply and exhaust flows, the sensible effectiveness is simple:

ε = (Tsupply − Toutdoor) ÷ (Texhaust − Toutdoor)

A value of 100% would deliver the incoming air at exactly the exhaust temperature. Latent effectiveness uses humidity ratio in the same way, and total effectiveness uses enthalpy. Ratings from standards such as AHRI 1060 report sensible, latent and total values, plus pressure drop and cross-leakage.

Worked example: winter heating

Outdoor air is at 0 °C and the exhaust air at 21 °C, with a 70% sensible effectiveness and 1 m³/s of airflow (about 1.2 kg/s).

  • Supply air leaving the exchanger: 0 + 0.70 × (21 − 0) = 14.7 °C.
  • Heating needed without recovery: 1.2 × 1.005 × 21 = 25.3 kW.
  • Heating needed with recovery, from 14.7 to 21 °C: 1.2 × 1.005 × 6.3 = 7.6 kW.
  • Heat recovered: 17.7 kW, which is 70% of the load, matching the effectiveness.

Recovery has a price, since the exchanger adds pressure drop and both fans work harder. If the added resistance is 250 Pa on each airstream, at 60% fan efficiency the extra fan power is about 2 × 250 ÷ 0.6 = 830 W. Against 17.7 kW recovered, that is roughly 21 times as much energy saved as spent at these conditions. The ratio is illustrative and falls as the weather mildens, so a yearly analysis is the real test. The sensible heat airflow calculator gives the airflow-to-load relationship, and fan static vs total pressure explains the pressure side.

Worked example: summer enthalpy

An ERV sees outdoor air at 35 °C and 60% RH, about 90 kJ/kg, and indoor exhaust air at 24 °C and 50% RH, about 48 kJ/kg. At a total effectiveness of 65%, the supply air leaves at 90 − 0.65 × (90 − 48) = 62.7 kJ/kg. At 1.2 kg/s, that removes 1.2 × 27.3 = 32.8 kW from the cooling coil load, mostly by drying the air. A sensible-only device with the same temperature effectiveness would leave the moisture load untouched. Use the psychrometric chart for these state points, and see sensible vs latent heat for why the moisture part matters.

Things that limit or complicate recovery

  • Frost. In cold climates the exhaust air can fall below 0 °C as it gives up its heat, and its moisture then freezes on the exchanger and blocks it. Systems prevent this by modulating wheel speed, bypassing outdoor air, preheating the incoming air or running defrost cycles, all of which reduce the recovery.
  • Cross-contamination. Wheels and some plates leak a little exhaust air into the supply. Ratings report this as the exhaust air transfer ratio, so it matters where exhaust air is dirty or hazardous, such as laboratories and kitchens.
  • Mild weather. When the outdoor air is close to the indoor temperature, recovery can add unwanted heat, so units bypass or stop the exchanger.
  • Pressure drop and fans. A higher-effectiveness core usually has a higher pressure drop. Weigh the fan energy against the saving.
  • Codes. Some energy codes require minimum recovery effectiveness for larger systems, for example a minimum sensible effectiveness of 60% or total effectiveness of 50%. Check the one that applies to your project.

To set the airflow in the first place, read air changes per hour. Recovery cuts the load of that ventilation air but does not change how much of it is needed.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Effectiveness is actual transfer over the maximum possible, so 70% recovers 70% of the available difference.
  • ✓Choose an ERV where moisture removal or humidification matters, and an HRV where only sensible heat does.
  • ✓Compare recovered energy with the extra fan energy, using the whole year and not one design point.
  • ✓Check frost protection and cross-contamination ratings for the application.
