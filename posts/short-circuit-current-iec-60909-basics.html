Prospective Short-Circuit Current: Why It Falls the Further You Are From Source

A breaker's interrupting rating is only ever checked against the fault current at its own location — not a single number for the whole switchboard.

Engineerz CorneRReference · Fault Current / IEC 60909
01
Prospective short-circuit current (Ik″) is the current that would flow in the first cycle after a fault, set entirely by the impedance between the source and the fault point.
02
Every transformer, cable and busbar between source and fault adds impedance in series, so Ik″ is highest right at the transformer terminals and falls the further downstream you go.
03
A breaker's interrupting rating (Icu/Ics) only needs to exceed the fault current available at that breaker's own location — not the highest fault level in the whole installation.
04
IEC 60909 applies a voltage factor c to bound the calculation for maximum and minimum fault conditions, since the exact pre-fault voltage is never precisely known.

What the number actually represents

Prospective short-circuit current is the current that would flow if a bolted fault occurred at a given point in the system, before any protective device operates. It is not what the breaker "sees" during normal operation — it's a hypothetical worst case used to size equipment so it can survive and safely interrupt that fault if one ever happens.

The number is set entirely by the total impedance between the source (utility grid or generator, via the transformer) and the fault point. Lower impedance means higher fault current, because less opposition stands between the source EMF and the short.

The core relationship

Ik″  =  (c × Un)  /  (√3 × Zk)
Un = nominal system voltage · Zk = total impedance from source to fault point · c = voltage factor per IEC 60909 (accounts for the range of realistic pre-fault voltage)

Zk is built up in series as you move down the distribution tree: the utility source impedance, plus the transformer's own impedance (from its rated kVA and %Z), plus the impedance of every cable segment between the transformer and the fault point. Cable impedance itself scales with length and drops with larger cross-section.

Why the number falls as you go downstream

Because Zk only grows as you add more cable in series, Ik″ is always highest immediately at the transformer's LV terminals and decreases at every subsequent board. A distribution board fed by 80m of cable from the main panel sees meaningfully less fault current than the main panel itself, because that 80m of cable added its own impedance to the chain.

Main LV Panel (transformer terminals)Source + transformer onlyHighest fault current in the installation
Sub-distribution board, 40m out+ cable impedance for 40m runNoticeably lower than main panel
Final DB, 120m out via sub-DB+ impedance of every upstream segmentLowest fault current on that branch

Why this matters for breaker selection

Because fault current drops with distance, it's a mistake to size every breaker in an installation to the same worst-case interrupting rating. The Main Breaker at the transformer needs a high Icu because it sees the full fault level. A final DB breaker 150m downstream may need a much lower — and cheaper — interrupting rating, because the cable impedance between it and the source has already reduced the available fault current substantially.

This is also why a short-circuit study is run point-by-point through the distribution tree rather than as a single system-wide figure: each breaker's rating is only meaningful when checked against the fault current actually available at its own busbar.

Key takeaways

  • ✓Fault current is a function of total impedance from source to fault point — more cable in the path means less fault current.
  • ✓The highest Ik″ in any installation is always at the transformer's LV terminals, never further downstream.
  • ✓Check breaker interrupting rating against the fault level at that specific point, not a single system-wide worst case.
  • ✓A full study still needs to check minimum fault current too — it decides whether protection will actually detect and clear a far-end fault fast enough.
