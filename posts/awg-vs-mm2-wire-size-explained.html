AWG vs mm²: Converting Wire Gauge to Metric Cross-Section

AWG numbers run backwards and metric sizes use area in square millimetres. Here is how AWG is defined, the handy doubling rules, a conversion table, and what the conversion cannot tell you.

American Wire Gauge numbers run backwards, and the metric sizes used almost everywhere else use cross-sectional area in square millimetres. Anyone working with imported equipment, North American drawings or solar and battery kits meets both. The conversion is a size match only, and it is worth knowing both how it works and where it stops.

How AWG is defined

AWG follows a geometric series. The diameter of a solid conductor is d = 0.127 × 92^((36 − n) ÷ 39) mm, where n is the gauge number, and the area is π ÷ 4 × d². A larger gauge number means a thinner wire. Sizes above 0 continue as 1/0, 2/0, 3/0 and 4/0, which are the thick power conductors, and the series goes on in kcmil beyond 4/0.

Handy rules of thumb

Every 3 gauge numbers doubles or halves the area. Every 6 numbers doubles or halves the diameter, and every 10 numbers changes the area about tenfold.

Why that works

Each gauge step multiplies the diameter by 92^(1/39), about 1.123. The area ratio per step is its square, 1.261, and three steps give 1.261³ = 2.005.

Take 10 AWG as a worked case: the diameter is 0.127 × 92^(26 ÷ 39) = 2.59 mm, and the area is π ÷ 4 × 2.59² = 5.26 mm². The nearest standard metric size at or above that is 6 mm².

Where the conversion stops

  • Area is not ampacity. The current a conductor can carry depends on insulation, temperature rating, installation method and grouping, and the North American and IEC rating methods differ. Size for current separately, as in cable size and current capacity and cable derating factors.
  • Rounding up is not always right. The next standard size up gives at least the same copper, which is the safe direction for current and voltage drop, but it may not fit the terminal or gland. Check that too.
  • Stranding changes the diameter, not the area. A stranded conductor with the same copper area as a solid one has a larger overall diameter, so use area for the match.
  • Voltage drop and fault withstand depend on the area and the length, so recheck them in the new size.

Key takeaways

  • ✓AWG diameter follows d = 0.127 × 92^((36 − n)/39) mm and the area is π/4 × d².
  • ✓Use the next standard metric size at or above the converted area, then check terminals and glands.
  • ✓Size for current, voltage drop and fault withstand separately in the new size.
  • ✓Stranded and solid conductors of the same area carry the same copper, even though their diameters differ.
