Bolt Torque vs Preload: Why K-Factor Is the Number That Actually Matters

Most of the torque applied to a bolt fights friction, not clamp load — which is exactly why a torque spec is only as good as the nut factor it assumes.

The short-form relationship

T  =  K × F × d
T = applied tightening torque · K = nut factor (dimensionless) · F = target preload (clamp force) · d = nominal bolt diameter

This is the standard field/spec-sheet shortcut for converting a target preload into a torque wrench setting. It's simple by design — and that simplicity hides where almost all of the sensitivity in the result actually lives.

Where the applied torque actually goes

Torque applied at the wrench doesn't convert directly into clamp load. In a typical bolted joint, roughly half the applied torque is consumed by friction under the bolt head or nut face, and another meaningful share by thread friction — leaving only a modest fraction to actually generate axial preload in the bolt shank. That split is exactly what the nut factor K represents: it's not a material property of the bolt at all, but an empirical coefficient capturing how much of the applied torque survives friction losses to become clamp load, for a specific combination of lubrication, coating, surface finish and thread condition.

Why K matters more than bolt grade for the torque number

Bolt grade (and its proof load) sets the ceiling on how much preload the bolt can safely carry — the target F in the formula, often specified around 75% of proof load for a reusable joint. But once that target preload is chosen, the torque figure needed to reach it is dominated by K, not by the bolt's strength grade. A dry, as-received fastener (K around 0.2) and the same fastener lubricated (K noticeably lower) can need meaningfully different torque to reach the identical target preload — and using the dry-condition K-factor on a lubricated joint overshoots preload substantially, potentially past yield, even though every other number in the calculation was correct.

Why this is a field check, not a final answer

This short-form method is a genuinely useful field or spec-sheet check for a quick torque figure, but it carries real scatter — typically ±25% or more on achieved preload, because K is an approximation for a range of real-world friction conditions, not a precisely known constant for a given joint. Critical or fatigue-loaded joints should be verified against the bolt manufacturer's own torque tables, or better, against a direct preload verification method (turn-of-nut, tension indicators, or ultrasonic measurement) rather than torque alone.

Key takeaways

  • ✓The torque-tension formula (T = K × F × d) is a field shortcut, not a precise preload guarantee.
  • ✓Most applied torque fights friction under the head and in the threads — only a modest share becomes actual clamp load.
  • ✓Nut factor K captures that friction behaviour and depends heavily on lubrication and surface condition, not on bolt grade.
  • ✓Critical or fatigue-loaded joints need verification against manufacturer torque tables or direct preload measurement, not this formula alone.
