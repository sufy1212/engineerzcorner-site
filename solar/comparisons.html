Comparisons

Head-to-head equipment and system choices — side by side, so the trade-off is visible at a glance
Eight common solar PV equipment and system decisions, laid out as direct comparisons rather than separate articles — same criteria, side by side, with a plain-language verdict on when each option actually wins. Jump straight to the one you need.

1

Monocrystalline vs Polycrystalline vs Thin-Film Cells

The three dominant PV cell technologies trade efficiency, cost, and appearance against each other differently.
CriteriaMonocrystallinePolycrystallineThin-Film
Typical efficiencyHighest, roughly 19-22%Moderate, roughly 15-17%Lowest, roughly 10-13%
Space required for a given outputLeastMore than mono for the same outputMost — needs the largest area
Relative costHighest per wattModerate, generally cheaper than monoLowest per watt, but more area needed offsets some savings
Temperature performanceGood, degrades less in high heatSlightly more heat-sensitive than monoOften the best high-temperature performance
Typical applicationSpace-constrained residential/commercial roofsBudget-conscious installations with adequate spaceLarge ground-mount or unconventional/flexible surfaces
Monocrystalline is the practical default wherever roof space is limited and the higher cost per watt is worth maximizing output from a fixed area — the large majority of residential installations. Polycrystalline remains a reasonable lower-cost choice where space isn't as constrained. Thin-film's lower efficiency is rarely competitive for rooftop use, but its low cost per watt, lighter weight, and better heat tolerance keep it relevant for large ground-mount arrays or specialized flexible/lightweight applications where mono/poly panels don't fit.
2

String vs Micro vs Power Optimizer Inverters

Where the DC-to-AC conversion (or DC optimization) happens changes how the system handles shading and monitoring.
CriteriaString InverterMicroinverterPower Optimizer
Shading tolerancePoor — one shaded module drags down the whole stringExcellent — each module operates independentlyGood — each module's DC output is optimized independently
Monitoring granularityString or array levelPer-modulePer-module
Relative upfront costLowestHighest — one unit per moduleModerate — optimizer per module plus one string inverter
Where AC/DC conversion happensCentrally, at one inverterAt each module, on the roofCentrally — optimizers only condition DC, don't invert
Typical applicationSimple, unshaded roofs, cost-sensitive projectsComplex roofs with partial shading or multiple orientationsShaded/complex roofs wanting per-module data at lower cost than micros
A string inverter remains the simplest, lowest-cost choice for a straightforward, unshaded roof with a single orientation. Microinverters earn their higher cost specifically where shading, multiple roof orientations, or per-module monitoring meaningfully improve production or diagnostics — since one shaded module no longer drags down the rest of the string. Power optimizers land in between, delivering much of the per-module benefit of micros at a lower incremental cost by keeping the actual DC-to-AC inversion centralized in one string inverter.
3

Lead-Acid vs Lithium (LiFePO4) Batteries

The two dominant off-grid/hybrid storage chemistries differ enormously in usable capacity and lifespan.
CriteriaLead-Acid (Flooded/AGM)Lithium (LiFePO4)
Usable depth of dischargeTypically 50%, to protect cycle lifeTypically 80-100%
Cycle lifeShorter, roughly 300-1000 cycles depending on DoDMuch longer, often 2000-6000+ cycles
Weight for a given usable capacityHeaviestSignificantly lighter for the same usable energy
Upfront cost per usable kWhLower upfrontHigher upfront, often lower over the system's life
MaintenanceFlooded types need regular watering/equalization; AGM is sealedEffectively maintenance-free
Typical applicationBudget-constrained or legacy off-grid systemsMost new off-grid and hybrid battery installations
Lithium (LiFePO4) has become the default for most new battery installations — its deeper usable discharge, much longer cycle life, and lower weight generally offset the higher upfront cost once compared on a per-usable-kWh, per-cycle basis over the system's life. Lead-acid persists mainly in budget-constrained new installations, legacy systems being maintained rather than replaced, or applications where its lower upfront cost outweighs lithium's lifecycle advantages for a shorter planned system life.
4

Grid-Tied vs Off-Grid vs Hybrid Systems

Whether and how the system connects to the utility grid defines almost everything else about its design.
CriteriaGrid-TiedOff-GridHybrid
Grid connectionAlways connected, no battery requiredNo grid connection at allGrid-connected, with battery backup
Power during a grid outageNone — shuts down for safety (anti-islanding)Unaffected, runs entirely on its own systemContinues from battery, seamlessly or with brief switchover
Relative system costLowest — no battery bank neededHigher — battery bank sized for full autonomyHigher than grid-tied — battery sized for backup, not full autonomy
Sizing philosophySized to offset annual consumptionSized for worst-case autonomy with no grid backupSized to offset consumption, with battery sized for backup duration, not full autonomy
Typical applicationGrid-connected homes/businesses, no backup requirementRemote sites with no practical grid accessGrid-connected sites wanting resilience during outages
Grid-tied is the simplest and lowest-cost option wherever grid power is reliably available and backup during an outage isn't a requirement — the grid effectively acts as unlimited, free storage. Off-grid is a necessity, not a preference, where grid connection genuinely isn't practical or economical to bring to a site. Hybrid systems have grown popular specifically for grid-connected sites that still want resilience — the battery only needs to cover expected outage duration, not the deeper year-round autonomy a true off-grid system requires, which keeps the battery bank smaller and more affordable than a comparable off-grid design.
5

MPPT vs PWM Charge Controllers

Both keep a battery from overcharging, but only one actually extracts the array's full available power.
CriteriaPWMMPPT
How it regulates chargingRapidly switches the array on/off, pulling array voltage down to battery voltageActively tracks the array's maximum power point and converts voltage to match the battery
Efficiency, array vs battery voltage mismatchLower — wastes the difference between array and battery voltageHigher — captures and converts that voltage difference into extra charging current
Array voltage flexibilityArray voltage must closely match battery voltageArray voltage can be significantly higher than battery voltage
Relative costLowerHigher
Typical applicationSmall, simple systems, closely matched array/battery voltageLarger systems, or any array voltage higher than the battery bank
MPPT has become the default for anything beyond a small, simple system — its ability to accept a higher array voltage than the battery bank, then convert that extra voltage into usable charging current, typically recovers 10-30% more energy than PWM from the same array, which usually justifies its higher cost. PWM remains viable specifically for small systems where the array is deliberately sized to closely match battery voltage, and the simpler, lower-cost controller is an acceptable trade for the efficiency it leaves on the table.
6

Series vs Parallel Module Wiring

The same modules wired differently produce very different voltage and current combinations — with real safety and efficiency consequences.
CriteriaSeries WiringParallel Wiring
Combined effectVoltages add, current stays the same as one moduleCurrents add, voltage stays the same as one module
Conductor sizing implicationLower current allows smaller conductors, over longer runsHigher current needs larger conductors for the same distance
Shading/mismatch impactOne shaded/mismatched module limits the whole string's currentA shaded module mainly reduces its own contribution, less impact on others
Voltage limit considerationString voltage must stay within inverter/controller and code limits, especially in cold temperaturesVoltage stays low regardless of module count, current limits become the concern instead
Typical applicationThe default for grid-tied string inverter systems, reaching operating voltage efficientlyLow-voltage off-grid systems, or combining strings after each is already series-wired
Series wiring within a string is the near-universal default for grid-tied systems — it reaches the inverter's required operating voltage efficiently with smaller conductors, at the cost of the whole string's current being limited by its weakest (most shaded) module. Parallel wiring is used both in low-voltage off-grid systems that need higher current at a fixed low voltage, and structurally, in combining multiple already-series-wired strings together at a combiner box — most real arrays actually use a series-parallel combination, not one or the other exclusively.
7

Fixed-Tilt vs Single-Axis Tracking Mounts

Tracking increases annual energy yield, but adds cost, moving parts, and maintenance that fixed mounts don't have.
CriteriaFixed-TiltSingle-Axis Tracking
Annual energy yieldBaselineTypically 15-25% higher for the same module count
Moving parts / maintenanceNoneMotors, bearings and controls require ongoing maintenance
Upfront costLowestHigher — mounting structure and drive system
Site suitabilityWorks on virtually any site, including rooftopsBest suited to ground-mount installations with adequate spacing
Typical applicationMost rooftop and many ground-mount systemsUtility-scale and larger ground-mount installations where the yield gain justifies the added cost/complexity
Fixed-tilt remains the default for the large majority of installations — rooftop systems can't track at all, and for smaller ground-mount systems the simplicity and lower cost usually outweigh the yield gain from tracking. Single-axis tracking earns its added cost and maintenance mainly at utility scale, where the meaningful annual yield increase, spread across a large enough array, offsets the tracker hardware and upkeep cost over the system's life.
8

Roof-Mount vs Ground-Mount Installation

The choice is often dictated by available space and roof condition more than by engineering preference alone.
CriteriaRoof-MountGround-Mount
Land/space useUses existing roof area, no extra land neededRequires dedicated open land area
Installation costGenerally lower — existing structure to mount toHigher — needs its own foundation/racking structure
Access for cleaning/maintenanceMore difficult, often needs specific roof accessEasier ground-level access
Orientation/tilt flexibilityConstrained by existing roof pitch and orientationFully flexible — can be optimized for maximum production
Roof condition dependencyRoof must be structurally sound and not near end-of-lifeNot applicable — independent structure
Typical applicationResidential and commercial buildings with suitable roof spaceSites with available land, aging roofs, or wanting optimal orientation
Roof-mount is the default wherever a structurally sound roof with reasonable orientation is available — it avoids using separate land and is typically the lower-cost installation. Ground-mount becomes the better (or only) choice where the roof can't support the array, its orientation is poor, available land is not a constraint, or the project specifically wants full control over tilt and orientation to maximize production — the added foundation and racking cost is the trade for that flexibility.
