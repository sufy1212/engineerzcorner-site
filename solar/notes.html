Notes on how solar power actually works.

PV Fundamentals

9 notes

A photovoltaic cell is essentially a large-area semiconductor junction, most commonly silicon, engineered so that photons striking it can knock electrons loose and set up a voltage across the cell — the photovoltaic effect.

Individual cells produce a small DC voltage each, so cells are wired together in series and parallel within a panel, and panels are combined into arrays, to reach the voltage and current a system needs.

Monocrystalline cells are cut from a single continuous silicon crystal, giving the highest efficiency and a uniform dark appearance, at the highest manufacturing cost. Polycrystalline cells are cast from multiple silicon fragments melted together — slightly lower efficiency, a distinctive speckled blue appearance, and historically lower cost.

Thin-film technologies deposit a much thinner photovoltaic layer onto a substrate, trading efficiency and space for lower weight, flexibility, and better performance in diffuse light or high heat — useful for specific applications like building-integrated PV, but rarely the choice for standard rooftop or ground-mount arrays where space is at a premium.

A solar panel's current-voltage (I-V) curve plots every possible combination of output current and voltage the panel can deliver under a given light and temperature condition — at one extreme, short-circuit current (maximum current, zero voltage); at the other, open-circuit voltage (maximum voltage, zero current).

Multiplying current by voltage at every point along the curve traces out a power curve with a single peak — the maximum power point — which is the one operating condition where the panel delivers its highest possible output for those conditions, and exactly what MPPT charge controllers and inverters are designed to track.

Standard Test Conditions (STC) — 1000 W/m² irradiance, 25°C cell temperature, a defined light spectrum — are the controlled laboratory conditions under which a panel's nameplate wattage is measured. Real installed panels rarely see all three conditions simultaneously, especially cell temperature, which climbs well above 25°C under full sun.

Nominal Operating Cell Temperature (NOCT) ratings instead describe expected performance under more realistic outdoor conditions — lower irradiance, higher ambient and cell temperature — and consistently show lower output than the STC nameplate figure, which is one reason real-world array output is reasonably expected to run below the sum of each panel's STC rating.

A photovoltaic cell's output voltage (and to a lesser extent its power) falls as cell temperature rises, a relationship quantified by the panel's temperature coefficient, typically around -0.3% to -0.5% per °C above 25°C. A panel running at 55°C cell temperature — entirely normal on a hot, sunny roof — can lose somewhere around 10% of its STC-rated output purely from heat.

This is why solar performance doesn't simply track irradiance alone — a clear, cold day can outperform a hazier but hotter day in terms of panel efficiency, even with lower total sunlight, and it's also why array mounting systems that allow airflow behind the panels for cooling generally outperform tightly roof-mounted arrays with poor ventilation.

Irradiance measures the instantaneous power of sunlight hitting a surface, in watts per square metre (W/m²) — it changes constantly through the day as the sun's angle and cloud cover shift, peaking around solar noon on a clear day.

Insolation (or irradiation) measures the accumulated energy delivered over a period of time, typically expressed as kWh/m²/day — this is the figure behind 'peak sun hours,' a convenient simplification that expresses a whole day's variable irradiance as an equivalent number of hours at the standard 1000 W/m² test condition, making energy yield calculations far more tractable than integrating a real irradiance curve by hand.

Panels connected in series (a 'string') add their voltages together while the current stays the same throughout the string — this is the usual way to reach the higher DC voltages most string inverters and charge controllers expect, without the heavy conductors and losses that higher current would otherwise require.

Multiple strings connected in parallel add their currents together while voltage stays common across them — used to scale total array power beyond what a single string's panel count or inverter input allows. Most real arrays are a combination of both: several panels in series per string, several strings in parallel feeding a common inverter or combiner.

Cells within a panel, and panels within a string, are normally wired in series, meaning the same current has to flow through every cell in the chain. A shaded cell produces far less current than its unshaded neighbors, but the series connection forces the whole string toward that weakest cell's current — disproportionately reducing the entire string's output, not just the shaded portion's expected share.

Bypass diodes, built into virtually all modern panels, mitigate this by routing current around a badly shaded section of a panel rather than forcing the whole panel down to the shaded cells' output — but even with bypass diodes doing their job, partial shading on any part of an array remains one of the most common, and most disproportionate, real-world causes of underperformance.

Photovoltaic cells slowly lose output capacity over their operating life through a mix of mechanisms — light-induced degradation in the first months of operation, gradual material and encapsulant aging, micro-cracking from thermal cycling — collectively totaling perhaps 0.3-0.8% of original output lost per year for modern crystalline silicon panels.

This is why manufacturers publish a performance warranty, not just a product defect warranty — typically guaranteeing something like 90% of rated output at 10 years and 80-85% at 25 years — and why long-term energy yield projections for a system explicitly account for this gradual decline rather than assuming day-one output holds constant for the system's entire service life.

System Design & Sizing

8 notes

A grid-tied system feeds power into the existing grid alongside the site's normal supply, using the grid as an effectively unlimited place to send excess generation and draw extra power when the array underproduces — simpler and cheaper for a given array size, since it doesn't need to store energy for later use itself, but it also typically provides no backup during a grid outage unless specifically designed to.

An off-grid system has no grid connection at all and has to be fully self-sufficient, needing battery storage sized to cover the site through low-generation periods plus a charge controller to manage that charging safely. A hybrid system sits between the two — grid-connected for day-to-day operation, but with battery storage and the additional switching equipment needed to island itself and keep critical loads running through a grid outage.

Solar array sizing properly starts by establishing the actual daily energy requirement — typically in kWh/day, derived from a genuine load assessment or utility bill history — rather than starting from 'how many panels can fit on the roof' and hoping the resulting output happens to match what's needed.

From that daily energy target, dividing by the site's average peak sun hours (itself dependent on geographic location and season) and applying a system loss factor for wiring, temperature, soiling and inverter conversion losses gives the required array capacity in kWp — the same basic method behind any first-pass PV array sizing calculation, however more sophisticated design software eventually refines it.

A solar array's DC output at its absolute peak (near solar noon, on a clear day, at optimal temperature) rarely occurs for more than a small fraction of total operating hours — most of the day, actual DC output sits well below that theoretical peak. Sizing an inverter for that rare peak wastes inverter capacity for the vast majority of the time it's actually running.

A DC:AC ratio (or inverter loading ratio) above 1.0 — commonly 1.1 to 1.3 — intentionally installs more DC array capacity than the inverter's rated AC output, accepting that a small amount of potential energy is 'clipped' (lost) during the rare peak-output periods, in exchange for a smaller, cheaper inverter that still captures nearly all the array's realistic energy yield across a full year.

A panel captures the most energy when sunlight strikes it as close to perpendicular as possible — since the sun's position in the sky changes throughout the day and across seasons, a fixed panel's tilt angle is chosen as a compromise that maximizes total annual (or sometimes seasonal) energy capture rather than chasing the sun's position at every instant, which only a tracking mount can actually do.

In the northern hemisphere, true south-facing orientation at a tilt roughly equal to the site's latitude is a common starting rule of thumb for annual yield, with real designs adjusted for specific roof constraints, seasonal priorities, or utility export/consumption timing — and orientation significantly off from this ideal can still perform well, just with a measurably lower annual yield than a design closer to the theoretical optimum for that specific location.

Balance of system (BOS) is the collective term for every component in a solar installation besides the panels themselves — mounting racking, DC and AC wiring, combiner boxes, disconnects, the inverter, monitoring equipment, and all the protective devices required by the applicable electrical code.

BOS components are frequently underestimated in early budget planning focused mainly on panel cost, but they routinely represent a substantial share of total installed system cost — and just as importantly, BOS design quality (proper conductor sizing, correct grounding, adequate overcurrent protection) is exactly where a technically sound array design can still end up as an unreliable or non-compliant installation if it's treated as an afterthought.

Every string inverter or MPPT charge controller specifies a minimum and maximum DC input voltage window it can accept, along with a maximum current rating — a string's total open-circuit voltage (each panel's Voc added together) has to stay below the inverter's maximum input voltage even at the coldest expected temperature, since panel voltage rises as temperature falls.

Similarly, a string's voltage at its normal operating point has to stay above the inverter's minimum MPPT tracking voltage even at the hottest expected temperature, since voltage falls as temperature rises — string sizing calculations explicitly check both temperature extremes against the inverter's voltage window, rather than only checking against the panels' nameplate STC voltage, precisely because real installed temperatures routinely differ substantially from the 25°C STC condition.

A DC-coupled battery system connects the battery directly to the DC side of the installation, typically through its own charge controller, before the array's power ever passes through a grid-interactive inverter — generally more efficient for storing solar energy directly, since it avoids an extra DC-AC-DC conversion step.

An AC-coupled system instead connects the battery through its own dedicated inverter/charger on the AC side, alongside (or retrofitted onto) an existing grid-tied solar inverter — often simpler to add to an existing solar installation that wasn't originally designed with storage in mind, at the cost of some additional conversion losses compared to a purpose-built DC-coupled design.

When multiple strings are connected in parallel, a fault or short circuit developing within one string can, under certain conditions, be fed not just by that string's own panels but also by reverse current flowing back from the other healthy parallel strings — a scenario a single isolated string can't produce on its own.

String fuses (or breakers) sized appropriately for each individual string's expected current are installed specifically to protect against this reverse-current fault scenario, disconnecting a faulted string before the combined reverse current from the other strings can cause damage — which is why string-level overcurrent protection becomes a design requirement once an array grows beyond a small number of parallel strings, even though a very small array might not strictly need it.

Storage & Batteries

7 notes

A battery's nameplate capacity describes its total energy storage if fully charged and fully discharged — but routinely discharging a battery all the way to zero accelerates degradation and shortens service life for most chemistries, particularly lead-acid.

Depth of discharge (DoD) is the fraction of that nameplate capacity a system is actually designed to use on a normal cycle — commonly around 80% for lithium chemistries and 50% for lead-acid — with the remaining reserved capacity left unused specifically to preserve battery longevity, meaning a battery bank has to be physically sized larger than the raw daily energy requirement alone would suggest, once DoD is properly accounted for.

Cycle life describes how many charge-discharge cycles a battery can complete before its capacity degrades below a defined threshold (commonly 80% of original capacity) — deeper discharge cycles and higher charge/discharge rates both tend to reduce achievable cycle life compared to shallower, gentler cycling.

Calendar life describes degradation that occurs simply from the passage of time, independent of how much the battery is actually cycled — driven by chemical aging processes that continue even in storage, accelerated by high temperature and by being stored at very high or very low states of charge. A battery bank's real service life is set by whichever of the two limits — cycling or calendar aging — is reached first for its specific usage pattern.

C-rate expresses charge or discharge current relative to a battery's capacity — a 1C discharge rate would fully discharge the battery's rated capacity in one hour, a 0.5C rate in two hours, and so on. A battery's maximum safe continuous C-rate is a distinct specification from its energy capacity, and exceeding it risks excess heat generation, accelerated degradation, or in extreme cases, thermal runaway.

This is why battery bank sizing for high-power, short-duration applications (a large motor starting surge, for instance) has to check the required discharge current against the battery's maximum C-rate rating, not just against total available kWh — a battery bank with plenty of total energy capacity can still be inadequate for a load that demands more instantaneous current than its C-rate allows.

A battery management system (BMS) continuously monitors individual cell voltages, pack temperature, and current, and actively protects against conditions that would otherwise damage the pack or create a safety hazard — overcharge, over-discharge, overcurrent, and excessive temperature all trigger protective action from a properly functioning BMS.

Cell balancing is another core BMS function — individual cells within a pack inevitably drift slightly out of balance with each other over repeated cycles, and without active or passive balancing, that drift compounds over time until the weakest cell in the pack effectively limits the usable capacity of the entire string, regardless of how healthy the other cells remain.

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) has become the dominant chemistry for stationary solar storage specifically because of its comparatively strong thermal stability and lower fire risk relative to some other lithium-ion formulations (like NMC, more common in electric vehicles and portable electronics where energy density per unit weight matters more).

LFP typically offers a longer cycle life and tolerates deeper routine discharge better than older lead-acid chemistries, at a higher upfront cost per kWh that has narrowed substantially in recent years — the specific chemistry choice for a stationary system reflects a deliberate trade-off between energy density, cost, cycle life and safety, not simply 'lithium is always better than lead-acid' as a blanket rule.

A pulse-width modulation (PWM) charge controller essentially connects the array directly to the battery bank, pulsing the connection on and off to control charge current — simple and inexpensive, but it forces the array to operate at close to the battery's voltage rather than its own optimal voltage, sacrificing some potential energy harvest, especially when panel and battery voltages don't closely match.

A maximum power point tracking (MPPT) charge controller instead lets the array operate at its own optimal voltage and current, then electronically converts that power to whatever voltage and current the battery needs to charge — extracting meaningfully more usable energy from the same physical array, particularly valuable when array voltage is deliberately higher than battery voltage, which is why MPPT has become the standard choice for anything beyond small, simple systems.

Battery capacity and performance are temperature-dependent for essentially every chemistry — capacity drops in cold conditions as the internal chemical reactions slow down, while high temperatures accelerate degradation and shorten overall service life even though they may temporarily appear to boost available capacity.

This is why battery bank sizing for sites with temperature extremes applies a specific derating factor from the manufacturer's data sheet rather than assuming the battery's rated capacity applies unchanged regardless of installation environment — and why battery enclosures for outdoor or extreme-climate installations often include their own dedicated heating or cooling provisions specifically to keep the battery within its optimal temperature range.

Economics & Maintenance

7 notes

Under net metering, exported solar energy is credited at the same rate the utility charges for imported energy — effectively letting the grid act as a one-for-one energy 'battery,' with the customer only billed for their net consumption over a billing period.

Net billing instead credits exported energy at a different (often lower) rate than the import rate, reflecting the utility's own wholesale cost or avoided-cost calculation rather than the full retail rate — a distinction that materially changes a system's payback economics, since a net-billed system's financial value depends much more heavily on self-consuming generated power directly rather than exporting it, favoring designs and battery storage that maximize on-site use.

Levelized cost of energy (LCOE) divides a solar system's total lifetime cost — installation, financing, operation and maintenance — by its total expected lifetime energy output, producing a single cost-per-kWh figure that can be directly compared against grid electricity rates or other generation sources.

LCOE calculations have to account for panel degradation reducing output over time, financing costs if the system isn't purchased outright, and any ongoing maintenance or eventual inverter replacement costs — a system with a lower upfront cost but faster degradation or a shorter-lived inverter can end up with a higher LCOE than a more expensive but longer-lasting, more efficient design, which is exactly the comparison LCOE is meant to make explicit.

Solar panels are largely passive devices with no moving parts and typically carry warranties extending 25 years or more, degrading only gradually over that period. Inverters, by contrast, are active power electronics running continuously under thermal and electrical stress, and typically carry shorter warranties — often 10 to 15 years — with actual replacement fairly common once or even twice over a system's full operating life.

This mismatch is a standard, expected part of solar system lifecycle economics, not a sign of poor design — long-term cost projections for a system properly budget for at least one inverter replacement over a 25-year panel lifespan, rather than assuming the original inverter will simply last as long as the panels themselves.

Accumulated dust, pollen, bird droppings, and general grime on a panel's surface physically block a portion of incoming sunlight from reaching the photovoltaic cells beneath — soiling losses in dry, dusty climates with infrequent rain can reach several percent of potential output, while sites with regular rainfall to naturally rinse panels see comparatively little soiling impact.

This is why system energy yield estimates for dusty or arid sites explicitly include a soiling loss factor, and why some larger installations budget for periodic panel washing as routine, cost-justified maintenance — the lost energy from accumulated soiling, left unaddressed, can outweigh the cost of a scheduled cleaning program over time, particularly for utility-scale arrays in consistently dry climates.

Modern solar systems typically include monitoring hardware and software reporting production data — often down to individual panel or string level on larger systems — letting an owner or installer compare actual energy output against the expected output for the given weather conditions.

This comparison is what actually catches problems like a failed panel, a degraded string connection, or inverter underperformance early, since a system that looks physically intact can still be silently underproducing for months without monitoring data to flag the discrepancy — treating ongoing performance verification, not just correct initial installation, as part of a solar system's expected operating life.

Unlike a conventional electrical circuit that can be de-energized at a single main breaker, a solar array continues generating DC voltage on its rooftop wiring any time light falls on the panels — even with the main AC disconnect open, firefighters accessing a roof during an emergency could still encounter live DC conductors.

Rapid shutdown requirements, now standard in many electrical codes, mandate that array wiring be brought down to a low, safe voltage within a defined short time (often 30 seconds) of a rapid shutdown switch being activated — typically implemented through panel-level or string-level shutdown devices — specifically to address this firefighter safety hazard that's unique to how solar DC systems are wired compared to conventional AC electrical distribution.

A solar panel reaching the end of its useful service life is mostly glass, aluminium framing, and a smaller quantity of silicon and other materials — the glass and aluminium are readily recyclable through conventional streams, while recovering the silicon and specialty materials economically requires dedicated PV recycling processes still maturing in many markets.

As the first large wave of panels installed during the industry's major growth period approaches 25-30 years of age, end-of-life volume is growing substantially, and several jurisdictions have begun introducing specific extended producer responsibility or recycling mandates for PV modules — treating panel disposal as a planned part of the technology's lifecycle rather than a problem deferred indefinitely.

Installation, Wiring & Safety

7 notes

A rooftop solar array is held to the roof by a racking system, typically attached through the roofing material into the structural framing below with flashed mounting points designed to shed water around the penetration rather than let it collect there. Getting the attachment point wrong — missing the structural framing, using an inadequate flashing detail, or spacing attachment points too far apart for the array's wind and snow load — can turn a small number of necessary penetrations into a chronic leak source or, in a severe wind event, a structural failure of the mounting itself.

Racking also has to be engineered for the specific site's wind and snow loading, not just the array's own weight — wind uplift forces on a tilted array can be substantial, and attachment spacing, racking rail strength, and the number of anchor points are all sized against those calculated loads for the specific location and roof type, not assumed generically the same everywhere.

Conductors carrying PV current are sized for ampacity — enough cross-sectional area to safely carry the circuit's expected current without overheating — the same fundamental principle as any electrical conductor, but PV circuits have a specific ampacity consideration: standard ampacity calculations use a current value derived from the panel's rated short-circuit current with a safety factor applied, rather than simply the panel's normal operating current, since a PV circuit can genuinely sustain something close to its short-circuit current continuously under bright, direct sun.

Voltage drop matters for PV wiring the same way it does elsewhere — excessive resistance in long conductor runs, common on rooftop or ground-mount arrays with meaningful distance between panels and inverter, wastes energy as heat and reduces the actual power delivered — which is why voltage drop is checked as a distinct design step from ampacity, since a conductor sized adequately for ampacity alone can still have unacceptable voltage drop over a long run and need to be upsized for that separate reason.

Equipment grounding connects the non-current-carrying metal parts of a PV system — panel frames, racking, enclosures — to ground, specifically to give any accidental fault current a low-resistance path back to the source, which is what allows overcurrent protection to actually detect and clear that fault quickly rather than leaving those metal parts energized indefinitely. This is a safety function, present in essentially every electrical system, not something unique to solar.

System grounding, by contrast, deliberately grounds one of the current-carrying conductors themselves (in some system topologies) and is a design choice with real implications for how ground faults in the array are detected — many modern inverters use ungrounded (or functionally grounded) DC arrays specifically because it allows more effective ground-fault detection across the whole array rather than just at one grounded reference point. Confusing these two grounding concepts, or treating equipment grounding as sufficient in a system topology that also needs a specific system grounding approach, is a documented source of both installation errors and fault-detection blind spots.

A PV system generates power on the DC side continuously whenever panels are exposed to light — unlike a conventional electrical source, it can't simply be switched off at a single point the way utility power can be interrupted at a breaker, since the panels themselves keep producing voltage regardless of what's happened downstream. This is exactly why PV systems require multiple, specifically located disconnects: a DC disconnect to isolate the array from the inverter, an AC disconnect to isolate the inverter from the building's electrical system, and often a utility-accessible disconnect so utility personnel can isolate the system from the grid during outage response.

Each disconnect serves a distinct purpose tied to who needs to safely isolate which part of the system and why — a firefighter needing to de-energize rooftop conductors during an emergency has a different need than a technician servicing the inverter, which is part of why disconnect requirements and rapid shutdown provisions have become increasingly specific in code requirements as rooftop solar has become more common.

An arc fault is an unintended electrical arc — often caused by a loose connection, damaged conductor insulation, or a connector that's degraded over time — that can generate intense, localized heat capable of igniting nearby combustible material, distinct from the overcurrent condition that a standard breaker or fuse is designed to detect. DC arc faults are a particular concern in PV systems because a DC arc, unlike an AC arc, doesn't naturally self-extinguish at a current zero-crossing the way AC arcs periodically do, so a DC arc fault can sustain and grow if nothing specifically detects and interrupts it.

PV arc-fault circuit interrupters (AFCIs) address this by monitoring the electrical signature of the current for the specific characteristic patterns an arc fault produces, distinguishing it from normal switching noise and other benign electrical events, and interrupting the circuit specifically when that arc-fault signature is detected. This is a PV-specific safety requirement layered on top of standard overcurrent protection, addressing a failure mode that ordinary fuses and breakers, by design, aren't positioned to catch.

A ground fault occurs when a current-carrying conductor unintentionally contacts a grounded metal part — panel frame, racking, enclosure — creating an unintended current path outside the circuit's normal, intended path. In a typical ungrounded or functionally-grounded PV array, this isn't automatically obvious the way it might be in a solidly grounded system, since there's no single fixed reference point where a stray current would show up as an easily measured imbalance.

Ground-fault protection devices in PV inverters and combiner equipment continuously monitor for that unintended current path across the whole array and are required to interrupt the circuit (and often signal a fault indication) when a ground fault is detected, since an undetected ground fault in an array — beyond posing a shock and fire hazard — can also quietly continue supplying current into a fault indefinitely given the array's continuous power generation whenever illuminated, unlike a conventional circuit that can simply be left unpowered until repaired.

PV systems carry markings well beyond what a typical residential electrical system requires — labels identifying DC disconnects, indicating that conductors remain energized whenever exposed to light, marking the location of rapid shutdown switches, and identifying different circuit types at junction points and combiner boxes. This isn't excess caution: PV systems have genuinely unusual characteristics compared to conventional electrical systems, chiefly that they can't simply be de-energized at a breaker the way a normal circuit can, since sunlight-exposed panels keep producing voltage regardless of what switch position anything else is in.

This matters most for firefighters and first responders who may need to work on or near a building with an unfamiliar PV installation during an emergency, often with limited time to investigate the system's specific layout — standardized labeling exists specifically so someone unfamiliar with a particular installation can quickly identify what's still energized, where the rapid shutdown control is, and which conductors to treat as hazardous, without needing to trace the system out first.

Interconnection & Codes

7 notes

Before a grid-tied PV system can be energized and connected, the utility typically requires a formal interconnection application and agreement, spelling out the technical requirements the system has to meet, the utility's rights around disconnection during grid maintenance or emergencies, and often liability and insurance terms for both parties. This process exists because a grid-tied system isn't simply consuming power the way a normal building load does — it's actively feeding power back into a shared network, which the utility has legitimate technical and safety reasons to review before allowing.

The scope of review scales with system size: a small residential system typically goes through a simplified, largely automatic interconnection process, while larger commercial or utility-scale systems often trigger more detailed engineering studies examining the system's impact on the local grid's voltage regulation, protection coordination, and capacity — which is why interconnection timelines can vary enormously between a small rooftop system and a large ground-mount array, even when both are technically straightforward installations otherwise.

IEEE 1547 is the standard governing how distributed energy resources like PV systems interconnect with and behave on the electric grid, and its more recent revisions require inverters to do meaningfully more than earlier generations did — including ride-through capability (staying connected through brief grid voltage or frequency disturbances rather than tripping offline immediately) and active grid support functions like voltage regulation, where the inverter adjusts its reactive power output to help support local grid voltage.

This shift reflects the grid's own changing reality: as distributed solar has grown from a negligible share of generation to a significant one in many areas, inverters simply disconnecting the moment grid conditions deviate even slightly from ideal would itself become a destabilizing influence at scale, removing generation exactly when the grid can least afford to lose it. Requiring inverters to ride through minor disturbances and actively support voltage instead turns what could be a liability at high penetration into a stabilizing resource.

The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) — typically a local building or electrical inspection department — reviews and permits a PV installation against building and electrical code requirements, a distinct process from utility interconnection approval and concerned with different questions: structural adequacy of the mounting, code-compliant wiring and overcurrent protection, fire and life-safety provisions like rapid shutdown, rather than the utility's concerns about grid impact and metering.

A completed PV installation typically needs both AHJ permit approval and utility interconnection approval before it can be legally operated, and the requirements enforced by each aren't always identical — an installation can be fully compliant with one and still need corrections to satisfy the other, which is why experienced installers track both approval processes as genuinely separate tracks rather than assuming one automatically implies the other.

Small PV systems are typically screened against a simplified set of technical criteria and, if they pass, approved for interconnection with minimal further review, since their individual impact on the surrounding distribution grid is small enough to be considered negligible. Larger systems, or systems proposed for a section of grid already carrying significant existing distributed generation, can trigger a more detailed interconnection study examining protection coordination, voltage regulation impact, and whether the local grid infrastructure can accommodate the added generation without upgrades.

These studies exist because a large generation source connected at the wrong point on a distribution circuit can genuinely create problems for other customers on that same circuit — voltage rising too high during periods of high generation and low local demand, for instance — problems that are far cheaper and safer to identify and address at the study stage than after the system is already built and energized.

A production meter measures the total energy a PV system generates, independent of how much of that energy the building consumes on-site versus exports to the grid — this is the number relevant to performance-based incentive programs and to simply knowing how much the system actually produced. A net meter (or the utility's existing bidirectional meter) instead measures the net flow of energy between the building and the grid — how much was imported when the system wasn't producing enough to cover load, and how much was exported when it produced more than the building was using at that moment.

These aren't interchangeable numbers: a system's total production can be considerably higher than what a net meter shows as exported, since a meaningful share of production may be consumed directly on-site and never register as an export at all. Programs and incentive structures that pay based on production require a dedicated production meter precisely because net metering data alone can't isolate how much energy the system actually generated versus how much was simply used on-site without ever crossing the meter.

Commissioning is the formal process of testing and verifying a newly installed PV system before it's accepted as complete and turned over for normal operation — checking that string voltages and currents match expected values for the array configuration and conditions, that ground-fault and arc-fault protection actually functions when tested, that all connections are properly torqued, and that the system's monitoring is reporting accurately. It exists because an installation can look complete and pass a purely visual inspection while still containing wiring errors, underperforming strings, or protective devices that were never actually verified to trip.

A commissioning report documenting these checks and their results also establishes a performance baseline the system can be compared against later — if output drops unexpectedly months or years into operation, having a documented commissioning baseline makes it far easier to identify that something has genuinely degraded or failed, rather than trying to guess what the system's original healthy performance actually looked like.

A panel's nameplate power rating is measured under Standard Test Conditions — a specific, controlled irradiance, cell temperature and spectral condition defined purely to let different panels be compared on equal footing, not to represent typical field operating conditions. Once installed and interconnected, a system's actual output is shaped by a much broader set of real-world factors — actual irradiance and temperature at the site, shading, soiling, inverter clipping when DC output exceeds the inverter's rated AC capacity, and grid conditions like voltage that can affect inverter operation.

This gap between nameplate rating and real-world interconnected performance is exactly why system design uses derating factors and performance modeling tools rather than simply multiplying a panel's nameplate wattage by panel count to predict actual energy production — a system built entirely from accurately rated components can still produce meaningfully less annual energy than a naive nameplate calculation would suggest, for reasons that have nothing to do with any component being defective or misrepresented.

These are summary explanations written for learning and quick reference — they're deliberately simplified and aren't a substitute for the current edition of the relevant standard (IEC 62548, IEC 61730, NEC/NFPA 70 Article 690, etc.) or for a qualified engineer's judgement on a real installation.
