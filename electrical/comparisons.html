Comparisons

Head-to-head technology and equipment choices — side by side, so the trade-off is visible at a glance

1

MCB vs MCCB vs ACB

Three breaker families that all interrupt overcurrent — separated mainly by how much current they're built to handle and how far you can adjust them.
CriteriaMCBMCCBACB
Typical rated currentUp to 125A125A – 2500A800A – 6300A+
Breaking capacityFixed, modest (typically ≤10kA)Higher, often adjustable by frame (up to ~100kA)Highest, built for main/incomer duty
Trip settingsFixed, non-adjustableOften adjustable (thermal-magnetic or electronic)Fully adjustable electronic trip units, standard
Typical applicationFinal circuits, distribution boardsSub-mains, distribution panels, motor feedersMain incomers, generator/utility tie breakers
Physical size / costSmallest, cheapestMid-size, mid-costLargest, most expensive
Draw-out capabilityNoOccasionally (larger frames)Common — allows maintenance without de-energizing the busbar
As a rule of thumb: MCB for final circuits under 125A with no need for adjustable settings, MCCB for anything larger or wherever adjustable trip settings matter for coordination, and ACB specifically for main incomers and generator/utility interfaces where draw-out maintenance and the highest breaking capacity are worth the extra size and cost.
2

Fuses vs Circuit Breakers

One sacrifices itself to clear a fault; the other resets. Both interrupt overcurrent, but the trade-offs run deeper than convenience.
CriteriaFuseCircuit Breaker
Reuse after operationNo — physical element must be replacedYes — mechanically resettable
Speed on high fault currentGenerally faster — no moving parts to actuateSlightly slower — mechanism has to physically trip
AdjustabilityNone — fixed rating per fuse elementOften adjustable (MCCB/ACB), fixed on MCB
Nuisance trip riskLow — no mechanism to drift out of calibrationSlightly higher — mechanical trip units can need recalibration
Local isolation / lockoutRequires a separate switch or disconnectBuilt-in — breaker itself provides isolation
Typical use todayHRC fuses in some distribution and motor circuits, especially where very fast clearing mattersDefault choice for most LV distribution and final circuits
Circuit breakers have displaced fuses as the default in most modern LV distribution simply because resetting a tripped breaker is faster and easier than sourcing and replacing a fuse element — but HRC fuses still earn their place wherever extremely fast fault clearing or simple, drift-free reliability matters more than resettability, such as some motor circuit and semiconductor protection applications.
3

TN vs TT vs IT Earthing Systems

How the supply's earth point and an installation's exposed metal are — or aren't — connected changes how faults get cleared and what protection you actually need.
CriteriaTNTTIT
Supply earthingEarthed at sourceEarthed at sourceUnearthed, or earthed via high impedance
Installation earthingBonded back to supply earth via protective conductorIndependent local earth electrodeIndependent local earth electrode
Typical fault clearanceOvercurrent protection (low loop impedance)RCD required (electrode resistance usually too high for overcurrent alone)First fault doesn't trip; insulation monitoring required
Continuity of supply on first faultTrips immediatelyTrips immediatelyContinues running — trips only on a second, independent fault
Typical applicationMost urban/utility-supplied LV networksRural networks, or where a shared supply earth isn't availableHospitals, ships, and other continuity-critical loads
TN is the default in most urban utility-supplied networks because it gives the lowest, most predictable fault-loop impedance. TT suits sites without a reliably low-impedance shared earth, at the cost of needing RCD protection to clear faults reliably. IT is a deliberate choice, not a fallback — it's specified specifically where continuity of supply through a first fault outweighs the added cost and complexity of insulation monitoring.
4

PVC vs XLPE vs EPR Cable Insulation

No insulation type is universally "better" — each survives a different combination of temperature, flexibility and cost.
CriteriaPVCXLPEEPR
Max conductor temp (continuous)70°C90°C90°C
Relative costLowestModerateHigher
FlexibilityGoodModerate — stiffer than PVCBest — designed for repeated flexing
Ampacity for a given CSALowest of the threeHigher (higher temp rating)Higher (higher temp rating)
Typical applicationGeneral LV building wiringMV cable, higher-capacity LV, fixed installationsMining, marine, portable/flexible industrial equipment
PVC remains the everyday choice for standard building wiring on cost alone. XLPE's higher temperature rating gives more ampacity per CSA, which is why it dominates MV cable and higher-capacity LV runs. EPR trades some of that thermal headroom for genuine flex-fatigue resistance — reach for it specifically where the cable will be handled, coiled or flexed repeatedly in service, not for fixed installations where XLPE's cost and performance balance wins.
5

Copper vs Aluminium Conductors

Aluminium isn't just "cheaper copper" — the two behave differently enough that they're not a drop-in swap.
CriteriaCopperAluminium
Conductivity (by volume)100% (reference)~61% of copper
CSA needed for equal ampacityBaselineRoughly 1.6× copper's CSA
Weight for equal ampacityHeavierRoughly half the weight of the equivalent copper run
Termination requirementsStandard lugs/terminalsNeeds oxide-inhibiting compound, specific torque values and often larger terminals
Relative material costHigherLower
Typical applicationBuilding wiring, tight spaces, panelsOverhead lines, large feeders where weight savings matter
Choose aluminium where its weight-per-ampacity advantage genuinely matters — long overhead runs, large feeder cables where every kilogram of supported weight counts — and be deliberate about termination practice, since aluminium's oxide layer and thermal expansion behavior make poorly prepared aluminium terminations a well-documented cause of overheating. Copper remains the safer default everywhere space, termination simplicity, or long-term reliability with less specialized workmanship outweighs the material cost saving.
6

DOL vs Star-Delta vs Soft Starter vs VFD Motor Starting

Four ways to get a motor from standstill to running speed, trading inrush current, starting torque, cost and control against each other.
CriteriaDOLStar-DeltaSoft StarterVFD
Inrush currentHighest (6–8× FLC)Reduced (~1/3 of DOL)Reduced, adjustable rampLowest — fully controlled
Starting torque controlNone — full torque instantlyTorque dip at transitionSmooth, adjustable rampFully adjustable, smoothest
Running speed controlNone — fixed speed onlyNone — fixed speed onlyNone — bypasses to fixed speed once startedFull variable speed throughout run
Relative costLowestLow-moderateModerateHighest
Typical applicationSmall motors, stiff supplyTraditional reduced-voltage starting, being phased outPumps/fans needing gentle start onlyAnywhere variable running speed adds value
DOL is fine wherever the supply can absorb the inrush and the load can tolerate full instant torque — small motors, mostly. Star-delta has been steadily displaced by soft starters, which give a smoother start without the torque-dip transition. A VFD costs the most but is the only option of the four that also controls running speed — spend the extra cost specifically when the application benefits from adjustable speed, not just a gentle start.
7

Air Circuit Breaker vs Vacuum Circuit Breaker (MV Switchgear)

Two arc-interruption media for medium-voltage switchgear, with different maintenance and lifespan trade-offs.
CriteriaAir Circuit Breaker (ACB, MV)Vacuum Circuit Breaker (VCB)
Arc interruption mediumAir (often with arc chutes)Vacuum interrupter bottle
Maintenance requirementHigher — contacts and arc chutes need periodic inspectionLower — vacuum interrupter is largely sealed and low-maintenance
Mechanical/electrical lifeModerateHigh — commonly rated for tens of thousands of operations
Physical sizeLarger for equivalent ratingMore compact
Typical voltage rangeHistorically common up to ~15kVDominant across most MV ranges today (up to ~38kV+)
Vacuum circuit breakers have become the default choice for most new medium-voltage switchgear specifically because of their low maintenance and long mechanical life compared to air breakers — air-break MV switchgear is now mostly encountered in older installations rather than specified new, except in some specific legacy or very high-current niche applications.
8

Synchronous vs Induction Motors

Both use a rotating magnetic field, but they lock onto it in fundamentally different ways — with real consequences for speed precision and power factor.
CriteriaInduction MotorSynchronous Motor
Rotor speed vs fieldAlways trails the field (slip)Locks exactly to synchronous speed, zero slip
Starting complexitySimple — direct or reduced-voltage startingMore complex — needs a starting method to reach synchronous speed
Power factorLagging, load-dependentCan run at leading PF — useful for site PF correction
Speed precision under loadVaries slightly with load (slip changes)Fixed regardless of load, as long as it stays in sync
Relative cost & complexityLower, simpler, more commonHigher, more complex construction and control
Typical applicationThe vast majority of general industrial and commercial motor loadsLarge constant-speed drives, sites wanting PF correction from the motor itself
Induction motors remain the default for the overwhelming majority of applications because they're simpler, cheaper, and more forgiving to start. Synchronous motors earn their extra cost and complexity specifically where precise, load-independent speed matters, or where running the motor at a leading power factor can offset a site's power factor correction requirement.
9

Lead-Acid vs Lithium-Ion Batteries (UPS / Standby Power)

Different battery chemistries trade upfront cost, footprint and lifespan against each other in different proportions.
CriteriaLead-Acid (VRLA)Lithium-Ion (e.g. LiFePO4)
Energy densityLower — larger, heavier for a given capacityHigher — smaller, lighter footprint
Cycle lifeShorter, typically a few hundred cycles at moderate depth of dischargeLonger, often several thousand cycles
Depth of discharge toleranceLimited — deep discharge shortens life significantlyMuch better tolerance for deep discharge
Upfront costLowerHigher (though the gap has narrowed)
Maintenance / monitoringSimpler, well-understoodRequires a battery management system
Typical applicationCost-sensitive, space-tolerant standby applicationsSpace-constrained or cycling-heavy applications where lifespan offsets upfront cost
Lead-acid remains a reasonable default for standby UPS applications that rarely cycle deeply and where space isn't tightly constrained — its lower upfront cost and simplicity are genuine advantages there. Lithium-ion earns its higher upfront cost specifically where space is limited, where the battery cycles more frequently, or where its much longer service life offsets the price difference over the system's lifetime.

IP Rating vs NEMA Rating (Enclosure Protection)

Two different rating systems, from two different regions, both describing how well an enclosure keeps the environment out — but not directly interchangeable.
CriteriaIP Rating (IEC 60529)NEMA Rating
StructureTwo digits: solids (0-6), then liquids (0-9K)A type number (e.g. 4, 4X, 12) bundling multiple protection aspects
Region of common useInternational (Europe, Asia, most of the world)North America
Covers corrosion resistanceNot directly — IP is purely ingress-focusedYes — some NEMA types specifically address corrosion resistance
Direct equivalence between systemsNo exact one-to-one mapping to NEMANo exact one-to-one mapping to IP
Typical spec usageUsed globally, including in IEC-based projectsUsed on North American projects and equipment
Match the rating system to what your project's governing code and equipment suppliers actually use — don't assume a NEMA 4 enclosure is simply "the same as" a specific IP rating and treat the two as interchangeable, since NEMA ratings bundle in considerations like corrosion resistance that the IP code doesn't directly address. When a project spans both conventions, check the specific published cross-reference tables rather than assuming a clean numeric equivalence.
