How to Size a Pump

Duty point first, then power, then the cavitation check — in that order, not the other way round.
  1. Establish the duty point: flow and total head. Flow comes from the actual process requirement. Total head is the sum of static lift (elevation change between suction and discharge), friction head loss through the full pipe run (see the pipe head loss guide below), and any residual pressure the fluid needs to arrive at on the discharge side — not just the elevation difference alone, which on its own routinely understates the real head a pump has to overcome.
  2. Plot the duty point against the pump's performance curve. A centrifugal pump doesn't deliver a single fixed flow and head — it delivers a range described by its curve, and the actual operating point is wherever that curve intersects the system's own head-loss curve. Selecting a pump means checking the duty point lands in the pump's efficient operating region on that curve, not just that the pump is theoretically capable of reaching the required head at some flow.
  3. Calculate shaft and motor power. Hydraulic power (P = ρgQH) is the useful work done on the fluid; shaft power is hydraulic power divided by pump efficiency at the duty point; motor power is shaft power with a margin added for drive losses and a safety margin against the pump curve's end-of-curve power draw, then rounded up to a standard motor size.
  4. Check NPSH available against NPSH required. This is the check most often skipped by hand, and the one most likely to cause real field problems — see the NPSH guide below for why it matters and how to calculate it.
Hydraulic power: P (kW) = ρ · g · Q · H / 1000  (Q in m³/s, H in m, ρ in kg/m³)
SH.02 on the Mechanical index runs the power and standard motor sizing steps together; the NPSH check on that same sheet is what actually catches a pump that would cavitate at its intended duty point, which power sizing alone never reveals.

How to Select a Pipe Size

The same trade-off as cable sizing — a bigger pipe costs more but loses less pressure; the right size balances both.
  1. Start from required flow rate, then work backward to find the pipe diameter that keeps velocity within a sensible range for that service — not the smallest pipe that can physically pass the flow.
  2. Apply a velocity guideline appropriate to the fluid and service. Typical liquid pipework is often kept in a rough 1–3 m/s range; too low wastes pipe material and space, too high increases friction loss, erosion risk, noise, and water-hammer severity on any sudden valve closure. Gas and steam lines run to their own, generally higher, velocity guidelines, and suction lines into a pump are kept deliberately slower than discharge lines to protect available NPSH.
  3. Check friction head loss at that size over the actual run length (see the head loss guide below) — a pipe sized purely on velocity can still produce more friction loss than the system can afford once fittings and total length are added up.
  4. Round to the nearest standard pipe size and re-check both velocity and head loss at that standard size, since actual internal diameter (especially for smaller nominal sizes) doesn't scale in an exactly linear way with nominal size.
Oversizing has a real, if less obvious, cost too: a pipe far larger than needed increases material and installation cost, and for a pump suction line, an oversized pipe can actually reduce velocity so much that entrained air or solids fail to stay in suspension — sizing is a genuine balance, not simply "bigger is always safer."

How to Calculate Pipe Head Loss

Friction loss from the straight pipe, plus a separate allowance for every fitting along the way.
Darcy-Weisbach: hf = f · (L/D) · (v²/2g)
f is the friction factor (from Colebrook-White or the Swamee-Jain approximation, itself a function of Reynolds number and pipe roughness), L is pipe length, D is internal diameter, v is flow velocity, and g is gravitational acceleration. This gives friction loss through straight pipe only.
  1. Calculate Reynolds number to confirm whether flow is laminar or turbulent — almost all practical pipe sizing work deals with turbulent flow, where the Colebrook-White/Swamee-Jain friction factor calculations apply; laminar flow uses a much simpler direct relationship, but is uncommon in typical pumped liquid service outside very viscous fluids or very small pipes.
  2. Get straight-pipe friction loss from the Darcy-Weisbach equation above, using the correct internal diameter and pipe roughness for the actual pipe material and condition (a corroded or scaled older pipe has a rougher effective internal surface than new pipe of the same nominal size).
  3. Add fitting losses. Every elbow, tee, valve, and reducer adds its own loss, commonly expressed either as an equivalent length of straight pipe to add to L, or as a K-factor multiplied by velocity head (K·v²/2g) and summed separately — either method works as long as it's applied consistently and not mixed inconsistently partway through a calculation.
  4. Sum straight-pipe loss and fitting losses to get total friction head loss for that pipe run, which then feeds directly into the pump sizing guide above as one component of total system head.
Fitting losses are easy to underestimate on a pipe run with many bends and valves packed into a short physical distance — a compact pump room with several close-together elbows and isolation valves can rack up fitting losses comparable to, or exceeding, the straight-pipe loss of a much longer, simpler run.

How to Size Ductwork

Pick a target velocity, work back to dimensions, then verify what that implies for friction loss and noise.
  1. Establish required airflow from room volume and target air-change rate, or directly from a load calculation (see the sensible heat load tool on this site) — airflow is the starting number everything else is sized from.
  2. Choose a target velocity appropriate to the duct's location. Main trunk ducts in mechanical spaces can run faster than branch ducts feeding diffusers directly above occupied, noise-sensitive space — velocity limits get progressively tighter the closer a duct run gets to occupants, specifically to keep air noise from becoming audible and objectionable.
  3. Calculate the required cross-sectional area from airflow and target velocity (A = Q/v), then convert to a round diameter or a rectangular width/height combination, rounding to a standard fabricated size.
  4. Check friction loss per unit length at the resulting size against the fan's available static pressure budget — a duct sized purely on velocity can still demand more total system pressure than the fan can deliver once the full run, including fittings, is added up, the same equal-friction-method logic used across a full duct network.
As with pipework, fittings — elbows, branch takeoffs, transitions — cost disproportionately more pressure loss per fitting than an equivalent length of straight duct, which is exactly why duct layout (fewer, gentler bends) has a real, measurable effect on total fan energy, not just on how tidy the ductwork looks above the ceiling.

How to Select a Belt Drive

Speed ratio sets the pulley sizes; transmitted power sets everything about the belt itself.
  1. Set the required speed ratio from driver speed (commonly the motor's rated RPM) and the required driven-equipment speed — the ratio between driven and driver pulley diameters is the inverse of the ratio between their speeds (a smaller driven pulley relative to the driver increases driven speed, and vice versa).
  2. Check belt pitch-line velocity. Once pulley diameters and driver speed are set, pitch-line velocity follows directly — most V-belt types have a recommended maximum velocity range, and exceeding it materially shortens belt life through excess flex fatigue and heat generation, independent of whether the transmitted power itself is within the belt's rated capacity.
  3. Size the belt (or belt set) for transmitted power, not just speed ratio — manufacturer belt selection charts convert design power (rated power with a service factor applied for shock loading, duty cycle, and starting characteristics) into a belt cross-section and quantity of belts needed in a multi-belt set.
  4. Check shaft torque and bearing loading. Belt tension itself imposes a radial load on both shafts that bearings have to be rated to carry, in addition to the torque being transmitted — a belt drive selected purely on transmitted power without checking resulting bearing loads can be adequate on power alone while still overloading the driven equipment's shaft bearings.
A service factor — an extra margin applied to actual required power before selecting a belt — exists specifically because belts see real-world shock loading, start-stop cycling, and duty variation that a simple steady-state power calculation doesn't capture; skipping it is a common cause of belts that wear out far faster than their nominal power rating would suggest.

Pump & Fan Affinity Laws Explained

One set of relationships that lets you predict performance at a new speed without re-testing the machine.
Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁    H₂/H₁ = (N₂/N₁)²    P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³
For a given centrifugal pump or fan with a fixed impeller diameter, flow scales linearly with speed, head (or pressure) scales with the square of speed, and power scales with the cube of speed. These relationships hold for the same machine at a different speed — they don't apply across two genuinely different machines or impeller designs.
  1. Use affinity laws to predict a VFD-driven machine's performance at reduced speed — a common and reliable use case, since the machine itself is unchanged, only its rotational speed varies.
  2. Understand the cubic power relationship's practical consequence: reducing speed by 20% cuts power draw by roughly 49% (0.8³ ≈ 0.512), which is exactly why variable-speed control on pumps and fans that spend meaningful time at part-load is such a high-value energy-saving measure compared to simply throttling a fixed-speed machine.
  3. Don't apply affinity laws across an impeller trim or a different machine. A separate, related set of relationships covers impeller diameter changes at fixed speed, but the two shouldn't be mixed casually — trimming an impeller changes the machine's efficiency characteristic in a way pure speed change doesn't, so trim-based scaling is generally only considered reliable over a fairly limited trim range.
  4. Re-check NPSH required at the new speed, since NPSHr also varies with speed (though not by the same simple cube-law relationship as power) — a machine confirmed safe from cavitation at full speed isn't automatically assumed safe at every other speed without checking.
SH.06 on the Mechanical index applies these three relationships directly to scale flow, head and power to a new speed for a specific pump or fan.

How to Size a Heat Exchanger

The core sizing relationship is simple; getting the driving temperature difference right is where the real work is.
Q = U · A · ΔTlm
Q is heat duty (the amount of heat that has to be transferred), U is the overall heat transfer coefficient (a property of the exchanger's construction, fluids, and fouling condition), A is the required heat transfer surface area, and ΔTlm is the log mean temperature difference between the two fluid streams across the exchanger.
  1. Establish the required heat duty from the process — the mass flow rate and required temperature change of the fluid being heated or cooled, using Q = ṁ · cp · ΔT for each stream.
  2. Calculate log mean temperature difference, not a simple arithmetic average — LMTD correctly accounts for the fact that the temperature difference between two streams typically varies along the exchanger's length rather than staying constant, and using a plain average instead of LMTD systematically overstates the effective driving temperature difference.
  3. Select or estimate an overall heat transfer coefficient (U) appropriate to the specific fluid pair and exchanger type — U varies enormously between, say, a gas-to-gas exchanger (low U) and a condensing steam-to-water exchanger (much higher U), so a generic assumed value borrowed from an unrelated application can produce a badly wrong sizing result.
  4. Include a fouling allowance. A brand-new, perfectly clean exchanger surface transfers heat noticeably better than the same surface after months or years of service accumulate scale, biological growth, or other fouling — sizing against clean-surface performance alone produces an exchanger that meets its duty on day one and then gradually falls short as fouling develops, which is why a fouling factor is applied to reduce the effective U used for sizing, not just for later performance-degradation prediction.
  5. Check flow arrangement (counter-current vs co-current vs cross-flow), since LMTD calculation and achievable performance both depend on it — counter-current arrangements generally achieve a higher effective LMTD than co-current for the same inlet and outlet temperatures, which is exactly why counter-current is the default arrangement wherever the exchanger's mechanical design allows it.

Bearing Selection Basics

A bearing's rated life is a statistical statement, not a guaranteed number for any one specific bearing.
  1. Determine actual applied loads — radial and axial (thrust) loads at the bearing location, from the equipment's actual operating conditions, not just the shaft's static weight. Belt drives, gears, and overhung loads all impose additional radial loads beyond a shaft's own weight that have to be calculated and included.
  2. Choose a bearing type suited to the actual load combination. Deep-groove ball bearings handle a mix of radial and modest axial load economically; angular contact and tapered roller bearings handle higher axial loads or combined loading more robustly; pure thrust bearings handle axial load with little to no radial capacity — selecting a bearing type mismatched to the actual load direction and magnitude is a common cause of premature failure even when the bearing's basic size looks adequate.
  3. Calculate required dynamic load rating for target L10 life. L10 life is the statistical life (typically in hours or millions of revolutions) that 90% of a population of identical bearings would be expected to reach or exceed under the calculated load — it's explicitly a statistical rating, not a guarantee for any single specific bearing, which is why critical applications often specify a target L10 life well beyond the equipment's expected service life, to keep the practical probability of an individual early failure acceptably low.
  4. Confirm lubrication regime and maintenance interval are compatible with the calculated life — a correctly selected bearing running with inadequate, contaminated, or infrequently replenished lubrication will fail well short of its calculated L10 life, since the life rating itself assumes a properly maintained lubrication film throughout that life.
Bearing selection interacts directly with belt drive design (see the belt drive guide above) — a belt tensioned tighter than necessary imposes extra radial shaft load that flows straight into the bearing life calculation, which is exactly why over-tensioning a belt "just to be safe" can quietly shorten bearing life even though the belt itself is running fine.

Thermal Expansion in Piping: A Design Guide

Pipe that heats up gets longer, and that growth has to go somewhere — deliberately, not by accident.
ΔL = α · L · ΔT
α is the material's coefficient of linear thermal expansion, L is the pipe run's original length, and ΔT is the temperature change between installed (cold) and operating (hot) condition. Steel expands noticeably less per degree than plastics like PVC or PEX, which is part of why plastic pipe runs typically need more, and more closely spaced, expansion accommodation than an equivalent steel run over the same temperature range.
  1. Calculate expected linear growth for the actual pipe run length and expected temperature swing using the formula above — see SH.07-equivalent on the Mechanical index for this calculation directly.
  2. Decide how that growth will be accommodated: a deliberately designed expansion loop or offset in the pipe run, a mechanical expansion joint, or natural flexibility built into the pipe routing itself (an "L" or "Z" shaped run flexes more readily than one long straight run of the same total length).
  3. Check anchor and guide placement. Fixed anchors define exactly where expansion is forced to occur (and not occur), while intermediate pipe guides keep the pipe moving in its intended direction as it grows rather than buckling sideways — anchor and guide placement is a deliberate part of the design, not simply wherever the pipe happens to touch a support.
  4. Verify the resulting stress on the pipe and any connected equipment nozzles is within allowable limits — uncontrolled thermal growth transmitted directly into a pump or vessel nozzle can overstress that connection well beyond what the pipe itself would tolerate, which is why equipment nozzle loading is specifically checked, not just the pipe's own stress.
A pipe run that looks perfectly straight and well-supported when cold can bind, buckle, or overstress a connected nozzle once it reaches operating temperature if expansion wasn't deliberately designed for — this is exactly the kind of problem that's invisible at installation and only shows up once the system is actually running hot.

NPSH and Cavitation Explained

The check that determines whether a pump can actually pull fluid in without the fluid boiling inside it.
As fluid accelerates into a pump's impeller eye, its local pressure drops — and if that pressure drops below the fluid's vapor pressure at the operating temperature, the fluid locally boils, forming vapor bubbles. Those bubbles then collapse violently as they move into the higher-pressure region further into the impeller, a phenomenon called cavitation, which causes both a sharp drop in pump performance and, over time, physical erosion damage to the impeller.
NPSHa = (Pa/ρg) + hs − hf − (Pv/ρg)
NPSH available (NPSHa) is calculated from the actual installation: atmospheric (or source vessel) pressure, static suction head (positive if the source is above the pump, negative if below), friction losses in the suction line, and the fluid's vapor pressure at operating temperature — all expressed as head. NPSH required (NPSHr) is a property of the specific pump at its specific operating point, published by the manufacturer on its performance curve.
  1. Calculate NPSHa for the actual installation — suction lift or flooded suction, suction line friction loss at the actual duty flow, and fluid vapor pressure at the actual (not assumed-cold) operating temperature, since vapor pressure rises sharply with temperature and a fluid comfortably safe when cold can be much closer to cavitating when hot.
  2. Read NPSHr from the pump curve at the actual duty flow, not at the pump's best-efficiency point — NPSHr increases with flow rate along the curve, so checking it only at BEP can miss a real cavitation risk at a higher actual operating flow.
  3. Confirm NPSHa exceeds NPSHr with adequate margin — a small margin might satisfy the numbers on paper while still leaving a pump vulnerable to cavitation from minor real-world variations (a partially fouled suction strainer, a slightly higher fluid temperature than assumed), which is why a deliberate safety margin above the bare minimum is standard practice, not just checking that NPSHa is technically greater than NPSHr.
Suction-side problems are disproportionately common causes of pump cavitation in the field — a partially closed suction valve, a clogged strainer, or a suction line that's undersized or has too many fittings all directly reduce NPSHa below what was assumed at design time, which is why cavitation troubleshooting generally starts on the suction side rather than assuming a pump fault.

Material Selection for Pressure Piping

Pressure and temperature rating are only the starting filter — corrosion compatibility usually narrows it the rest of the way.
  1. Confirm pressure-temperature rating. Every pipe material and schedule has a maximum allowable pressure that decreases as operating temperature increases — a material rated adequately for the system's design pressure at room temperature can be inadequate at the system's actual elevated operating temperature, which is why pressure rating always has to be checked at the actual operating temperature, not at a generic room-temperature rating.
  2. Check chemical compatibility with the actual process fluid. A material that's mechanically strong enough for the pressure and temperature can still fail rapidly through corrosion if it's chemically incompatible with the specific fluid, its concentration, and any contaminants it carries — compatibility has to be checked against the actual fluid composition, not just its generic name (many corrosive failures come from a fluid's trace contaminants or additives, not the nominal base fluid itself).
  3. Consider erosion and abrasion for fluids carrying entrained solids or operating at high velocity — a material with excellent general corrosion resistance can still wear through relatively quickly under erosive service if it wasn't also selected with erosion resistance in mind.
  4. Account for thermal expansion characteristics (see the thermal expansion guide above) as part of material selection, not as an afterthought once the material is already chosen — different materials expand at meaningfully different rates, which affects how much expansion accommodation the piping design needs.
  5. Verify code compliance and required certifications for the specific service — pressure piping codes typically require materials to be from a qualified, traceable source with appropriate certification, particularly for higher-pressure or hazardous service, and substituting an uncertified but nominally equivalent material can be a genuine code compliance failure even if the material itself is metallurgically similar.
Material selection is rarely a single clear-cut answer — it's a deliberate trade-off between cost, availability, corrosion life, and installation practicality, which is why the same service condition can reasonably be met with more than one acceptable material choice depending on a project's specific priorities.

How to Read a P&ID

A piping and instrumentation diagram shows the full process detail an SLD deliberately leaves out.
A P&ID (piping and instrumentation diagram) shows every pipe, valve, instrument, and major equipment item in a process system, along with how they're connected — unlike a simplified process flow diagram (PFD), which shows the overall process concept without full valve and instrument detail, a P&ID is meant to be complete enough to actually build and operate the system from.
  1. Identify major equipment first — vessels, pumps, heat exchangers, tanks — using their standard symbols, then trace the pipe lines connecting them to understand overall process flow before focusing on any one section's instrument detail.
  2. Learn the standard instrument tag format (largely following ISA-5.1 conventions) — a circle represents a field-mounted instrument, a circle inside a square represents a shared control-system display, and the tag's letters identify what it measures and does: the first letter for the measured variable (F for flow, P for pressure, T for temperature, L for level), following letters for its function (I for indicator, C for controller, T for transmitter, V for valve) — a tag like "PIC-204" reads directly as a pressure indicating controller, loop 204.
  3. Trace valve types and their function. Isolation valves (typically gate or ball valves) simply open or close a line for maintenance; control valves throttle continuously to maintain a process variable; check valves allow flow in one direction only, protecting against reverse flow; relief valves open automatically on overpressure as a safety device — distinguishing these by their P&ID symbol clarifies each valve's actual role in the system, not just that "there's a valve there."
  4. Note line specifications and service. Pipe lines on a P&ID are typically labeled with a line number encoding size, material specification, and service, which matters directly for the material selection and pipe sizing guides above — the P&ID is often the reference document those decisions get pulled from during design, and get recorded back into once finalized.
  5. Check the revision block before relying on the drawing. As with any engineering drawing, a P&ID used for operations, troubleshooting, or further design work has to be the current revision — an out-of-date P&ID can be actively misleading if the actual installed system has since been modified.
