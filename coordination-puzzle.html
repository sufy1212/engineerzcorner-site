    About Coordination Puzzle

    Protection coordination (also called selectivity or discrimination) means that when a fault occurs, only the protective device closest to it operates, so the rest of the network stays energised. On a time–current chart that shows up as two curves that never touch: the downstream device’s curve sits below and to the left of the upstream device’s curve, with a gap of at least the grading margin (also called the coordination time interval, CTI) at every current up to the maximum fault.

    What each goal checks

    Carries the load, protects the cable. The pickup current must sit above about 1.1 × the normal load current, or the relay would trip in service, and at or below the cable’s current rating, or an overload could cook the cable without a trip. Rides through the motor start. A motor draws several times its full-load current for a few seconds while it accelerates; the relay curve has to stay above that point. Cable survives the fault. The cable’s thermal limit (k²S² = I²t) is drawn as a dotted amber curve, and your relay has to trip well inside it. Trips before the upstream relay. This is the coordination check itself.

    The relay curves in this game

    Each relay follows the IEC 60255 inverse-time form t = TMS × k / ((I / Is)α − 1), where Is is the pickup current and TMS is the time multiplier setting. Changing the pickup slides the curve left or right on the log chart; changing the TMS slides it up or down — which is why you can drag it like a rigid shape.

    This is a simplified learning model with a fixed set of rules of thumb: a 1.1 × load pickup floor, a 90% cable-damage limit and a 15% motor-start allowance. Real coordination studies use manufacturer curves, CT saturation checks, breaker and fuse characteristics and the site’s protection philosophy.
