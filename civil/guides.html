How to Size a Beam for Bending & Deflection

Two separate checks, and a beam that passes one can still fail the other.
  1. Establish the span and loading first. Get the actual support condition (simply supported, cantilever, fixed) and the real load pattern (uniformly distributed, point load, or a combination) — the bending moment and deflection formulas are entirely different for each case, so starting from the wrong support/load assumption invalidates everything downstream.
  2. Calculate the maximum bending moment and check it against the section's moment capacity (allowable stress × section modulus, or the code-specific moment capacity for the material). This is the strength check — it confirms the beam won't fail structurally.
  3. Separately calculate deflection at the point of maximum deflection (usually midspan for a simply supported beam) and check it against a serviceability limit, typically expressed as span/240, span/360, or similar depending on what the beam supports. A beam can easily pass the strength check with room to spare and still fail deflection — deflection governs more often than people expect, especially on longer spans or with brittle finishes like plaster or tile above.
  4. Re-check both if you change the section — increasing depth helps deflection far more than it helps bending stress (deflection scales with depth cubed via I, bending stress only scales with depth via Z), so the section that just barely passes bending by depth alone may still fail deflection, and vice versa.
UDL, simply supported: M = wL²/8  ·  δ = 5wL⁴/384EI
CV.01 on the Civil index runs both checks together from the same span and load inputs, for either a UDL or a midspan point load.

How to Check a Column for Axial Capacity

Short columns fail by crushing; slender columns fail by buckling — and most real columns need both checks.
  1. Calculate the slenderness ratio (effective length ÷ radius of gyration, or a similar code-specific ratio) first — this single number determines which failure mode governs and which formula actually applies.
  2. For a short, stocky column, capacity is governed by material crushing — cross-sectional area times the material's allowable compressive stress, with relatively little reduction for buckling since the column is too short to buckle before it crushes.
  3. For a slender column, capacity is governed by buckling, not material strength — the column can fail well below its crushing capacity because it bows sideways under load. Buckling capacity depends heavily on the effective length, which itself depends on the end restraint conditions (pinned, fixed, or free) — getting the effective length factor wrong is one of the most common column-sizing mistakes.
  4. Apply the appropriate reduction/safety factor for the governing failure mode and confirm the applied axial load, including any eccentricity, stays within the reduced capacity — an eccentric or off-center load on a column adds bending on top of the axial load, which most simple axial checks don't account for and needs a separate combined-stress check.
End restraint is the detail most often assumed rather than actually verified — a column intended to be fixed at both ends but actually only partially restrained in practice has a longer effective length (and lower capacity) than the design assumed.

How to Size a Spread Footing

The footing has to be big enough that the soil beneath it doesn't get overstressed — nothing more, nothing less.
  1. Get the actual allowable soil bearing pressure for the site, from a geotechnical report where one exists — see the soil bearing capacity guide below for how this number is actually derived, and why an assumed generic value is a real risk on anything but a small, low-consequence structure.
  2. Calculate the required footing plan area by dividing the total applied load (including the footing's own weight and any soil above it) by the allowable bearing pressure — this gives the minimum area, which then gets rounded up to a practical, buildable footing size.
  3. Check bearing pressure isn't uniform if the load is eccentric. A column load applied off the footing's centroid, or a footing carrying a moment as well as axial load, produces a non-uniform pressure distribution across the footing base — the maximum edge pressure (not the average) is what has to stay within the allowable bearing pressure.
  4. Size the footing thickness and reinforcement separately, as a structural (not geotechnical) check — the footing itself has to resist the bending and shear the soil pressure induces underneath it, which is a completely separate calculation from sizing the plan area for bearing.
A footing sized purely for bearing pressure without checking settlement can still perform poorly — bearing capacity and settlement are two different failure modes, and on soft or compressible soils, settlement (not bearing failure) is often what actually governs the allowable pressure in the geotechnical report to begin with.

How to Check a Retaining Wall for Stability

Three separate ways a retaining wall can fail, and passing one check says nothing about the other two.
  1. Calculate the lateral earth pressure the retained soil exerts on the wall, using an appropriate earth pressure coefficient for the soil type and wall movement condition (active, at-rest, or passive) — this lateral force is what drives every stability check that follows.
  2. Check overturning stability — the ratio of the resisting moment (from the wall and footing's own weight, and any soil on the footing heel) to the overturning moment (from the lateral earth pressure) has to exceed a code-specified minimum factor of safety, commonly around 1.5–2.0.
  3. Check sliding stability separately — the ratio of the resisting friction force at the base (footing weight times a friction coefficient, sometimes plus passive resistance in front of the footing) to the lateral driving force, again against its own minimum factor of safety. A wall can easily pass overturning while failing sliding, or vice versa — they're independent checks with independent safety factors.
  4. Check bearing pressure at the footing base as a third, separate check, the same way as an ordinary spread footing (see the footing guide above) — note the pressure distribution under a retaining wall footing is rarely uniform, since the lateral load creates an overturning moment on the footing itself.
Drainage behind the wall matters as much as any of these three calculations — a retaining wall designed assuming drained, non-saturated backfill can see a dramatic, sometimes doubling, increase in lateral pressure if that drainage fails and the backfill saturates, which is why a functioning drainage system isn't optional on a real wall design.

How to Design a Concrete Mix

Strength, workability and durability all pull the water-cement ratio in different directions.
  1. Start from the required strength (f'c) and exposure conditions — a mix designed purely for strength can still fail prematurely in a harsh exposure (freeze-thaw, sulfate soils, marine environment) if durability requirements for that exposure class aren't also built into the mix.
  2. Set the water-cement ratio. This single ratio is the dominant factor controlling both strength (lower w/c ratio generally means higher strength) and durability (lower w/c ratio generally means lower permeability, better durability) — but a mix that's too dry becomes difficult to place and consolidate properly, which introduces its own strength-robbing defects (voids, honeycombing).
  3. Select aggregate size and gradation appropriate for the element being cast — a mix with aggregate too large for a heavily reinforced section won't flow properly between closely spaced bars, again risking voids regardless of how well the water-cement ratio was chosen.
  4. Add admixtures deliberately, not automatically — water reducers, air-entrainment (essential for freeze-thaw exposure), retarders or accelerators each solve a specific placement or durability problem, and adding them without a specific reason can alter the mix's behavior in ways that weren't accounted for in the original design.
  5. Verify with trial batches and cylinder breaks, not just the mix design calculation alone — actual materials vary from assumed properties, and a mix design is a starting point that gets confirmed (and adjusted) with real test data before it's used structurally.
Adding extra water on site to make a stiff mix easier to place is one of the most common ways a correctly designed mix ends up under-strength in practice — it directly raises the water-cement ratio beyond what the mix was designed for, and the strength loss isn't visually obvious until the cylinder breaks come back low.

Rebar Development Length & Lap Splices Explained

A bar has to be embedded far enough into the concrete to actually transfer its full tensile force through bond — cutting it short doesn't just weaken the connection, it can fail it outright.
Development length is the embedment length a reinforcing bar needs, on each side of the point where its full tensile force is required, for bond stress between the bar and surrounding concrete to actually develop that full force before the bar could pull out. A bar that ends (or is spliced) before reaching its required development length simply can't deliver its full rated strength at that location, no matter how strong the bar itself is.
  1. Development length increases with bar size and yield strength, and decreases with concrete strength — a larger-diameter or higher-strength bar needs more embedment length to develop its (larger) full tensile force, while higher-strength concrete provides better bond per unit length, reducing the length needed.
  2. A lap splice is two bars overlapped by at least the required lap length so that force transfers from one bar, through the surrounding concrete, into the other — lap length is typically somewhat longer than the basic development length, and code minimums vary by splice classification (tension vs compression, and how much of the bar area is spliced at one location).
  3. Bar spacing, cover, and confinement (stirrups/ties) all affect actual bond strength — the code-tabulated development lengths already build in factors for these, which is why using a bar spacing or cover thinner than what a development-length table assumes can leave the actual capacity below what the table implies, even though the calculated length looks adequate on paper.
  4. Stagger lap splices where possible rather than lapping every bar in a group at the same cross-section — concentrating every splice at one location concentrates a weaker, more congested section right there, which is generally avoided in critical members.

How to Read a Structural Drawing

A general arrangement drawing shows what's there; the schedules and details show what it's actually made of.
  1. Start with the general arrangement (GA) drawings to understand overall layout — grid lines, member locations, and how the structure fits together as a whole — before diving into any individual member's detail.
  2. Learn the grid reference system. Structural drawings label a grid of reference lines (typically numbers one direction, letters the other) so every member and detail can be located unambiguously by its grid intersection — this is how a beam, column, or footing gets referenced consistently across the GA, schedules, and details.
  3. Cross-reference member schedules (beam schedule, column schedule, footing schedule) for the actual section sizes, reinforcement, and material grade — the GA drawing typically shows a member's location and a reference mark, not its full specification, which lives in the schedule.
  4. Check details and sections for connection and reinforcement specifics — how a beam actually connects to a column, or exactly how reinforcement is arranged in a critical section, is shown in a separate detail or section cut, not on the GA drawing itself.
  5. Always check the revision block and confirm you have the current issue before relying on any drawing — as with any engineering drawing, a superseded revision can be actively misleading if the design has since changed, and construction from an outdated revision is a common, entirely avoidable source of costly rework.

Load Types and Load Combinations Explained

A structure is never checked against just one load acting alone — it's checked against combinations, because the worst case usually isn't any single load by itself.
  1. Dead load is the permanent, unchanging weight of the structure itself and anything permanently attached to it — generally the most predictable load, since it doesn't vary over time.
  2. Live load is the variable, transient load from occupancy and use — people, furniture, stored goods, vehicles — and is inherently less predictable, which is why codes specify conservative minimum live load values by occupancy type rather than relying on an actual measured load.
  3. Environmental loads (wind, snow, seismic) are separate load types again, each with their own code-specified calculation method, and each capable of governing the design in different circumstances — a structure in a high-wind coastal zone and a structure in a high-seismic zone can end up with very different governing load cases despite identical dead and live loads.
  4. Load combinations apply factored sums of these loads together, not each load checked in isolation — a typical combination might factor dead load higher than 1.0 and add a factored live load and a factored environmental load together, because the realistic worst case is usually several loads acting simultaneously, each at some level below their own individual maximum.
  5. The governing combination often isn't the one with the most loads added together — different combinations can govern different members or failure modes in the same structure, which is why a proper structural check runs every applicable combination rather than assuming the "biggest-looking" one automatically controls.

How to Size a Staircase

Riser and tread dimensions aren't independent choices — comfortable stairs follow a fairly narrow, well-established relationship between the two.
  1. Start from the total floor-to-floor rise and divide it into a whole number of equal risers — risers within a single flight have to be uniform, since an inconsistent riser height is a well-documented trip hazard.
  2. Check the riser/tread combination against a comfort rule, commonly expressed as 2×riser + tread landing in a target range (roughly 600–650mm, or the equivalent in your applicable code) — this rule reflects the natural stride length people use on stairs, and combinations well outside it feel awkward or unsafe even if each individual dimension seems reasonable.
  3. Confirm riser and tread each independently meet code minimums/maximums for the occupancy type — a combination can satisfy the 2R+T comfort rule while still having an individual riser or tread outside what your governing code separately requires.
  4. Check headroom clearance along the full stair run, not just at the top and bottom — headroom is commonly checked at a fixed height above the nosing line, and a structural element (beam, soffit) that clears at one point along the stair can still intrude on required headroom partway up the flight.
Comfort check: 2 × Riser + Tread ≈ 600–650 mm
CV.06 on the Civil index runs the riser/tread split and comfort check together from a target floor-to-floor rise.

Soil Bearing Capacity Basics

The number a footing design actually depends on almost always comes from a geotechnical investigation, not a generic assumption.
  1. Ultimate bearing capacity is the soil pressure that causes a shear failure beneath a footing — this is a theoretical maximum, derived from soil strength parameters (friction angle, cohesion), footing geometry, and depth of embedment, using a bearing capacity theory such as Terzaghi's or a similar code-adopted method.
  2. Allowable bearing pressure applies a substantial safety factor to the ultimate capacity — commonly a factor of 2.5–3 or more — both to guard against uncertainty in the soil parameters and because, in practice, settlement rather than outright shear failure is often the actual limiting concern at pressures well below ultimate capacity.
  3. Bearing capacity depends heavily on footing depth and width, not just the soil type alone — a deeper footing generally has higher bearing capacity in the same soil, because of the confining effect of the soil above founding level, which is one reason minimum footing depths are specified even where bearing capacity alone might seem to allow a shallower footing.
  4. Soil conditions vary across a site and with depth — a bearing value from one borehole or test pit doesn't necessarily apply uniformly across an entire building footprint, which is why larger or more sensitive projects use multiple investigation points rather than a single assumed value for the whole site.
An assumed generic bearing pressure (a common shortcut for very small, low-consequence structures) is a real risk once the structure or the soil gets less straightforward — soft clays, fill, or high water table conditions can produce allowable pressures well below generic assumptions, and only a real geotechnical investigation reveals that.

How to Read a Geotechnical Report

The report exists to answer specific design questions — bearing capacity, settlement, water table, and any special site hazards.
  1. Check the boring/test pit logs first to understand the actual soil profile at the site — layer types, depths, and any groundwater encountered — since every recommendation in the report is specific to that profile, not a generic site condition.
  2. Find the allowable bearing capacity recommendation and note what founding depth and footing type it assumes — a bearing value in the report is almost always tied to a specific assumed minimum founding depth, and using it at a shallower depth than assumed isn't valid.
  3. Check the groundwater table depth — a high water table affects bearing capacity, can create buoyancy/uplift concerns for below-grade structures, and often drives specific drainage or waterproofing recommendations elsewhere in the report.
  4. Read the settlement estimate, not just the bearing capacity — a report often recommends an allowable bearing pressure specifically limited by an acceptable settlement amount, which can be a lower number than what bearing/shear capacity alone would allow.
  5. Note any special hazards or recommendations — expansive soils, fill material, liquefaction potential, or corrosive/aggressive soil chemistry — these can drive specific foundation type or material recommendations that override what a bearing-capacity number alone would suggest.

Concrete Curing: Why Time and Moisture Matter

Concrete doesn't just dry — it gains strength through an ongoing chemical reaction that needs sustained moisture to continue.
  1. Strength gain comes from cement hydration, a chemical reaction between cement and water that continues for weeks (and at a slower rate, much longer) after initial set — it's not simply the concrete "drying out," and in fact drying out too early actively stops the reaction before it's finished.
  2. Curing means keeping concrete moist (and within a reasonable temperature range) for a sustained period after placement — commonly a minimum of about 7 days for standard portland cement concrete, longer for mixes designed to reach strength more slowly, using methods like wet burlap, curing compounds, or ponding.
  3. The commonly cited "28-day strength" is a standard reference point, not a hard deadline — hydration continues beyond 28 days, just at a progressively slower rate, which is why 28 days was adopted as a practical, standardized point for specifying and testing concrete strength, not because strength gain actually stops there.
  4. Inadequate curing is a common, often invisible cause of underperforming concrete — concrete that loses moisture too early can look visually fine while never developing its full specified strength, and the deficiency typically isn't obvious until a cylinder break or core test comes back low.
  5. Hot weather and cold weather both need specific curing adjustments — hot, windy conditions accelerate surface moisture loss and need more aggressive moisture retention; cold conditions slow hydration and risk freezing before adequate strength develops, needing insulation or heating instead.
