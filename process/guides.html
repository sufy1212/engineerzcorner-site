How to Size a Relief Valve (Preliminary Method)

A first-pass orifice estimate before handing the job to a manufacturer's certified sizing software.
  1. Identify the governing overpressure scenario — fire, blocked outlet, control valve failure, tube rupture, and so on. The largest credible relief load sets the design case, not the average operating condition.
  2. Establish set pressure and allowable accumulation from the vessel's design pressure and the applicable code — typically 10% overpressure for a single valve on non-fire cases.
  3. Determine the relieving phase — liquid, vapor, or two-phase. This calculator only covers the first two; two-phase relief needs a dedicated method (e.g. the omega method).
  4. Calculate the required relieving rate for the governing scenario — often the hardest step, and usually specific to the scenario (e.g. a heat-input correlation for fire case).
  5. Apply the API 520 sizing equation for the relieving phase to get a required orifice area, then round up to the nearest standard API 526 orifice letter.
  6. Check backpressure and installation effects — a conventional valve with high built-up backpressure, or a balanced-bellows/pilot-operated valve, may need a backpressure correction factor this simplified calculator doesn't include.
2

How to Check a Control Valve for Cavitation

A quick sigma-index screen to catch a cavitation-prone application before it becomes a maintenance problem.
  1. Gather the process conditions at the worst-case operating point — usually maximum pressure drop, not average flow — since cavitation risk is highest where ΔP is largest relative to available pressure.
  2. Look up the fluid's vapor pressure at the process temperature, not at standard conditions — vapor pressure rises sharply with temperature for most fluids.
  3. Calculate the service sigma from upstream pressure, downstream pressure and vapor pressure.
  4. Compare against the valve's incipient cavitation sigma from the manufacturer's data — a generic default only gets you a rough screen, not a final answer.
  5. If cavitation risk is flagged, consider an anti-cavitation trim, splitting the pressure drop across two valves in series, or relocating the valve to a point with more favorable downstream pressure.
3

How to Read a Process P&ID

A systematic way to make sense of a drawing you're seeing for the first time.
  1. Start with the major equipment — vessels, pumps, exchangers, columns — and identify each by its equipment tag before worrying about instrumentation.
  2. Trace the main process flow path from feed to product, following the heavy process lines rather than the thinner instrument signal lines.
  3. Identify each instrument bubble's tag letters — first letter is the measured variable, later letters describe the function (indicator, controller, transmitter, alarm).
  4. Follow each control loop from sensor to final element — a transmitter feeding a controller feeding a control valve, tracing the signal line type (electric, pneumatic, software) along the way.
  5. Note every safety element — relief valves, ESD valves, interlocks — since these define the vessel's protection envelope, not just its normal operation.
  6. Cross-check against the line list and equipment list if available — a P&ID alone doesn't carry line sizes, materials or design conditions.

How to Size a Dilution Purge

Getting from "we need to purge this vessel" to an actual flow rate and time.
  1. Define the initial and target concentrations clearly — for an oxygen purge ahead of vessel entry, this typically means normal air (20.9% O₂) down to a code-defined safe entry threshold.
  2. Confirm the enclosure volume, including any internals that reduce the free gas volume — a vessel full of packing or trays has meaningfully less free volume than its nominal shell volume.
  3. Choose a purge gas flow rate the available supply can actually sustain continuously for the calculated duration.
  4. Calculate the theoretical purge time from the dilution model, then add a practical safety margin — real vessels rarely mix as perfectly as the ideal model assumes, especially with dead legs or low-velocity zones.
  5. Verify by direct measurement before entry or hot work — the calculation sets an expected purge time, but a gas test at the point of use is what actually confirms the target concentration was reached.

How to Estimate Vessel Liquid Volume From a Level Reading

Converting a level transmitter reading into an actual inventory number.
  1. Confirm the vessel orientation and geometry — horizontal and vertical cylindrical tanks use different volume-vs-level relationships, and the difference is largest around the mid-fill point on a horizontal tank.
  2. Establish the level measurement's zero reference — a DP level transmitter's 0% point may not be the physical bottom of the shell, depending on how it's ranged.
  3. Calculate the shell (cylindrical) volume at the measured level using the appropriate segment or straight cylindrical formula.
  4. Add head volume separately if the tank has dished, elliptical or hemispherical heads — these aren't included in a flat-head cylindrical approximation and can be a meaningful fraction of total volume on shorter vessels.
  5. Cross-check against a strapping table or calibration chart if one exists for the specific tank — a calculated volume is only as good as the geometry it assumes, while a strapping table reflects the tank's actual as-built shape.

How to Apply the API RP 14E Erosional Velocity Guideline

Using a decades-old rule of thumb correctly, including knowing where it doesn't apply well.
  1. Establish the fluid's mixture density at actual flowing conditions — for two-phase flow this means a mixture density, not the liquid or gas density alone.
  2. Select the correct C constant for the service — continuous vs intermittent, and whether the line is corrosion-resistant or has effective corrosion inhibition.
  3. Calculate the erosional velocity limit and compare against the actual design velocity at maximum expected flow, not average flow.
  4. Treat a comfortable margin below the limit as the goal, not the limit itself as a target — operating right at Ve leaves no room for measurement uncertainty or future flow increases.
  5. Remember the guideline's limitations — it doesn't account for solids content, sharp bends, or specific corrosive species (CO₂, H₂S) that can lower the practical erosion threshold well below the generic API 14E number.
