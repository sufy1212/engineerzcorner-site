How this is calculated
—
Counter-current: ΔT1 = Thi−Tco, ΔT2 = Tho−Tci. Co-current: ΔT1 = Thi−Tci, ΔT2 = Tho−Tco. LMTD = (ΔT1−ΔT2)/ln(ΔT1/ΔT2) — for ΔT1 ≈ ΔT2, LMTD converges to that common value. Area = Q / (U · F · LMTD).

Counter-Current vs Co-Current

Counter-current (the hot and cold streams flowing in opposite directions) almost always gives a higher LMTD than co-current for the same four terminal temperatures, which means a smaller required area for the same duty — it's the default choice for most shell-and-tube and double-pipe exchangers. Co-current (parallel flow) is occasionally chosen when a fast initial approach temperature is wanted, or where limiting the maximum metal/tube-wall temperature matters, since the temperature difference is highest at the inlet end and falls off quickly.

If either ΔT1 or ΔT2 comes out zero or negative, that particular arrangement can't achieve those four terminal temperatures — the hot stream would need to end up colder than the cold stream at that end, which is a temperature cross. Counter-current can sometimes still work in that case even when co-current can't, because of how the temperature differences are paired at each end.
