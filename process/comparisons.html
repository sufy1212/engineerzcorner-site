1

Relief Valve vs Rupture Disk

Two ways to protect a vessel from overpressure, with very different behavior once they've operated.
CriteriaSpring-Loaded Relief ValveRupture Disk
Behavior after actuationRecloses once pressure drops back below set/reseat pressureRuptures permanently — the process stays open to atmosphere/flare until manually isolated and the disk replaced
Response speedFast, but not instantaneous — some inertia in openingEssentially instantaneous, no moving parts to overcome
Leak-tight at normal operationYes, typically bubble-tight to a high percentage of set pressureYes, and often better — no seat to wear or foul over time
Typical useGeneral overpressure protection where reclosing after a transient event is desirableFast-rising pressure events (runaway reaction), corrosive/fouling service unsuitable for a valve seat, or as a PSV inlet isolator
DownsideMore expensive, more maintenance (seat leakage, chatter)Loses containment entirely once it bursts — not suitable alone where continuous protection after a transient is required
In practice: the two are often combined — a rupture disk installed upstream of a PSV protects the valve seat from a fouling or corrosive process while the PSV still recloses after normal transients.
2

Cavitation vs Flashing

Related liquid-service phenomena in a control valve, but with different symptoms and consequences.
CriteriaCavitationFlashing
What happensVapor bubbles form at the vena contracta, then collapse violently once downstream pressure recovers above vapor pressureVapor bubbles form and never collapse — downstream pressure stays at or below vapor pressure
Physical damageErosion from repeated micro-shockwave bubble collapse — can be severeGenerally no bubble-collapse erosion, though high-velocity two-phase flow can still erode downstream piping
NoiseOften loud, characteristic "gravel" or rattling soundCan be noisy from high-velocity two-phase flow, different character than cavitation noise
FixAnti-cavitation trim, staged pressure drop, or relocate the valveUsually inherent to the process pressures — trim material/design to handle erosion, or accept it as an unavoidable feature of the service
In practice: whether a valve cavitates or flashes depends entirely on whether downstream pressure recovers above vapor pressure — check both against the actual downstream pressure profile, not just the nominal ΔP.
3

PSV vs PRV vs Safety Valve Terminology

Overlapping terms that get used inconsistently across industries and codes.
TermCommon MeaningWhere it's used
PSV (Pressure Safety Valve)Generic term for a spring-loaded relief device, most common in US oil & gas/process usageProcess P&IDs, API terminology
PRV (Pressure Relief Valve)Also generic; sometimes used specifically for a valve that opens proportionally to overpressure (rather than popping fully open)General industrial and plumbing/HVAC usage
Safety ValveTraditionally implies rapid, full-open ("pop") action, historically for compressible/steam serviceBoiler and steam code terminology (e.g. ASME Section I)
Relief ValveTraditionally implies proportional opening, historically for liquid serviceASME Section VIII and general liquid relief usage
In practice: the distinctions have blurred — a modern "safety relief valve" often handles both liquid and vapor service with the same basic design. Confirm which code and terminology convention a specific project or document is using rather than assuming.
4

Conventional vs Balanced-Bellows vs Pilot-Operated PSV

Three relief valve designs that handle backpressure very differently.
CriteriaConventionalBalanced BellowsPilot-Operated
Backpressure sensitivityHigh — set pressure and capacity both affected by variable/built-up backpressureLow — bellows isolates the disk from backpressure effects on set pressureLow — main valve opening is largely independent of backpressure
Best suited toLow or constant backpressure applications, simplest and cheapest designVariable backpressure (e.g. common flare header) applicationsHigh-capacity, high-pressure, or where tight seat leakage up to very near set pressure matters
Complexity/costLowestModerate — added bellows and ventHighest — separate pilot mechanism
In practice: a common flare header with multiple relief valves discharging together almost always rules out a plain conventional valve — the built-up backpressure from other valves lifting simultaneously would otherwise affect this valve's own set pressure.
5

API 520 vs API 521

Two companion standards that are often confused because they're both about relief systems.
CriteriaAPI 520API 521
ScopeSizing, selection and installation of the pressure-relieving device itselfIdentifying overpressure scenarios and sizing the required relief load for each
Typical question it answers"Given this required relief rate, what orifice size do I need?""What's the largest credible relief load this vessel could see, and from what scenario?"
SplitPart I (sizing/selection) and Part II (installation)Single document covering scenario identification and disposal system design (flare headers, knockout drums)
In practice: a real relief system design uses both together — API 521 to establish the governing scenario and required relief rate, then API 520 to size and select the device that handles it.
6

Nitrogen Blanketing vs Dilution Purging

Both use an inert gas, but for different jobs on a different timescale.
CriteriaNitrogen BlanketingDilution Purging
PurposeOngoing protection — keep a tank's vapor space continuously inert during normal operationOne-time event — reduce a specific contaminant/oxygen concentration before entry, hot work or a maintenance activity
DurationContinuous, for as long as the tank is in that serviceA defined period until the target concentration is reached, then stopped
Typical flow rate driverSized for thermal in-breathing and liquid pump-out displacementSized to reach a target dilution ratio in an acceptable time, per the perfectly-mixed dilution model
Governing referenceAPI 2000 (venting) for the breathing rates involvedConfined space entry standards and site-specific gas testing procedures
In practice: a tank taken out of blanketed service for internal inspection typically needs a dilution purge afterward — blanketing keeps the vapor space inert day-to-day, purging is what actually makes it safe to enter.
