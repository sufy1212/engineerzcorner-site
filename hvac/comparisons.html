Comparisons

Head-to-head technology and equipment choices — side by side, so the trade-off is visible at a glance
HVAC Index Guides Comparisons

1

VAV vs CAV Systems

Both deliver conditioned air to a zone — the difference is whether airflow or temperature is what varies to meet the load.
CriteriaVAV (Variable Air Volume)CAV (Constant Air Volume)
What varies with loadAirflow rate, at roughly constant supply temperatureSupply air temperature, at constant airflow
Energy efficiency at part loadHigher — fan power drops sharply as airflow reducesLower — fan runs at full airflow regardless of load
Zone-by-zone controlGood — each VAV box modulates independentlyLimited — typically one zone per air handler loop, or reheat needed per zone
First costHigher — VAV boxes, controls, and static pressure reset add costLower — simpler ductwork and controls
Typical applicationMulti-zone commercial buildings with varying, independent loadsSingle-zone spaces, small systems, or constant-load industrial spaces
VAV has become the default for multi-zone commercial buildings mainly because of the fan energy savings at part load, which dominates annual energy cost in most climates. CAV still earns its place in single-zone applications where the added VAV box cost and control complexity wouldn't buy any real benefit.
2

Chilled Water vs DX Cooling

Both cool air at a coil — the difference is whether the coil sees chilled water from a central plant or refrigerant expanding directly.
CriteriaChilled WaterDX (Direct Expansion)
Cooling medium at the coilChilled water from a central chiller plantRefrigerant, expanding directly in the coil
Central plant requiredYes — chiller, pumps, piping distributionNo — each unit is self-contained (or paired to one outdoor condenser)
Scalability across a large buildingEfficient — one plant serves many air handlersLess efficient at large scale — many separate refrigerant circuits
Zoning flexibilityGood, via multiple air handlers off one plantGood, especially with multi-split/VRF configurations
Typical applicationLarge buildings, campuses, central plantsSmaller buildings, standalone units, retail, residential
Chilled water wins on efficiency and flexibility at large scale because one central plant can be optimized and serve dozens of air handlers; DX wins on simplicity and lower first cost for smaller, standalone applications where a central plant wouldn't be economical.
3

Air-Cooled vs Water-Cooled Chillers

Both reject heat from the refrigeration cycle — the difference is what carries that heat away to the outdoors.
CriteriaAir-CooledWater-Cooled
Heat rejection mediumAmbient air, via condenser coil and fansCondenser water, via a cooling tower
EfficiencyLower, especially in hot climatesHigher — water rejects heat more effectively than air
Water useNoneRequires makeup water and treatment for the cooling tower
Space and structural needsOutdoor pad or roof space for the unitChiller room plus a cooling tower, typically on the roof
MaintenanceSimpler — no tower or condenser water treatmentMore involved — tower, water treatment, and Legionella control
Water-cooled chillers are more efficient and are the default for large commercial buildings where the cooling tower's water treatment and maintenance burden is manageable at scale. Air-cooled chillers earn their place wherever water availability, tower maintenance, or simplicity outweighs the efficiency difference — smaller buildings especially.
4

Centrifugal vs Axial Fans

Both move air — the difference is the direction air travels through the fan and what that means for pressure capability.
CriteriaCentrifugalAxial
Airflow direction through fanRadial — air turns 90° through the impellerParallel to the shaft, straight through
Pressure capabilityHigher — suited to ducted systems with real resistanceLower — suited to low-resistance, high-volume applications
Typical noise characterLower at a given duty pointCan be higher, especially at high pressure
Typical applicationAHUs, ducted supply/return systemsWall/roof exhaust fans, condenser fans, low-resistance ventilation
Physical footprintLarger for a given airflowCompact, inline with the duct or opening
Centrifugal fans dominate ducted AHU applications because real ductwork has real resistance to overcome; axial fans earn their place wherever airflow needs to move through a low-resistance path, like a straight wall opening or a condenser coil, where their compact size and simplicity are the bigger win.
5

RTU vs Split System vs VRF

Three ways to deliver packaged DX cooling — differing mainly in how centralized the equipment is and how many independent zones it can serve.
CriteriaRTU (Rooftop Unit)Split SystemVRF
ConfigurationSelf-contained packaged unit, usually on the roofSeparate indoor and outdoor units, one pair per zoneOne or few outdoor units serving many indoor units
ZoningTypically single or few zones per unitOne zone per indoor/outdoor pairMany independent zones, each with individual control
Simultaneous heating/coolingNo, typicallyNoYes, on heat-recovery VRF systems
First costLower per unitLow per zone, but adds up across many zonesHigher upfront, more efficient in operation across many zones
Typical applicationRetail, light commercial, single-story buildingsSmall spaces, single rooms, additionsMulti-zone commercial and multi-family buildings needing individual control
RTUs win on simplicity and cost for straightforward single- or few-zone buildings. VRF earns its higher upfront cost on buildings with many zones needing independent control and where simultaneous heating and cooling across zones (e.g., a shaded north room and a sunny south room on the same day) genuinely matters.
6

Fixed Plate vs Rotary Wheel Heat Recovery

Both recover energy from exhaust air before it's discarded — the difference is whether air streams stay fully separated or briefly share a medium.
CriteriaFixed PlateRotary Wheel (Enthalpy Wheel)
Airstream separationFully separated — no moving parts, no cross-contaminationBrief shared contact as the wheel rotates between streams
Moisture (latent) recoverySensible only, unless a specialized membrane plate is usedSensible and latent, standard on enthalpy wheels
Cross-contamination riskNone — a real advantage in critical or contaminated exhaust applicationsSmall but nonzero carryover between streams
Moving parts / maintenanceNoneMotor-driven wheel, requires periodic maintenance
Typical applicationLabs, hospitals, and other exhaust streams that must stay isolatedGeneral commercial ventilation wanting both heat and moisture recovery
Rotary wheels recover more total energy (sensible plus latent) and are the default where general ventilation efficiency is the goal. Fixed plate exchangers earn their place specifically where any cross-contamination between supply and exhaust air is unacceptable, even at the cost of leaving latent energy unrecovered.
7

Single-Duct vs Dual-Duct Systems

One duct that varies temperature or flow per zone, versus two ducts that let each zone mix its own blend.
CriteriaSingle-Duct (VAV/CAV)Dual-Duct
Duct runs requiredOne supply duct per zoneSeparate hot and cold supply ducts, mixed at each zone
Zone response speedGood, especially with VAVVery fast — hot/cold mixing responds almost instantly
Energy efficiencyHigher, particularly single-duct VAVLower — simultaneously producing hot and cold air wastes energy
Space for ductworkLess — one duct per zoneMore — two full-size ducts run in parallel
Typical applicationMost modern commercial buildingsOlder buildings, or spaces needing very fast, precise temperature swings
Dual-duct systems were more common before VAV controls matured, since mixing hot and cold air gives very fast, precise zone control — but that responsiveness comes at a real energy cost from conditioning air in both directions simultaneously. Single-duct VAV has displaced dual-duct in most new construction for exactly that reason.
8

Dry-Bulb vs Enthalpy Economizer Control

Both decide when outside air is "free cooling" worth using — the difference is whether humidity is part of that decision.
CriteriaDry-Bulb EconomizerEnthalpy Economizer
Decision variableOutside air temperature onlyOutside air total heat content (temperature + humidity)
Accuracy of the "free cooling" decisionCan be wrong in humid climates — cool but humid air isn't actually free coolingMore accurate — correctly excludes cool-but-humid conditions
Sensor requirementSimple temperature sensorTemperature and humidity sensors, more prone to drift/calibration error
Typical applicationDry climates, where humidity rarely confuses the temperature-only signalHumid climates, where dry-bulb-only control would use humid air incorrectly
Dry-bulb control is simpler and cheaper, and works fine in dry climates where a cool day is reliably a low-humidity day too. Enthalpy control earns its added sensor cost and calibration burden specifically in humid climates, where a cool but humid outside air condition would otherwise be mistaken for free cooling.
