Heat Pump Balance Point & Backup Heat

The outdoor temperature where heat pump output stops matching heat loss
A heat pump's heating output falls as outdoor temperature falls, while a building's heat loss rises as it gets colder — the balance point is the outdoor temperature where those two lines cross. Below it, the heat pump alone can no longer keep up and backup heat has to cover the gap. Enter the building's heat loss at design temperature and two points from the heat pump's own capacity-at-temperature table to find it.

How this is calculated
Heat loss line: Qloss(T) = Qdesign × (Tin − T) / (Tin − Tdesign)
Heat pump line: Qhp(T) = Qrated + [(Qhp,design − Qrated) / (Tdesign − Trated)] × (T − Trated)
Balance point: the outdoor temperature Tbal where Qloss(T) = Qhp(T)  ·  Backup heat = max(0, Qdesign − Qhp,design)
Both lines are treated as straight between the two given points — heat loss really is close to linear with outdoor temperature, but a heat pump's real capacity curve bends slightly, so treat the balance point as a good estimate rather than an exact figure. For a tighter answer, use two capacity points closer to your actual design temperature instead of the full rated-to-design span.

About the Heat Pump Balance Point Calculator

A heat pump's heating capacity isn't a single number — it falls as outdoor temperature drops, while the building's heat loss does the opposite and rises as it gets colder. Plot both against outdoor temperature and they cross at one point: the balance point. Above it, the heat pump alone covers the full heating load. Below it, the gap between the two lines is exactly the capacity that backup heat (a resistance strip, or a hybrid furnace) needs to supply.

This sheet builds both lines from simple two-point data — the building's heat loss at its design temperature, and the heat pump's rated capacity plus its capacity at that same design temperature, both of which should come straight from the manufacturer's performance table. Sizing off the rated/brochure capacity alone is a common mistake here, since that figure is measured at a mild outdoor condition and overstates what the unit can actually deliver on a cold day.

A balance point sitting well above the design temperature isn't necessarily a problem — it just means backup heat needs to be sized for a real, non-trivial share of the coldest days, which is normal for a lot of standard (non cold-climate) heat pump installations. What matters is that the backup capacity is actually sized to cover that gap, rather than assumed to be a small top-up.
