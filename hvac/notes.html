Notes on how HVAC systems actually work.

Psychrometrics

4 notes

Dry-bulb temperature is the plain air temperature an ordinary thermometer reads. Wet-bulb temperature is what a thermometer reads with its bulb wrapped in a wet wick and exposed to moving air — evaporation cools the wick, and how much cooling happens depends on how much moisture the surrounding air can still absorb. In dry air, evaporation is rapid and wet-bulb reads well below dry-bulb; in saturated air, no more moisture can evaporate and the two readings converge.

Dew point is the temperature at which the air's actual moisture content would just start to condense if the air were cooled with nothing added or removed. All three describe the same parcel of air from a different angle, and together — usually alongside a third property like relative humidity — they're enough to fully place that air's condition on a psychrometric chart.

Relative humidity (RH) is the ratio of moisture actually in the air to the maximum it could hold at that specific temperature, expressed as a percentage. Because warmer air can hold more moisture, RH changes automatically whenever temperature changes, even if the actual water content of the air hasn't moved at all — heating a parcel of air lowers its RH without adding or removing a single gram of water.

Humidity ratio (also called moisture content, typically in grams of water per kilogram of dry air) instead measures the actual mass of water vapor mixed into the air, independent of temperature. It only changes when moisture is genuinely added or removed — by a humidifier, a cooling coil condensing water out, or fresh air mixing in — which is exactly why load calculations and coil selection work from humidity ratio rather than from RH.

Sensible Heat Ratio is the fraction of a cooling coil's total capacity that goes toward sensible cooling, calculated as sensible load divided by total (sensible plus latent) load. An SHR near 1.0 means the load is almost entirely sensible — the coil is mostly dropping temperature with little moisture removal. A lower SHR, more common in humid climates or high-occupancy spaces like gyms and restaurants, means a larger share of the coil's work is condensing moisture out of the air.

On a psychrometric chart, SHR is literally the slope of the process line from entering to leaving air condition — protractor scales printed on most chart editions let you set that slope directly. Selecting equipment against the wrong SHR is a common source of comfort complaints: a coil sized for a high-SHR (dry) load but installed in a low-SHR (humid) space will hit its sensible target and cycle off before it's removed enough moisture, leaving the space cool but clammy.

A psychrometric chart plots dry-bulb temperature along the bottom and humidity ratio up the side, with curved lines of constant relative humidity sweeping across it — the topmost curve, at 100% RH, is the saturation line, where air is holding all the moisture it physically can at that temperature. Any point on the chart fully describes one state of moist air, and reading across from that point to the chart's edges gives properties like wet-bulb temperature, dew point and enthalpy without further calculation.

The real value of the chart is following processes as straight lines across it: heating a coil moves a point horizontally right at constant moisture content, cooling and dehumidifying moves it down and to the left as the air's path crosses the saturation curve, and mixing two airstreams lands on the straight line connecting their two starting points. Most HVAC equipment selection — a cooling coil's split between sensible and latent removal, for instance — is really just tracing one of these paths on the chart.

Air Distribution & Ductwork

3 notes

Static pressure is the outward push against a duct's walls, the pressure that drives air through fittings, filters and coils and is lost to friction along the way. Velocity pressure is the pressure associated with the air actually moving at a given speed, and it converts back and forth with static pressure as a duct narrows or widens — a smaller duct trades some static pressure for higher velocity pressure, which is why a poorly transitioned duct can lose more pressure at a fitting than a long straight run.

Total pressure is simply the sum of the two, and it's total pressure that steadily falls in the direction of airflow as friction and fittings consume it — a fan exists specifically to add total pressure back into the system. A fan is selected against the total static pressure the whole duct run, its fittings, and any equipment in the airstream (coils, filters, dampers) adds up to at the design airflow, not against any single component in isolation.

Sizing a duct sets its cross-section for an acceptable velocity and pressure drop, but it doesn't by itself guarantee any particular branch gets the airflow it was designed for — air, like any fluid, takes the path of least resistance, and branches closer to the fan or with straighter runs tend to get more flow than their design share unless something forces the split. That's the job of balancing: adjusting dampers throughout the system, branch by branch, until measured airflow at each outlet matches the design intent.

Balancing is normally done after installation with a flow hood or pitot-tube traverse, working from the index run (the branch with the least available pressure) outward, since throttling a damper only ever adds resistance — it can bring an over-supplied branch down to match the worst one, but it can never push more air through a branch than the fan and duct system can deliver to it.

A diffuser or grille's job isn't just to let air out — its geometry shapes how that air spreads into the room. Throw is the distance a jet of supply air travels before its velocity drops to a specified low value (often around 15 to 20 metres per minute), and it has to be matched to the room: too short a throw and air dumps straight down near the outlet, too long and it slams into a far wall or an occupant before it's mixed with room air and lost its chill or draft.

Diffuser selection is really a balance between throw, spread pattern, noise (which rises sharply with outlet velocity) and the pressure drop the diffuser itself adds to the system — a diffuser sized purely for airflow without checking throw and noise can deliver the correct volume of air while still leaving the room drafty, stuffy in the corners, or audibly noisy.

Heating & Cooling Equipment

4 notes

A vapor-compression chiller runs the same refrigeration cycle as any air conditioner, just scaled up and arranged to cool a stream of water (or a water-glycol mix) instead of air directly — an electrically driven compressor raises refrigerant pressure, it rejects heat at the condenser, expands, and absorbs heat from the chilled-water loop at the evaporator. That chilled water is then piped out to cooling coils in air handling units throughout a building, which is what lets one central chiller plant serve many separate air-side systems.

An absorption chiller reaches the same end result — chilled water out — through an entirely different mechanism: rather than a mechanical compressor, it uses a heat source (steam, hot water, or a gas burner) together with a chemical absorbent pair (commonly lithium bromide and water) to drive the refrigeration cycle. Absorption chillers trade higher capital cost and lower efficiency for the ability to run on waste heat or cheap thermal energy instead of electricity, which is why they show up mainly where a large, otherwise-wasted heat source is already available on site.

An air handling unit (AHU) is a casing that houses, in sequence, the components needed to condition and move air: a mixing section that blends return air with outside air, filters to protect the coils and improve indoor air quality, a cooling coil and often a separate heating coil, and a supply fan to push the conditioned air out into the ductwork. Some units add a heat-recovery section, humidifier, or sound attenuator depending on the application.

Air moves through each section in a fixed order, and that order matters: filters are placed upstream of coils specifically to keep the coil's fins clean (a fouled coil loses both airflow and heat-transfer capacity), and the fan is placed downstream of the coils in a typical draw-through arrangement so it pulls air evenly across the coil face rather than blowing unevenly into it. Rooftop units and packaged units bundle the same basic sections — plus, often, the refrigeration equipment itself — into a single outdoor-rated enclosure.

A variable refrigerant flow (VRF) system connects one or a few outdoor condensing units directly to many indoor fan-coil units through refrigerant piping, rather than distributing chilled water or conditioned air through ducts from a central plant. Each indoor unit has its own electronic expansion valve, letting the system deliver a different amount of cooling or heating to each zone independently by metering how much refrigerant flows to that unit at any moment.

Because refrigerant carries far more heat per unit of pipe cross-section than air or even water, VRF piping runs are far smaller than equivalent ductwork, which is a major reason VRF is popular in renovations and buildings with limited space for ductwork. Heat-recovery VRF variants take this further, letting some indoor units heat while others simultaneously cool, moving the rejected heat from the cooling zones directly to the heating zones instead of discarding it outdoors.

A hydronic heating system uses a boiler to heat water (or a water-glycol mix), then circulates that hot water through piping to terminal units — radiators, baseboard convectors, fan-coil units, or embedded radiant floor loops — that release the heat into each space. Because water holds vastly more heat per unit volume than air for a given temperature rise, hydronic distribution piping can be far smaller than the ductwork an equivalent air-based system would need, at the cost of needing pumps, piping, and freeze protection instead of fans and ducts.

Boilers themselves range from simple atmospheric gas-fired units to high-efficiency condensing boilers, which recover additional heat by cooling flue gases enough to condense the water vapor in them — a trick that only works if the return water is cool enough to accept that extra heat, which is why condensing boiler efficiency depends heavily on the temperatures the rest of the hydronic system is designed around.

Controls & BMS

3 notes

An open-loop control simply applies a fixed action without checking the result — running a fan at a set speed on a timer regardless of the temperature it's actually achieving is open-loop control. It's simple and cheap, but it can't correct itself if conditions change: a fixed heating output that was right on a mild day will overheat a room on a milder one and underheat it on a colder one.

A closed-loop (feedback) control instead measures the actual result — room temperature, for instance — and continuously adjusts its output to close the gap between that measurement and a setpoint. Nearly all HVAC comfort control is closed-loop for exactly this reason: a thermostat doesn't just turn heating on for a fixed time, it watches the measured temperature and keeps adjusting until the space actually reaches and holds the setpoint, compensating automatically for weather, occupancy, and solar gain that an open-loop system would have no way to detect.

A building management system (BMS, sometimes BAS) ties together the individual control loops running throughout a building's mechanical systems — AHU discharge temperature, chiller staging, VAV box airflow, boiler sequencing — into one monitored, schedulable, and centrally adjustable system. Each piece of equipment typically still has its own local controller doing the fast, moment-to-moment control loop; the BMS sits above that layer, setting schedules and setpoints, watching for alarms, and letting operators see and adjust the whole building from one interface instead of walking to each piece of equipment individually.

Beyond convenience, a BMS is also where genuine energy savings get captured at the building level — night setback schedules, demand-based ventilation, chiller sequencing that runs the most efficient combination of equipment for the current load, and trend logging that lets an operator spot equipment running longer or harder than it should, often long before anyone would otherwise notice a problem.

An economizer is a control strategy — usually a set of dampers and a controller — that brings in extra outside air to cool a building whenever outdoor conditions are favorable enough to reduce or eliminate the need for mechanical cooling. Instead of drawing the usual minimum ventilation air and recirculating the rest, the dampers open further, sometimes to 100% outside air, whenever doing so is cheaper than running the chiller or compressor to achieve the same effect.

A dry-bulb economizer switches on this extra outside air purely based on outdoor temperature; an enthalpy economizer instead compares the total heat content (temperature and humidity together) of outside air against return air, since cool but very humid outside air can actually take more energy to condition than warmer, drier return air. Economizers are one of the most effective single energy-saving strategies for buildings in mild climates, precisely because they let ordinary weather do work the mechanical cooling system would otherwise have to do at a real energy cost.

Ventilation & Indoor Air Quality

4 notes

Air changes per hour (ACH) is the ratio of a space's supply (or extract) airflow to its volume, expressed as how many times that volume would be fully replaced in one hour if the air mixed perfectly. It's a convenient shorthand for comparing ventilation intensity across very different rooms, and it's the number most codes and design guides set minimums against — offices around 4–6 ACH, classrooms 6–8, bathrooms and kitchens 10–15, operating theatres 20–25.

The catch is the 'perfectly mixed' assumption baked into the formula. A room can hit its target ACH on paper while air short-circuits from a supply diffuser straight to a nearby return grille, barely sweeping the occupied zone, or while corners and areas behind furniture sit well below the room average. ACH is a sizing and code-compliance metric, not a guarantee of air quality at any specific point — diffuser placement, throw pattern, and return location decide whether the nominal ACH actually reaches people.

Total supply airflow to a room and the fresh outside-air component of that airflow measure two different things. A space can have plenty of total air moving through it — enough for comfortable temperature control — while still recirculating the same stale, contaminant-laden air over and over if very little of that supply is actually fresh outside air. Ventilation standards like ASHRAE 62.1 therefore specify minimum outside-air rates directly, not just total airflow, because it's the outside-air fraction that actually dilutes CO2, odors, and other indoor-generated contaminants.

This is also why demand-controlled ventilation, which uses CO2 sensors to modulate outside-air intake based on actual occupancy, works: CO2 concentration is used as a practical proxy for how well a space's ventilation is currently diluting occupant-generated contaminants, letting a system bring in more outside air only when it's actually needed rather than running a fixed rate regardless of how many people are in the room.

MERV (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value) rates air filters by how effectively they capture particles across a range of sizes, with the rating driven specifically by performance in the hardest-to-catch size range the standard tests — particles too large to be dodged easily by airflow, but too small to be reliably intercepted by a filter's fibers, roughly in the range of 0.3 to 3 microns. A higher MERV rating means better capture efficiency in that range, at the cost of higher airflow resistance, which is why swapping to a much higher-MERV filter without checking the fan and system can starve airflow or overload the fan motor.

MERV isn't the only filtration scale in use — HEPA filters, rated by a different standard entirely, capture an even higher percentage of very fine particles and are used where that level of filtration is specifically required, such as certain healthcare or cleanroom spaces — but for typical commercial and residential HVAC filtration, MERV is the number most equipment, ductwork, and design guidance is built around.

A building is positively pressurized when slightly more air is supplied to it than is exhausted or otherwise leaves, so the small excess pushes outward through any gaps, door openings, and intentional relief paths — this is generally the goal for most commercial buildings, since it keeps unfiltered, unconditioned outside air (and along with it, dust, humidity, and insects) from being drawn in uncontrolled through the envelope. Negative pressure is the reverse: more air leaves than is supplied, so outside air gets pulled in through any available opening.

Negative pressure is sometimes deliberately created and useful — an isolation room or a kitchen with heavy exhaust needs negative pressure specifically to keep odors, smoke, or contaminants from migrating out into surrounding spaces — but an unintentionally negative building, from an exhaust system that isn't matched by enough outside-air makeup, commonly shows up as doors that are hard to open, whistling gaps, and uncontrolled infiltration that both wastes energy and undermines filtration and humidity control.

Energy & Efficiency

3 notes

COP (coefficient of performance) is the most fundamental of the three: the ratio of useful heating or cooling output to the energy input required to produce it, measured at one specific set of operating conditions. EER (energy efficiency ratio) is a similar ratio — cooling output in Btu/h divided by power input in watts — but measured under a single standardized full-load test condition, making it a snapshot of performance at one operating point rather than across a season.

SEER (seasonal energy efficiency ratio) instead averages performance across a full range of outdoor temperatures and part-load conditions meant to represent a typical cooling season, which is why a unit's SEER is usually noticeably higher than its EER at peak conditions — SEER credits the efficiency gains a modern variable-speed unit achieves at partial load, which a single full-load EER test can't capture at all. Comparing equipment on the wrong metric, or comparing a SEER rating directly against a COP figure, produces a misleading picture of efficiency even when both numbers are individually accurate.

A fan or pump's power draw scales roughly with the cube of its speed (or flow) for a fixed system — halving the speed cuts required power to roughly an eighth of its full-speed value, not half. A variable-speed drive (VSD or VFD) that lets a fan slow down to match actual demand, instead of running at full speed and throttling with a damper or valve, therefore captures energy savings that grow disproportionately fast as load drops below full design conditions.

This cube-law relationship is also why VSDs pay back fastest on systems that spend most of their operating hours at partial load — a fan sized for a rare peak condition but usually running at 60–70% of that peak sees the largest share of its runtime in the steepest part of the power-versus-speed curve, which is exactly where a VSD's savings are largest relative to a fixed-speed, damper-throttled alternative.

Every unit of outside air brought in for ventilation has to be heated or cooled to match the space it's entering, which is a real and often substantial energy cost, especially in climates with large gaps between outdoor and indoor conditions. A heat (or energy) recovery ventilator places the incoming outside airstream and the outgoing exhaust airstream on either side of a heat exchanger, letting the exhaust air — which is already at or near room condition — pre-condition the incoming outside air before it ever reaches the main heating or cooling coil.

Sensible heat recovery exchangers transfer temperature only; energy recovery (enthalpy) exchangers transfer both temperature and moisture between the two airstreams, which is particularly valuable in humid climates where dehumidifying incoming outside air is a large part of the total cooling load. Either way, the exhaust air that would otherwise simply be thrown away is instead put to work reducing the load the rest of the system has to handle.

Standards & Codes

2 notes

ASHRAE Standard 62.1, Ventilation for Acceptable Indoor Air Quality, sets minimum outside-air ventilation rates for commercial and institutional buildings, typically expressed as a combination of a rate per person and a rate per unit floor area, varying by space type — an office, a classroom, and a gym have different assumed occupant densities and different pollutant sources, so the standard sets different minimums for each. It also defines the Ventilation Rate Procedure, the standard's prescriptive method for calculating minimum outside-air requirements for a given space and occupancy.

Many national and local mechanical codes adopt ASHRAE 62.1's requirements directly or with local amendments, which is why it functions as a de facto minimum ventilation baseline across much of the design industry even where it isn't the literal legally adopted code text — a design that fails to meet 62.1 rates without justification is, in practice, treated as under-ventilated by almost any reviewing authority.

ASHRAE Standard 90.1, Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential Buildings, sets minimum energy-efficiency requirements covering a building's envelope, lighting, and mechanical systems together — insulation levels, equipment efficiency minimums, duct and pipe insulation, and control requirements like economizers and demand-controlled ventilation above certain thresholds all trace back to provisions in this standard.

Like 62.1, many jurisdictions' energy codes are built directly on top of 90.1, either adopting it wholesale or amending specific sections to be more stringent for local climate or policy reasons, which makes 90.1 the practical reference point for what counts as a minimally code-compliant building's energy performance, even in places where the locally enacted code carries a different name.

Safety

3 notes

Refrigerants are classified under a two-character system: a letter for toxicity and a number for flammability. Class A refrigerants show low toxicity at normal exposure levels; Class B refrigerants show evidence of toxicity at lower concentrations, requiring more caution around leaks and confined spaces. On the flammability side, Class 1 refrigerants don't propagate flame at all under standard test conditions, Class 2 (and the lower-flammability subclass 2L) ignite but burn relatively slowly with a lower heat of combustion, and Class 3 refrigerants are highly flammable.

A refrigerant labeled A1, for instance, is both low-toxicity and non-flammable, while an A2L refrigerant — increasingly common as the industry shifts toward lower-global-warming-potential refrigerants — is low-toxicity but mildly flammable, which brings additional requirements around leak detection, ventilation, and equipment design that A1 refrigerants didn't need. Knowing a refrigerant's classification is the starting point for the ventilation, quantity limits, and equipment room requirements that apply to the system using it.

Cooling towers reject heat by evaporating a portion of their circulating water into the atmosphere, and in doing so they generate a fine mist — exactly the mechanism by which Legionella bacteria, if present in the water, can become airborne and be inhaled, potentially causing Legionnaires' disease. The warm water temperatures typical of a cooling tower's operating range sit squarely within the range where Legionella multiplies most readily, which is what makes towers a recognized risk point rather than an unusual one.

Managing that risk centers on water treatment (biocides and regular disinfection), keeping the tower and its basin clean of the scale and biofilm that shelter bacteria from treatment, and maintaining a documented water management plan with regular testing — particularly after any period the tower has sat idle, since stagnant water significantly raises the risk before the system is brought back into active service.

A confined space is broadly defined as an area large enough to enter and work in, with limited entry or exit, that isn't designed for continuous occupancy — some HVAC equipment rooms, pits, tanks, and duct interiors meet this definition, and a subset are further classified as permit-required confined spaces if they can contain a hazardous atmosphere, engulfment risk, or other serious hazard. Refrigerant leaks are a specific and relevant risk in enclosed mechanical spaces: many refrigerants are heavier than air and can displace oxygen in a confined area without any obvious smell or visible sign.

Entering a permit-required confined space calls for atmospheric testing before entry, continuous monitoring, ventilation, and a trained attendant outside the space at minimum — treating every mechanical room the same as an open, freely ventilated workspace is exactly the assumption that confined-space procedures exist to override, since the hazard often isn't obvious from a doorway glance.

Hydronic Systems & Piping

7 notes

Water has a far higher heat capacity per unit volume than air, so moving a given amount of heat through a hydronic pipe takes a fraction of the volumetric flow that moving the same heat through ductwork would require — a hydronic pipe a few centimetres across can carry as much heating or cooling capacity as ductwork many times its cross-section. That difference is why hydronic distribution is common in taller buildings and long runs, where routing large ductwork everywhere the air-based equivalent would require becomes impractical.

The trade-off is that water still ultimately has to exchange its heat with air somewhere — at a coil, a radiator, a fan-coil unit — for it to actually condition a space, so a hydronic system is really only the distribution piece of the puzzle, not a replacement for air-side heat exchange entirely. Most large hydronic-served buildings still have air-handling equipment; the hydronic piping just moves the primary heating or cooling capacity efficiently from central plant to that equipment.

Chillers and boilers are typically designed around a fairly narrow acceptable flow range through their heat exchangers — too little flow risks freezing (chillers) or overheating (boilers) at the heat exchanger surface, and too much flow wastes pumping energy without adding benefit. The building's actual heating or cooling demand, though, varies constantly through the day, which would otherwise force the plant's flow to vary right along with it if there were only a single pumping loop.

Primary-secondary (or primary-secondary-tertiary) pumping decouples these two requirements with a hydraulic connection — a short common pipe section, or a low-loss header — between a primary loop that keeps constant, plant-appropriate flow through the chillers or boilers, and a secondary loop whose flow can vary freely to match building load without ever forcing the primary loop's flow to change with it. This separation is exactly what lets variable-flow building distribution coexist with equipment that needs comparatively stable flow through itself.

Delta-T is the temperature difference between a hydronic loop's supply and return water, and it's directly tied to how much heat a given flow rate is actually carrying — for a fixed flow, a bigger delta-T means more heat moved per unit of pumping energy spent. Design delta-T (commonly around 10°F/5.5°C for chilled water systems, though it varies by design) is chosen specifically to balance pipe sizing, pump energy, and coil performance.

In practice, delta-T often runs lower than design — a condition often called 'low delta-T syndrome' — usually caused by coils, control valves, or three-way valves that let water bypass without picking up its full design temperature change. Low delta-T forces a system to pump more flow than intended to deliver the same actual cooling or heating capacity, which quietly increases pump energy and can even limit how much total capacity the plant can deliver even though its equipment is nominally sized adequately.

Water expands as it's heated, and a hydronic loop is a closed, essentially rigid, completely filled system — without somewhere for that expansion volume to go, heating the water would rapidly build enormous pressure with nowhere to relieve it, well beyond what the piping and equipment could safely contain. An expansion tank solves this by providing a flexible or air-cushioned volume connected to the loop that absorbs the water's expansion and contraction as system temperature changes through its operating range.

A properly sized expansion tank is sized against the specific volume of water in the system and the full temperature range that water will actually see, not an arbitrary standard size — undersizing it defeats its purpose just as surely as omitting it entirely, since it would simply fill its available capacity and then offer no further relief once the system's temperature swing exceeds what the tank was actually sized to absorb.

Air introduced into a hydronic system during initial fill, or released from water as it heats (dissolved gases become less soluble at higher temperature), tends to collect at high points in the piping and inside heat exchangers and coils, where it displaces water and interferes directly with heat transfer at exactly the surfaces that are supposed to be transferring it. Trapped air pockets can also cause pump cavitation-like noise and vibration, and contribute to internal corrosion since oxygen is a key ingredient in the corrosion process.

This is why hydronic systems include air separation and venting provisions — automatic air vents at high points, and often a dedicated air separator near the point of lowest pressure in the loop where dissolved gases are most likely to come out of solution — rather than simply relying on a one-time fill and bleed to remove all the air a system will ever generate. Air management is treated as an ongoing system function, not a one-time commissioning step.

A fan-coil unit combines a simple fan with a hydronic coil (chilled water, hot water, or both) to condition air locally at or near the space it serves, drawing its heating or cooling capacity from the building's central hydronic distribution rather than generating it locally itself. This lets a central plant serve many separate zones, each with its own small fan-coil and independent temperature control, without needing to duct conditioned air from a central air handler to every zone individually.

Because each fan-coil is essentially an independent small heat exchanger drawing from the same shared hydronic loop, proper valve control and balancing at each unit matters for the same reason it matters at the whole-building level — an unbalanced or poorly controlled fan-coil can draw more than its fair share of flow from the loop, at the expense of other zones served by the same distribution system.

Glycol (ethylene or propylene) is added to hydronic system water specifically to lower its freezing point, protecting piping and equipment exposed to freezing temperatures — rooftop equipment, piping in unheated spaces, or systems in cold climates that could otherwise freeze and rupture during a shutdown or cold snap. The concentration used is chosen to protect against the coldest temperature the system could realistically be exposed to, not simply added at an arbitrary strength.

Glycol solutions have real trade-offs compared to plain water, though: reduced heat capacity and higher viscosity both mean a glycol solution transfers heat somewhat less effectively and needs slightly more pumping energy to move the same flow, and pumps, coils and control valves sized purely for plain water can underperform if glycol is added afterward without re-checking their selection. This is why glycol percentage is a deliberate design input from the start in systems that need it, not an afterthought added once freeze risk is noticed.

Fans, Motors & Air Moving Equipment

7 notes

A centrifugal fan draws air in along its axis and throws it out radially using a spinning wheel of blades, a geometry that's well suited to generating higher static pressure, which makes it the standard choice for ducted HVAC systems that have to push air through significant duct, filter and coil resistance. An axial fan instead moves air straight through in the same direction as its shaft, using propeller-like blades, and it's generally better suited to moving large volumes of air against comparatively low resistance, which is why axial fans show up in applications like wall-mounted exhaust fans or cooling tower fans rather than deep inside a ducted air handler.

Choosing between them isn't really a matter of one being universally better — it comes down to matching the fan's characteristic strength to the system's actual resistance curve, since a fan well suited to low-resistance, high-volume applications can perform poorly if forced into a high-resistance ducted system, and vice versa.

The fan laws describe how a fan's airflow, pressure and power draw each change with rotational speed: airflow varies directly with speed, pressure varies with the square of speed, and power varies with the cube of speed. That cubic relationship for power is the practically important one — reducing a fan's speed by 20% doesn't reduce its power draw by 20%, it reduces it by roughly 50%, since 0.8 cubed is about 0.51.

This is the underlying reason variable-speed fan control (via a VFD) is such an effective energy-saving measure in HVAC systems with varying load: even modest reductions in required airflow during partial-load conditions translate into disproportionately large reductions in fan energy, far more than a linear relationship would suggest — which is also why oversizing a fan and then throttling it with a damper wastes far more energy than right-sizing the fan or slowing it down to begin with.

A conventional variable-air-volume system controls its supply fan to maintain a fixed duct static pressure setpoint, sized conservatively enough to satisfy the system's worst-case zone under worst-case conditions — but that fixed setpoint is unnecessarily high most of the time, since worst-case conditions aren't the norm. Static pressure reset instead continuously lowers the fan's static pressure setpoint until at least one VAV box damper is nearly fully open, meaning the fan is doing the least work that still satisfies every zone's actual current demand.

Because fan power scales with roughly the cube of speed, even a modest reduction in static pressure setpoint under typical partial-load conditions produces a meaningful fan energy saving across the many hours a system doesn't need its full worst-case pressure — static pressure reset is one of the more consistently effective control-only (no equipment change) energy measures available on VAV systems that don't already use it.

A direct-drive fan has its wheel mounted straight onto the motor shaft, with no belt or pulley in between, which eliminates belt slippage and belt-related maintenance entirely and is generally more energy efficient since there's no belt drive loss. The trade-off is less flexibility in matching fan speed to airflow requirement, since fan speed is tied directly to motor speed unless a VFD is added.

A belt-driven fan uses a belt and pulley system between motor and fan wheel, which allows fan speed to be adjusted independently of motor speed simply by changing pulley sizes, and lets a single standard motor speed serve fans across a range of different speed requirements. The belt itself is a real maintenance item, though — belts stretch, wear, and occasionally slip or break, and belt tension has to be periodically checked and adjusted, which is exactly the ongoing maintenance direct drive eliminates.

A fan curve, published by the manufacturer, plots the airflow a fan delivers against the static pressure it has to work against, tested under controlled laboratory conditions with clean, uniform airflow approaching and leaving the fan. Where a system's actual resistance curve crosses that fan curve is the fan's real operating point — which is why fan selection is really a matter of matching a fan curve against a system curve, not simply picking a fan rated for a target airflow in isolation.

System effect is the often-underestimated performance penalty that occurs when actual installation conditions — a duct elbow too close to the fan inlet, an obstructed outlet, non-uniform approach flow — disrupt the clean airflow the fan curve assumes, effectively reducing the fan's real-world performance below what its published curve promises. A fan that's correctly selected on paper can still underperform in the field if system effect at its actual installation isn't accounted for during design, which is exactly why fan manufacturers publish system effect correction factors for common installation configurations.

Fan efficiency describes how much of the electrical power driving a fan actually converts into useful airflow work, versus how much is lost to aerodynamic inefficiency within the fan itself — and this efficiency varies meaningfully between fan types, wheel designs, and even between different models of the same basic type. Two fans that both satisfy a project's required airflow and pressure can still differ substantially in how much energy they consume delivering that same performance.

This is why fan efficiency grade (FEG) or similar efficiency metrics are increasingly specified as a selection criterion in their own right, not just airflow and pressure capability — selecting purely on the basis of meeting airflow and pressure while ignoring efficiency can lock a project into a fan that costs meaningfully more to operate over its service life than a more efficient alternative that would have met the same airflow and pressure requirement equally well.

Every fan has a stable operating range on its performance curve, and operating too far into a region of high resistance relative to its design point can push a fan into stall — a condition where airflow separates from the fan blades in an unstable way, producing reduced and unpredictable airflow, increased noise and vibration, rather than the smooth performance the fan curve would otherwise suggest. Surge is a related, often more severe instability, particularly associated with certain fan types operating at very low flow against high pressure, where airflow can briefly reverse and oscillate rather than staying steady in one direction.

These conditions are why fan selection deliberately avoids operating points too close to the unstable region of a fan's curve, even if that point technically meets the required airflow and pressure on paper — a fan chosen to sit safely within its stable range, with reasonable margin, avoids the noise, vibration, and reduced reliability that operating near or in stall or surge would otherwise introduce over the fan's service life.
