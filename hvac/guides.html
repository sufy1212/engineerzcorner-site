How to Perform a Cooling Load Calculation

Every heat source in the space adds up separately — miss one category and the whole estimate reads low.
A cooling load is the sum of several independent heat gain categories. Skipping one doesn't just shrink the total a little — it can leave a system meaningfully undersized on the specific days it matters most.
  1. Solar gain through glazing. Depends on glass area, orientation, shading, and solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) — a west-facing window in the afternoon contributes far more than the same area facing north.
  2. Conduction through the envelope. Walls, roof and glazing all conduct heat in proportion to their U-value, area, and the indoor/outdoor temperature difference.
  3. Internal gains. People (sensible + latent per person, varies with activity level), lighting (W/m² × usage factor), and equipment — often the largest single category in an office or data-heavy space.
  4. Ventilation and infiltration load. Outside air brought in deliberately (ventilation) or leaking in unintentionally (infiltration) has to be cooled and dehumidified from outdoor to indoor conditions.
Keep sensible and latent gains separate all the way through the calculation — a coil selected against total load alone can end up oversized on sensible capacity and undersized on dehumidification, leaving a space that's cold but clammy. See the psychrometrics guide below for why that split matters.
2

How to Perform a Heating Load Calculation

Simpler than cooling in one way — no solar or latent gain to track — but the design condition you pick matters just as much.
  1. Envelope conduction loss. Same U-value × area × ΔT approach as cooling, but now heat is flowing out, so internal gains (people, lights, equipment) work in your favor and are normally excluded from the peak heating estimate rather than added — a heating load is deliberately a worst-case, no-internal-gain scenario.
  2. Ventilation and infiltration loss. Outside air has to be warmed from the winter design temperature up to room condition; infiltration through envelope gaps behaves the same way.
  3. Design outdoor temperature. Pick a winter design condition from local climate data (commonly a 99% or 99.6% annual percentile, not the coldest recorded temperature ever) — sizing against the absolute record low wastes capacity for the other 8,750+ hours of the year.
Heating loads are usually smaller and simpler to calculate than cooling loads for the same space, but don't skip them for buildings with large glazing areas or high ventilation rates — a heavily glazed space can have a heating load that rivals its cooling load.

How to Size Ductwork

Two competing goals — enough cross-section to keep velocity (and noise) down, but not so much that the system becomes unbuildable or expensive.
Friction loss (Darcy-Weisbach, simplified for duct): ΔP = f · (L/D) · (ρV²/2)
  1. Start from required airflow (CFM or L/s) — set by the load calculation and the supply air temperature difference you've chosen.
  2. Pick a target velocity appropriate to the duct's location: lower in occupied, noise-sensitive spaces (roughly 5-8 m/s in main ducts near diffusers), higher acceptable in mechanical rooms and main risers away from occupants.
  3. Size cross-section from airflow ÷ velocity, then check the resulting friction loss per 100ft/30m isn't excessive — most designers target a consistent friction rate (equal-friction method) across the whole system rather than a fixed velocity everywhere.
  4. Add fitting losses — elbows, transitions, and branches all add pressure drop beyond the straight-duct friction, usually accounted for as an equivalent length or a local loss coefficient per fitting.
The equal-friction method (same Pa/m or in.wg/100ft throughout) is the most common hand-sizing approach because it self-balances reasonably well without extra dampering — but always verify the fan can actually deliver the total system pressure drop once every section and fitting is added up along the longest run (the "index run").

How to Select an AHU

An air handling unit is really a bundle of separate selections — filter, coil, fan and casing each sized to the same airflow.
  1. Airflow (CFM/L/s). Set by the load calculation and the number of air changes or the supply air ΔT chosen for the space served.
  2. Cooling/heating coil capacity. Sized against the peak sensible + latent load, with entering/leaving air conditions and chilled/hot water (or refrigerant) supply temperatures specified to the coil manufacturer.
  3. Fan static pressure. Total of duct friction, fitting losses, filter pressure drop (dirty-filter condition, not clean), and coil pressure drop — undersizing this is one of the most common AHU selection mistakes, since filter and coil drop are easy to forget.
  4. Filtration. MERV/ISO rating appropriate to the space's air quality requirement, balancing filtration efficiency against the added pressure drop (and therefore fan energy) a finer filter demands.
Always size the fan against dirty-filter pressure drop, not clean — a system that only just meets its design airflow with a brand-new filter will fall short well before the filter is due for replacement.

How to Size a Chiller

Peak building cooling load plus a margin — but the margin has real cost and efficiency consequences if it's too generous.
  1. Sum peak cooling loads across all zones served, accounting for diversity (not every zone peaks at the same hour) rather than simply adding each zone's individual peak.
  2. Add a safety margin — commonly 10-15% for future growth and load estimate uncertainty, applied deliberately rather than stacked informally at every step of the calculation (over-margining at each stage compounds into serious oversizing).
  3. Check part-load efficiency, not just peak capacity. A chiller spends the vast majority of its operating hours well below peak load — IPLV/NPLV ratings reflect part-load performance and matter more to annual energy cost than the peak-condition efficiency number alone.
  4. Consider multiple smaller chillers vs one large one. Splitting capacity across two or more chillers improves part-load efficiency and gives redundancy, at the cost of more equipment, piping and controls complexity.
Oversizing is the most common chiller sizing mistake — a chiller that's too large short-cycles at low load, which is hard on compressors and wastes energy. Resist stacking margin on margin (load estimate margin + diversity margin + growth margin + "just to be safe" margin) — pick one deliberate total margin and apply it once.

Understanding Psychrometrics

The chart that ties together temperature, humidity, and the sensible/latent split every cooling calculation depends on.
  1. Sensible heat changes air temperature without changing its moisture content — the heat you'd feel as a temperature change on a thermometer.
  2. Latent heat changes moisture content (humidity) without necessarily changing dry-bulb temperature — condensing water vapor out of the air at a cooling coil is a latent process.
  3. Sensible heat ratio (SHR) is the fraction of total load that's sensible — a space with a lot of people or high ventilation rates has a lower SHR (more latent) than a space dominated by equipment and solar gain (mostly sensible).
  4. Coil selection depends on SHR. A coil selected only against total load, ignoring the sensible/latent split, can dehumidify too little (if SHR was overestimated) or overcool the space chasing humidity control (if SHR was underestimated).
A psychrometric chart plots dry-bulb temperature against humidity ratio, with curves for relative humidity, wet-bulb temperature, and enthalpy — every point on it represents one moist-air condition, and cooling/heating/humidifying/dehumidifying processes trace a specific path across it. Worth learning to read directly rather than only trusting the numbers a load calculation spits out.

How to Calculate Duct Insulation Thickness

Two separate reasons to insulate a duct — energy loss and condensation — and they don't always need the same thickness.
  1. Thermal (energy) requirement. Set by the temperature difference between the duct's contents and its surroundings, the insulation's R-value/thermal conductivity, and an acceptable heat gain/loss target — usually driven by an energy code minimum R-value for the duct location (conditioned space, unconditioned attic, outdoors).
  2. Condensation control. On cold supply air ducts running through warm, humid spaces, insulation also has to keep the duct's outer surface above the surrounding air's dew point — otherwise moisture condenses on the duct jacket. This can require more thickness (or a vapor barrier) than the thermal-only calculation alone would suggest.
  3. Check both, use the larger. In humid climates or tightly enclosed ceiling spaces, the condensation-control thickness often governs over the pure energy-loss calculation.
A continuous, sealed vapor barrier matters as much as insulation thickness for condensation control — insulation with gaps or a punctured vapor barrier can still sweat even if the R-value on paper looks adequate.

How to Select and Balance a VAV Box

Sized on airflow range, not just a single design point — a VAV box has to work correctly across its whole modulation range.
  1. Determine minimum and maximum airflow for the zone — maximum from the peak cooling load, minimum from ventilation requirements or a code-minimum turndown ratio (commonly 30-50% of maximum), whichever is higher.
  2. Select box size so the required maximum airflow falls within the manufacturer's acceptable velocity/pressure-drop range for that box size — an oversized box can be noisy and inaccurate at low flow; an undersized one chokes at design airflow.
  3. Check minimum static pressure requirement at the box against what the AHU fan and duct system can actually deliver at that point in the system — VAV boxes need a minimum inlet pressure to control accurately.
  4. Balance and commission by verifying actual delivered airflow against the box's design minimum and maximum with a calibrated flow hood or the box's built-in flow sensor, not just by trusting the nameplate rating.
A VAV system's overall pressure balancing happens in a loop with the AHU fan — as boxes throttle down at part load, static pressure downstream tends to rise, and fan control (usually static pressure reset) has to respond, or the whole system can become noisy and unstable at partial load.

How to Balance an Air Distribution System

Design airflow on paper and actual airflow after installation are rarely the same without deliberate balancing.
  1. Measure before adjusting. Record actual airflow at every outlet with a calibrated instrument (flow hood, pitot traverse, or anemometer with correction factor) before touching any dampers — adjusting blind almost always makes the overall balance worse.
  2. Balance branch-by-branch, starting from the index run. The index run is the longest or highest-resistance path through the system; other branches typically have excess pressure available and are throttled back toward it, rather than trying to push the index run up to match everything else.
  3. Confirm total system airflow at the fan matches design, not just that individual outlets sum correctly — a system can have every outlet reading close to design while total fan airflow is off if there's unaccounted leakage in the ductwork.
  4. Re-check after balancing since throttling one branch changes the pressure available to every other branch on the same system — balancing is iterative, not a single pass.
Duct leakage is often the hidden reason a system won't balance cleanly — a leaky duct run upstream of a balancing damper means the damper is balancing against less airflow than the design assumed, no matter how carefully it's adjusted.

Reading an HVAC Schematic

Trace air first, then water/refrigerant, then controls — reading all three tangled together at once is where confusion starts.
  1. Trace the airside path first. Outside air intake → mixing box → filter → coils → fan → supply ductwork → diffusers → return path back to the AHU (or exhaust). This is usually the easiest path to follow since it's drawn as the "main line" of most HVAC schematics.
  2. Then trace the waterside or refrigerant side. Chilled/hot water piping to coils, or refrigerant piping in a DX system, is typically drawn as a separate overlaid or adjacent circuit — follow supply and return separately.
  3. Then read the controls layer last. Sensor locations, control valve positions, and damper actuators are what actually make the system respond to load — understanding airside and waterside first makes the control logic make sense, rather than trying to absorb all three simultaneously.
  4. Use the Symbols Library on this site's HVAC section for unfamiliar diffuser, damper, and equipment symbols as you go.
Most HVAC schematics use the Symbols Library's ASHRAE/SMACNA-style conventions — diffusers, dampers, coils and equipment. If a symbol looks unfamiliar, check there before assuming it's project-specific.
