Comparisons

Head-to-head instrumentation, signal and control choices — side by side, so the trade-off is visible at a glance
Eight common automation/controls decisions, laid out as direct comparisons rather than separate articles — same criteria, side by side, with a plain-language verdict on when each option actually wins. Jump straight to the one you need.
1. 4-20mA vs 0-10V vs HART Signals 2. PLC vs DCS vs SCADA 3. PID vs On-Off (Bang-Bang) Control 4. Globe vs Ball vs Butterfly Control Valves 5. RTD vs Thermocouple Temperature Sensors 6. DP vs Ultrasonic vs Radar Level Measurement 7. Relay Logic vs PLC Logic 8. Wired vs Wireless Instrumentation
1

4-20mA vs 0-10V vs HART Signals

The three most common ways an analog field signal actually reaches the controller — and they aren't interchangeable.
Criteria4-20mA Current Loop0-10V Voltage SignalHART
Noise immunity over long cable runsExcellent — current doesn't drop with cable resistancePoor over long runs — voltage drops with cable resistanceExcellent — same 4-20mA carrier
Wire fault detectionGood — 0mA reads as a clear break/fault, distinct from a real 0% signalPoor — 0V can mean either a real zero or a broken wireGood — same as 4-20mA, plus digital diagnostics
Extra data carriedNone — one variable onlyNone — one variable onlyDigital overlay — diagnostics, secondary variables, config
Typical distanceLong runs, hundreds of meters, commonShort runs — panel-internal or close-coupledSame as 4-20mA
Typical applicationThe default for field transmitter-to-PLC/DCS wiringShort local connections, some drive/VFD referencesSmart transmitters needing remote diagnostics/configuration
4-20mA is the practical default for field instrumentation specifically because the "live zero" (4mA, not 0mA, represents 0%) lets a broken wire be distinguished from a genuine zero reading — a safety-relevant distinction 0-10V can't make on its own. HART simply layers a digital signal on top of the same 4-20mA current, so existing loops keep working with legacy equipment while newer, HART-capable equipment gets extra diagnostic and configuration data for free. 0-10V mostly survives in short, local connections where cable length and fault detection are less of a concern.

PLC vs DCS vs SCADA

Three overlapping terms for automation architecture, distinguished more by scale and intent than by any hard technical line.
CriteriaPLCDCSSCADA
Primary roleFast, localized discrete/sequential controlCoordinated, continuous process control across a whole plantSupervisory monitoring and control over a wide geographic area
Typical scan/control speedVery fast — millisecondsFast, plant-optimized for continuous loopsSlower — polling remote sites, seconds typical
Geographic scopeSingle machine or process areaSingle plant/site, tightly integratedCan span a wide area — pipelines, water networks, multiple remote sites
Redundancy/engineering focusSimpler, cost-optimized for the taskHeavily redundant, engineered for continuous plant uptimeFocused on reliable remote communication (RTUs, radio, cellular)
Typical applicationMachine control, packaging lines, discrete manufacturingRefineries, chemical plants, power generationOil & gas pipelines, water/wastewater networks, utility grids
A PLC is fundamentally a fast, focused controller — the building block used inside both DCS and SCADA systems, not a separate category of hardware. A DCS is really a PLC-class control philosophy scaled up and engineered for redundancy and tight coordination across many loops in one plant. SCADA solves a different problem — supervising and occasionally controlling assets spread over a wide area where constant, tightly coupled communication isn't practical, leaning on local PLCs/RTUs to handle the real-time control at each remote site.
3

PID vs On-Off (Bang-Bang) Control

The simplest possible control strategy against the one that dominates modern process control — and each has its place.
CriteriaOn-Off ControlPID Control
Final element behaviorFully on or fully off onlyContinuously modulated position
Steady-state accuracyCycles around setpoint within a deadbandCan hold very close to setpoint continuously
Wear on the final elementHigher — frequent full-cycle switchingLower — smaller, continuous adjustments
Tuning complexitySimple — mainly deadband widthMore involved — three interacting gains to tune
Typical applicationSimple heaters, basic level/pressure switches, refrigerationFlow, pressure, temperature, and level loops needing tight, steady control
On-off control is the right default wherever cycling around a deadband is genuinely acceptable and simplicity/cost matter more than precision — a household-style thermostat is the classic example. PID control earns its added tuning complexity specifically where a process needs to be held close and steadily at a setpoint, or where cycling a valve or motor fully on/off would cause unacceptable wear, mechanical shock, or product quality issues. Many real loops actually use PID logic with a wide proportional band to approximate simple on-off behavior when that's genuinely all that's needed.
4

Globe vs Ball vs Butterfly Control Valves

Three valve bodies, each trading throttling precision against cost, size, and pressure drop differently.
CriteriaGlobe ValveBall ValveButterfly Valve
Throttling precisionBest — designed specifically for modulating controlGood with a characterized (V-notch) ballModerate — best near mid-travel
Pressure drop when fully openHigher — flow path changes direction internallyLow — straight-through boreLow to moderate — disc remains in the flow path
Size/weight for a given line sizeLarger, heavierCompactMost compact, lightest for large diameters
Relative cost at large sizesHighestModerateLowest — the usual choice for large-diameter lines
Typical applicationPrecise process throttling, high pressure drop serviceGeneral throttling and tight shutoff, moderate sizesLarge-diameter, low-pressure-drop throttling — water, air, HVAC
Globe valves remain the benchmark wherever precise, repeatable throttling is the priority and the higher inherent pressure drop and cost are acceptable — most classic process control loops default here. Ball valves with a characterized ball trade a little throttling precision for a more compact, lower-pressure-drop package, and offer excellent tight shutoff. Butterfly valves become the economical choice specifically at large diameters and lower pressure-drop service, where a globe valve's size and cost stop being justified.

RTD vs Thermocouple Temperature Sensors

Two dominant temperature sensing technologies, splitting mainly on accuracy versus temperature range.
CriteriaRTDThermocouple
AccuracyHigher, more repeatableLower, more prone to drift over time
Temperature rangeModerate — roughly -200°C to 850°CVery wide — some types beyond 1700°C
Response timeSlower — more thermal massFaster — smaller sensing junction
Output signalResistance change — needs excitation currentSmall self-generated millivolt signal
Relative costHigherLower, widely available in many types
Typical applicationProcess control needing high accuracy, moderate temperaturesHigh-temperature service, furnaces, fast-response needs
RTDs are the default wherever accuracy and long-term stability matter most and the process temperature stays within their moderate range — most general process temperature control loops. Thermocouples take over specifically at high temperatures beyond a practical RTD's range, or where a fast-responding, low-cost, rugged sensor is more important than the last degree of accuracy — furnace and combustion applications are the classic case.
6

DP vs Ultrasonic vs Radar Level Measurement

Three ways to infer level, each vulnerable to a different kind of process interference.
CriteriaDifferential PressureUltrasonicRadar
Measurement principleHydrostatic pressure from fluid headSound wave time-of-flight to the surfaceMicrowave time-of-flight to the surface
Sensitive to fluid density changesYes — directly affects the pressure/level relationshipNo — measures distance, not pressureNo — measures distance, not pressure
Affected by vapor, foam, or dustNoYes — can scatter or absorb the sound signalLess affected — generally the most robust of the three
Contact with process fluidWetted or remote-seal diaphragm in contactNon-contact, mounted above the surfaceNon-contact, mounted above the surface
Typical applicationSimple liquid tanks, consistent fluid densityClean liquids, moderate-accuracy solids/liquid levelTurbulent, vapor-laden, or high-accuracy applications
DP level measurement remains a simple, well-understood default for liquid tanks with a reasonably consistent, known fluid density — its accuracy actually depends on that density assumption staying valid. Ultrasonic level offers a straightforward non-contact option for cleaner applications, but loses reliability where foam, heavy vapor, or dust interferes with the sound path. Radar generally handles those difficult atmospheres better and has become the common choice where DP's density-sensitivity or ultrasonic's vulnerability to vapor/foam would be a real problem.
7

Relay Logic vs PLC Logic

The same ladder-logic thinking, but one is wired in hardware and the other is software running on a processor.
CriteriaRelay LogicPLC Logic
How logic is implementedPhysical relay contacts and coils, hardwired togetherSoftware program (typically ladder logic) executed by a processor
Making a logic changeRequires rewiring the physical panelRequires only reprogramming — no rewiring
Diagnostics/troubleshootingLimited — visual inspection and multimeter checksExtensive — online monitoring, forcing, fault logging
Panel size/complexity for large logicGrows large and complex quicklyStays compact regardless of logic complexity
Typical application todayVery simple, rarely-changed circuits; legacy installationsThe default for essentially all new discrete/sequential control
PLC logic has effectively replaced relay logic as the default for new installations of any meaningful complexity — the ability to change logic in software rather than rewiring a panel, combined with far better diagnostics, outweighs relay logic's simplicity in almost every modern case. Relay logic mostly persists today in very simple, rarely-changed circuits, safety circuits built to a specific hardwired standard, or legacy equipment that hasn't been modernized — understanding it is still valuable for troubleshooting older plants and for basic safety-relay circuits that intentionally stay hardwired.
8

Wired vs Wireless Instrumentation

Wireless removes the cable run, but that trade isn't free — it comes with its own constraints.
CriteriaWired InstrumentationWireless Instrumentation
Installation cost, long/difficult runsHigher — cable tray, conduit, trenchingLower — no cable run needed
Signal reliability/latencyVery high, deterministicGood but not deterministic — subject to interference, battery/network issues
Update rateContinuous, fastTypically slower, battery-life-driven reporting intervals
Power sourceLoop-powered or externally poweredBattery, often with multi-year life at low update rates
Typical applicationCritical control loops, tight-tolerance continuous controlMonitoring points, remote/hard-to-cable locations, non-critical trending
Wired instrumentation remains the default for any loop where control performance, update rate, or reliability genuinely matters — most closed-loop process control. Wireless instrumentation earns its place specifically for monitoring points that are expensive or impractical to cable — remote tanks, rotating equipment, retrofit projects — where a slower update rate and battery-powered operation are an acceptable trade for avoiding a costly cable run. It's rarely the right choice yet for tight, fast-acting control loops.
